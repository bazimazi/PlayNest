# Research round 5: what the research agrees on (release 1.8)

Releases 1.6 and 1.7 taught parenting skills and covered the first months, injuries, learning and mental health. Release 1.8
steps back to ask what decades of studies around the world actually agree on, checks popular beliefs against them, and fills
the gaps parents meet most often at home: tummy bugs, coughs and earache, eczema, hot days, frightening events, stuttering,
learning difficulties, fire festivals, animals, furniture that tips and water. It also adds the first goal for parents of
babies under 1.

Everything is in English and Persian. **None of it has been read by a clinician, psychologist or Persian-speaking reviewer yet.**
The checklist is in §11 of [REVIEW-1.5.md](REVIEW-1.5.md).

## What the research changed

**A few things hold everywhere.** Across nine countries, from China and Jordan to Kenya and Sweden, children of warm parents did
better, and more physical punishment went with more aggression and anxiety even where smacking was common (Lansford, Parenting
Across Cultures). Back-and-forth talk matters more than word counts (Romeo 2018; the "30 million word gap" itself is contested).
Childhood self-control was linked with adult health and finances in a gradient, beyond IQ and wealth (Dunedin). Regular bedtimes
went with calmer behaviour, and behaviour improved when families switched (UK Millennium Cohort). Home mattered more than childcare
(NICHD). These are associations, and the new guide says so, with England's null home-visiting trial as the example of why science
checks again in new places.

**Correction to round 3.** Round 3's notes quoted the Jamaica home-visit trial as "+42% earnings", from a 2013 working paper. The
published figure (Science 2014) is +25% at about 22. The number never reached the app, and the new guide describes the trial
without one.

**Some beliefs parents hold are simply wrong, and some are half right.** Sugar doesn't change behaviour in blinded trials; mothers
who thought their son had sugar rated him as wilder though he'd had none. Teething doesn't cause a real fever. Baby walkers don't
help walking: in an Iranian sample over half of babies used one, with no difference in walking age, and walkers cause stair falls.
Baby videos didn't teach words. Learning styles and left/right-brain types aren't supported. Cold weather is "half true": colds are
viral, but one small trial linked chilling to symptoms appearing. The guide tells each with respect, because most were passed down
with love.

**Common illnesses: fluids, time and fewer antibiotics.** Most tummy bugs pass in days, and the job is fluids: ORS mixed exactly,
small sips, breastfeeding more. Iran's sick-child programme adds zinc for 10 to 14 days; WHO lowered the zinc dose in 2024, one more
reason the app never prints an amount. The rotavirus vaccine has been in Iran's national schedule since 1403 (provincial and
national news; an official page still to be found). Colds last about two weeks and coughs three to four (UKHSA 2024). Antibiotics
for ear infections don't ease pain in the first day and give 1 in 14 children a side effect (Cochrane 2023); in one Iranian city,
62% of under-6s had antibiotics without a prescription in a year. Steam («بخور») doesn't help croup and can scald. Daily moisturiser
from birth didn't prevent eczema in the large trials and raised skin infections, though one newer trial found a small benefit, so
the guide says "not needed" rather than "harmful". Henna on babies' skin can trigger a serious reaction in G6PD deficiency.

**Frightening events: calm, honest, unhurried.** Children cope best with a calm, familiar adult; most recover, and most recovery
comes in the first months (PTSD rates fall about half between months 1 and 6, Hiller 2016), so "still getting in the way after
about a month" is the point to ask. Forcing a child to talk is out: single-session debriefing didn't help and one trial found more
PTSD a year later. Heavy news exposure went with more stress symptoms in adults and children. Parent and child stress are linked
(r ≈ 0.3), framed as "you affect each other", never as blame. Iran lived through armed conflict in 2025 and again in early 2026; the
guides name no side, date or place.

**Stuttering recovers, but slowly.** About 1 in 10 children had started stuttering by 4 in a large Australian study, and they were
not shyer and had slightly stronger language. Most recover, but over years: only 6% within a year of onset, 65% by 7. So the guide
gives no timeline, lists reasons to see a speech therapist, and warns against "slow down" and praising smooth speech outside therapy.
Bilingualism doesn't cause it.

**Learning differences: early help, no quick fixes.** Dyslexia is a continuum (IDA 2025), affecting roughly 1 in 20 to 1 in 10
depending on the test (5.7% in an Iranian meta-analysis). Structured sound–letter teaching has the strongest evidence. Coloured
overlays, eye exercises and brain training don't treat it. There's no evidence of a "dyslexic superpower".

**Iran's injuries shaped the safety guides.** In the 1403 Chaharshanbe Suri, Iran's Emergency Organisation reported 44% of the
injured were under 18, with many eye and hand injuries and many passers-by hurt; homemade explosives caused many of the worst. Iran
records hundreds of thousands of animal bites a year, three quarters from dogs; rabies is always fatal once symptoms start but
preventable with washing for 15 minutes and same-day care at a rabies centre. In Hormozgan, under-5s drowned in pools, buckets,
farm ponds, baths and yard water tanks. No single step prevents drowning, so the guide uses layers.

**A baby goal, gentle by design.** Crying education raises knowledge and coping, but population injury results are mixed (North
Carolina null; British Columbia borderline), so the crying plan never claims to prevent shaking. Parents lose the most sleep in the
first three months. The six practices ride on care parents already give, with a one-minute version that counts.

## What shipped in 1.8

- **16 new guides (124 in all):**
  - *Parenting skills (2):* what decades of research say matters most; parenting myths, checked.
  - *Health (4):* tummy bugs; coughs, colds, sore throats and earache; eczema, dry skin and nappy rash; hot days and sun.
  - *Feelings (2):* when scary things happen; helping children in the weeks after.
  - *Talking (1):* stuttering. *School (1):* when learning is hard.
  - *Safety (4):* festival nights and fireworks (the Persian is about چهارشنبه‌سوری); children, dogs and other animals; furniture
    and TVs that tip; water safety in layers.
- **A 15th goal, "Our first year together"**, for parents of babies under 1: a crying plan, little conversations, a song or book a
  day, reading the baby's signals, sharing the nights, and one small thing for the parent.
- **61 new daily tips** (458 in all).
- **The dog-bite first-aid card** now says to wash for 15 minutes under running water, even small scratches, following WHO and Iran's
  national rabies guideline, and cites WHO.

## Not added, and why

- **190, extension 9.** A peer-reviewed paper describes this as the Ministry of Health's free crisis-counselling line during the 2026
  conflict, kept on afterwards. One source isn't enough, and extensions change: test-call it first. It also suggests the old "190 =
  poison line?" doubt in `helplines.ts` was a mix-up.
- **Breastfeeding and IQ.** Genuinely debated (Pelotas vs PROBIT and sibling studies). It isn't needed for any decision a parent
  makes, and could be misread in Iran as discouraging breastfeeding.
- **Honey from 1 or from 2.** Iran's sick-child booklet says lemon and honey over 2; NHS, AAP and WHO say from 1. The app keeps "never
  under 1" and asks a clinician (§11).
- **Electric shock and exposed wiring, e-scooters.** Not researched this round.
- **Zinc, ORS and sunscreen amounts, steroid strengths.** Never, by house rule.

## Toward being recommended by health services

The round adds three things a reviewer from a health system would look for: every new guide cites the national programme where
one exists (Iran's «مانا» for sick children, the rabies guideline, the school-entry screening, the postnatal mental-health screen
in the maternal booklet); disagreements between bodies are named instead of hidden; and the "honest box" in each guide says when
something is only linked, small or untested. The next steps in [RESEARCH-5.md](RESEARCH-5.md) still stand: a named clinical
reviewer and next-review date on each guide, a content-governance document, and co-design with Iranian parents.
