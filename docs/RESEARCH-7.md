# Research round 6: checking what we have, and the everyday worries (release 1.9)

Round 6 did two things. First, it checked the app's existing high-stakes advice (first-aid cards, safe sleep, feeding, fever, car
seats, teeth) against the newest guidance from 2024 to 2026, including the 2025 resuscitation guidelines, and fixed what had fallen
behind. Second, it filled gaps parents meet every week: screens with young children, "is my child eating enough?", iron and tea,
constipation, worms and lice, legs and feet, brothers and sisters, children's curiosity about bodies, risky play, wheels and
electricity. It also looked at what health systems ask of an app like this, and wrote PlayNest's content-governance policy.

Everything is in English and Persian. **No clinician, psychologist or Persian-speaking reviewer has read any of it yet.** The
checklist is in §12 of [REVIEW-1.5.md](REVIEW-1.5.md); the governance policy is [CONTENT-GOVERNANCE.md](CONTENT-GOVERNANCE.md).

## What the audit found and fixed

**One thing could have caused harm, and is fixed.** The fever guide's table put "a baby 3 to 6 months with a fever" under "see a
doctor soon". The NHS sends a 3-to-6-month-old with 39°C or more for urgent advice, NICE rates it amber, and the app's own first-aid
card and Crying page already said "get help now". The guide now agrees with them.

**Smaller fixes, each from a source that was opened:**
- Powdered formula is made with water boiled no more than 30 minutes earlier, so it is still hot enough to kill germs (NHS).
- No fever medicine for a baby under 3 months unless a doctor, nurse or pharmacist says so (NHS).
- The US button-battery card says honey "up to 6 times", not "6 doses".
- Booster seats until the adult belt fits, usually between about 8 and 12 (AAP 2026, was "10 and 12").
- **The electric-shock card** now says the "push them clear with a dry wooden object" step is for plugs, cords and appliances only,
  and adds: power lines, pylons, railway lines or fallen cables, don't go near, stay 10 metres or more away and call for help.
  High-voltage injuries mostly affect older children and teenagers, and are the most severe.
- Two goal steps said a warning before screens go off "helps avoid meltdowns". The one study of 1-to-5-year-olds linked warnings with
  more upset, so the goals now offer warnings as optional.
- The original risky-play guide said physical play helped "as long as the adult isn't overly dominant". That couldn't be verified and
  may be backwards, so the rewritten guide says only that the adult stays in charge of the game.
- The `screens` guide's Canadian row and England date were out of date.

**Everything else held up.** The cards updated for the 2025 resuscitation guidelines (CPR, choking, recovery position) agree with
current US, Canadian, UK and European guidance, as do anaphylaxis, seizures, burns, the button-battery honey step, safe sleep,
swaddling, jaundice, car seats and teeth.

**A new test** now fails if any guide, tip, goal, hard-moment card, first-aid card or Persian translation contains a medicine or
supplement amount. Until now that house rule depended on people remembering it.

## What the research changed

**Screens: small links, honest advice.** More screen time at 1 to 3 went with slightly lower language and development scores later,
clearest in children watching many hours a day, and each extra minute of screen time went with about one fewer back-and-forth at
age 3. Reverse causation is real: children who find calming down harder get screens more, and exhausted or low parents use them
more. England's 2026 expert panel found no proven safe or unsafe number of minutes. Fast-paced shows had no short-term effect on
attention in a 2025 review, and short-form video has never been studied in young children, so the guide is cautious without scaring.
Video calls with family are the use every body allows; for Iranian families with relatives far away, the Persian leads with them.

**Growth: the chart, not the plate.** WHO's growth study found children in six countries (including Oman) grew remarkably alike with
good care, so a child following a low line is usually healthy. Pressure to eat runs in a circle with fussiness (Generation R). The
Persian says plainly that appetite syrups are medicines with side effects (their use for thin children is documented in Iran), and
that no syrup makes a healthy child taller.

**Iron and tea.** About 1 in 5 young Iranian children have iron-deficiency anaemia in pooled studies. Black tea with a meal cut iron
absorption by 79 to 94% in a lab study, and herbal teas also cut it; whether tea causes anaemia in children is less clear. The
guide follows Iran's Ministry of Health: no tea for babies, and weak, unsweetened tea between meals for older children. Paleness isn't
proof of low iron, because thalassaemia trait is common in Iran, and a trial found extra iron may slow growth in babies who already
had enough, so no extra iron without a test.

**Everyday complaints.** Constipation medicine is often needed for months and is safe for that long; extra fibre and water don't fix
it on their own. Nothing goes into a baby's bottom, and no oil by mouth (it can reach the lungs). Lice have nothing to do with dirt;
an Iranian 3-year-old needed emergency care after insecticide was sprayed on her hair. In a trial, corrective shoes and insoles made
no difference to flat feet over 3 years.

**Siblings and bodies.** About 4 in 10 children were hurt or picked on by a sibling in a year in a US survey, and repeated sibling
bullying was linked with later depression and self-harm, so the guide separates squabbling from bullying. Birth order doesn't shape
personality. Iranian parents in a qualitative study asked for exactly the "is this normal?" guidance the body-curiosity guide gives;
it uses modest wording («حریم بدن»), gives no ages or rulings on family customs, and sends anything worrying to a professional.

**Risk, wheels and electricity.** Risky outdoor play is linked with more activity and better risk skills, in modest-quality studies.
Helmets roughly halve the odds of a head injury in a crash. Iran's traffic rules require helmets for motorbike passengers, and Iran's
health network advises no children under 5 on motorbikes at all; the law sets no minimum age, so the app never says carrying a
child in front is illegal, only unsafe. Electrical burns are 1 to 3 in 100 children's burns in Iranian units, mostly toddlers.

## Open checks from round 5, now answered

- **Rotavirus** is in Iran's national programme at 2, 4 and 6 months, launched nationwide on 13 Azar 1403 (a university health centre
  and the head of the Ministry's vaccine office). Pneumococcal vaccine was added in 1403 at 2, 4 and 12 months. behdasht.gov.ir still
  couldn't be opened.
- **190, extension 9** is the Ministry of Health's free, anonymous counselling line, 8:00 to 24:00, according to the head of the
  Ministry's mental health office. It is described as counselling, not a crisis line, and it isn't the poison line (1490). It is
  still not in the app until someone test-calls it.
- **Honey:** the Ministry's well-child booklet says not under 1 (seen in search results), the same as the app. Only «مانا» and one
  nutrition school say 2.
- **Supplements:** A+D drops from the first days to age 2; iron drops from the end of 6 months (or when solids start) to age 2. The
  Persian iron guide now says so, with no amounts.
- **The festival figure** (44% of the injured under 18 in 1403) was said by the head of the Emergency Organisation and reported by
  IRNA; the organisation's own release is no longer online. The citation now says that.
- **Learning-difficulty centres:** still unknown whether parents can refer themselves. The app already says "ask the school".

## Not added, and why

- **190 ext. 9 and 1490**, until test-called.
- **Two-finger infant CPR on the Australia and New Zealand card.** It is still that region's official guideline, but the 2025 US and
  European guidelines dropped it. Iranian parents have no default region and may pick it. A decision for the owner and a clinician.
- **Bee stings and tweezers.** The card says no tweezers; US first-aid bodies (2024) say pluck or scrape, whatever is fastest. Bodies
  differ; left for the clinician.
- **Mouth burns from cords can bleed up to about 3 weeks later.** Seen in clinical summaries only; the card keeps "hours or days"
  until a primary source is read.
- **Honey for button batteries on the UK and European cards.** Supported by one NHS hospital leaflet; no national UK or European
  source was opened.
- **A full Iranian vaccine table**, until the 1403 table itself is read.
- **A "home alone" guide.** `chores-independence` already covers readiness; the research (readiness signs, no legal age in Iran, the
  1399 child protection law on neglect) is in note 05 for a later round.
- **Doses, brand names, Iranian prevalence figures with blame attached, and any political explanation of health trends.** Never.

## Toward being recommended by health services

Note 06 found that an information app like PlayNest sits in NICE's tier B, where reviewers look for clinical assurance above all: a
named expert reviewer, a review date and a clear source policy. ORCHA-style reviews fail most apps on exactly that. In Iran, no
licensing scheme for information apps exists; the realistic route is a named reviewer or endorsement from a university of medical
sciences or a Ministry office. The next steps are therefore: appoint reviewers and work through REVIEW-1.5.md §8–12; add `reviewedBy`
and `nextReview` to guides and show them; build "report a problem"; write the privacy statement; test with Iranian parents; and
prepare a one-page map of PlayNest content to «کودک سالم» and «مانا» for a first conversation with a university health deputy.
