# Development stages, routines and everyday-life skills, birth to 12 years (PlayNest phase 5)

Checked 4 Nov 2026. Builds on `docs/RESEARCH-2.md` §2, which is cited rather than repeated. Text below is paraphrased; short quotes are marked. Most web pages were read through a summarising fetch tool, so re-check any quote against the page before it ships. These PDFs were extracted and read directly: AAP periodicity schedule, WHO motor windows, Bright Futures pocket guide, Harvard EF guides, Ofsted Bold Beginnings, McLeod & Crowe, CFPB, AFIRM.

Research was cut short in two ways: the session's web-search budget ran out part-way (later lookups were direct fetches of known URLs only), and several sites block automated reading (listed under the last Gaps). Items not confirmed against a source sit in Gaps, never in Cited Findings.

## Per-age-band fact packs (17 bands)

### Takeaway
No single body covers the whole span. CDC checklists run 2 months to 5 years and CDC's positive-parenting index now stops at age 5, so 0–6 weeks and 6–12 years have to be built from AAP, ZERO TO THREE, Bright Futures, ASHA, KidsHealth and Harvard. After 5 years the sources describe what is common and which topics to raise; none retrieved publishes age-keyed warning lists.

### Cited Findings

Conventions: "By X" means most children by about that age, never a deadline. "Ask" lines keep each body's own framing. Sleep figures are per 24 hours, including naps until the school years. Loss of skills at any age is a "talk to the clinician now" prompt ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html)). Emergency signs live in RESEARCH-2 §2.6 and are not repeated here.

#### 0–6 weeks
- **Social-emotional:** calming when held or spoken to is only emerging; early smiles are often reflexive, with a social smile in the following weeks ([ZERO TO THREE, Sep 2026](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/one-month-old-milestones-what-parents-can-expect/)). A first real smile often appears around week six ([AAP, 2020](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-Birth-to-Three-Months.aspx)).
- **Language:** crying is the main signal, with small throaty sounds ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/one-month-old-milestones-what-parents-can-expect/)); recognises parents' voices by about 1 month ([AAP, 2021](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Hearing-and-Making-Sounds.aspx)).
- **Thinking and senses:** sees best at about 20–30 cm, roughly the distance to a parent's face during a feed ([AAP, 2024](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Babys-Vision-Development.aspx)); prefers faces and high-contrast patterns ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/one-month-old-milestones-what-parents-can-expect/)).
- **Movement:** mostly reflexes (rooting, sucking, grasping, startle); jerky, with head support needed ([AAP, 2020](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-Birth-to-Three-Months.aspx)).
- **Sleep:** about 18 h a day in short bursts, varying between babies ([NHS, 2025](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/helping-your-baby-to-sleep/)); 14–17 h for 0–3 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)). The two figures differ; show a range.
- **Worried about, usually normal:** eyes that cross or wander, which should be going away by 2–3 months ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Babys-Vision-Development.aspx)); crying that builds from about 2 weeks and is worst in afternoons and evenings ([NHS, 2026](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/soothing-a-crying-baby/)); looking away, yawning or arching, which signal overstimulation ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/one-month-old-milestones-what-parents-can-expect/)).
- **Ask:** AAP: stops doing something she used to do, not using a part or side of the body, "too floppy", jitters or shakes too much ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-Birth-to-Three-Months.aspx)). ZERO TO THREE: little response to sounds, concerns about vision or eye contact, unusually hard to wake or feed; "any concern" counts, without waiting for a visit ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/one-month-old-milestones-what-parents-can-expect/)). NHS: constant crying you cannot comfort, or a cry that does not sound usual; speak to a GP or health visitor and trust your instincts ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/soothing-a-crying-baby/)).
- **Play and talk:** short, cue-led face time; narrate feeds and nappy changes; copy sounds; sing ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/help-your-baby-learn-to-talk/)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Hold baby about 20–30 cm from your face, speak softly, then pause to see the response ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/one-month-old-milestones-what-parents-can-expect/)).
  2. Do tummy time lying baby on your chest, from birth, while you are wide awake ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/keep-baby-or-toddler-active/)).
  3. Say what you are doing during one nappy change ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/help-your-baby-learn-to-talk/)).
  4. Move your face or a high-contrast picture slowly side to side ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/one-month-old-milestones-what-parents-can-expect/)).
  5. Make a crying plan: put baby somewhere safe, step out for a set time such as 10 minutes, then return ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/soothing-a-crying-baby/)).

#### 6 weeks–3 months
- **Social-emotional:** by 2 months calms when picked up, looks at your face, seems happy to see you, smiles back ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-months.html)). Starts preferring familiar carers ([AAP, 2026](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-Birth-to-3-Months.aspx)).
- **Language:** sounds other than crying; reacts to loud sounds ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-months.html)); coos from about 2 months ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Hearing-and-Making-Sounds.aspx)); takes turns making sounds ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-birth-to-1-year/)).
- **Thinking:** watches you move; looks at a toy for several seconds ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-months.html)).
- **Movement:** lifts head on tummy, moves both arms and legs, opens hands briefly ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-months.html)); by month 3 movements smooth out, hands open and close, may swipe at objects ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-Birth-to-Three-Months.aspx)).
- **Sleep:** 14–17 h for 0–3 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)); AASM makes no recommendation under 4 months (RESEARCH-2 §2.4).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** baby looking past you at first ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-Birth-to-3-Months.aspx)); clenched hands ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-Birth-to-Three-Months.aspx)); evening crying and colic, which usually affects babies under 5 months and has no known cause ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/soothing-a-crying-baby/)).
- **Ask:** CDC's list for any visit: what you do together, what baby enjoys, anything baby does or does not do that concerns you, lost skills, prematurity or special needs ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-months.html)). AAP's four movement flags above still apply. Eye crossing past 2–3 months is worth raising (inference from [AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Babys-Vision-Development.aspx)). AAP also screens the mother for depression at the 1, 2, 4 and 6-month visits ([AAP](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/periodicity_schedule.pdf)).
- **Play and talk:** back-and-forth sounds, tummy time with toys at eye level, talk during care, cuddles; holding does not spoil a baby ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-months.html)).
- **One thing to try** (all [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-months.html)):
  1. Answer each coo, then wait for baby's turn.
  2. Shake a rattle on one side and see if baby looks for the sound.
  3. Look at bright pictures or faces together and talk about them.
  4. Tummy time with a toy at eye level, always supervised.

#### 3–6 months
- **Social-emotional:** smiles on purpose and chuckles by 4 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-months.html)); recognises familiar people, enjoys a mirror, laughs by 6 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html)). Temperament starts to show; it is largely inborn, and AAP advises working with it rather than changing it ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-4-7-Months.aspx)).
- **Language:** coos, answers when spoken to, turns to voices ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-months.html)); raspberries, squeals, turn-taking ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html)); babbling usually begins around 4 months ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Language-Development-4-to-7-Months.aspx)).
- **Thinking:** around 4 months sees that actions cause results, such as shaking a rattle; after 4 months begins to grasp that hidden things still exist ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Cognitive-Development-4-to-7-Months.aspx)).
- **Movement:** steady head, swings at toys, pushes up on forearms ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-months.html)); by 6 months rolls tummy-to-back, pushes up on straight arms, leans on hands to sit ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html)). WHO's sitting window only opens at 3.8 months ([WHO](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/child-growth/child-growth-standards/indicators/motor-development-milestones/mm_windows_table.pdf)).
- **Sleep:** 12–16 h from 4 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)). Some babies sleep 5–8 h or more at night, but not all ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/helping-your-baby-to-sleep/)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** rolling timing varies, and most roll tummy-to-back first ([AAP, 2021](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-4-to-7-Months.aspx)); mouthing everything is how babies explore ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html)); dropping things on purpose is learning ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Cognitive-Development-4-to-7-Months.aspx)); not sleeping through the night ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/helping-your-baby-to-sleep/)).
- **Ask:** AAP: no babbling or imitating sounds by about 7 months, no variety of sounds, frequent ear infections, or startling at noise but struggling to imitate speech ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Language-Development-4-to-7-Months.aspx)). AAP on feelings: raise concerns and write down day-to-day observations, since they are hard to spot in a routine visit ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-4-7-Months.aspx)).
- **Play and talk:** floor time on a blanket with easy-grip toys, peek-a-boo, copying sounds, using baby's name ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-months.html)). Everyday lightweight items such as lids, spoons and boxes hold attention ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Cognitive-Development-4-to-7-Months.aspx)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Put a toy just out of reach during tummy time ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html)).
  2. Try a whisper, a clap and a silly noise; see which baby prefers ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html)).
  3. Pick up a dropped toy and hand it back, again and again ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html)).
  4. Half-hide a toy under a cloth and let baby find it ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Cognitive-Development-4-to-7-Months.aspx)).
  5. Repeat a syllable baby says, then offer a word with that sound, such as "bah", "bottle" ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Language-Development-4-to-7-Months.aspx)).

#### 6–9 months
- **Social-emotional:** by 9 months may be shy, clingy or fearful with strangers; several facial expressions; looks at own name; reacts when you leave; enjoys peek-a-boo ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html)).
- **Language:** many different sounds, repeated syllables; lifts arms to be picked up ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html)); pauses at "no", pushes away what is unwanted ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-birth-to-1-year/)).
- **Thinking:** looks for dropped or hidden things; bangs two things together ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html)); spends about 2–3 minutes on one toy at 8 months ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Cognitive-Development-8-to-12-Months.aspx)).
- **Movement:** gets to sitting alone, sits unsupported, passes objects between hands, rakes food ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html)). WHO windows: sitting 3.8–9.2 months, hands-and-knees crawling 5.2–13.5 months ([WHO](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/child-growth/child-growth-standards/indicators/motor-development-milestones/mm_windows_table.pdf)).
- **Sleep:** 12–16 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html)). Some no longer need night feeds, and sleep around 15 h, mostly at night ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/helping-your-baby-to-sleep/)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** fear of strangers, including relatives and sitters baby used to accept, is an expected emotional step ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-8-12-Months.aspx)); separation anxiety is common from 6 months to 3 years ([NHS, 2026](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/babys-development/behaviour/separation-anxiety/)); skipping crawling or scooting on the bottom is fine if both sides of the body are used equally ([AAP, 2021](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-8-to-12-Months.aspx)); fear of the vacuum cleaner or the dark ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-8-12-Months.aspx)).
- **Ask:** AAP: not sitting alone by 9 months; "not moving normally" ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-8-to-12-Months.aspx)). NHS on separation: a lot of distress, upset for a long time after you leave, or lasting more than a few weeks; talk to your health visitor ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/babys-development/behaviour/separation-anxiety/)). AAP's first developmental screening falls at 9 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html)).
- **Play and talk:** lap games and peek-a-boo practise memory and waiting ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-6-to-18-month-olds.pdf)); name and point at objects, talk about pictures ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/help-your-baby-learn-to-talk/)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Hide a toy under a cloth; once that is easy, hide it, move it while baby watches, and let baby search ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-6-to-18-month-olds.pdf)).
  2. Say a quick, cheerful goodbye when leaving, every time ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html)).
  3. Pass a toy back and forth, taking turns ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html)).
  4. Repeat baby's sounds and add a simple word ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html)).
  5. Play a short separation: go, come back, and say what you will do together next ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/babys-development/behaviour/separation-anxiety/)).

#### 9–12 months
- **Social-emotional:** games like pat-a-cake ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)); wants things their own way; often says "no" ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-8-12-Months.aspx)).
- **Language:** waves, calls a parent by a special name, understands "no" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)); points, shows, gives, may say one or two words ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-birth-to-1-year/)). Any sound used consistently for one thing counts as a word ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Language-Development-8-to-12-Months.aspx)).
- **Thinking:** puts things in containers, looks for what you hid ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)); attention grows to as much as 15 minutes at 12 months, and early pretend appears, such as a toy phone to the ear ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Cognitive-Development-8-to-12-Months.aspx)).
- **Movement:** pulls to stand, cruises furniture, pincer grasp ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)). WHO windows: walking with help 6.0–13.7 months, standing alone 6.9–16.9, walking alone 8.2–17.6 ([WHO](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/child-growth/child-growth-standards/indicators/motor-development-milestones/mm_windows_table.pdf)).
- **Sleep:** 12–16 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** not walking at the first birthday; many take first steps around then, and earlier or later is normal ([AAP, 2021](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-8-to-12-Months.aspx)). A 12-month-old whose speech is mostly babble with speech-like rhythm is the more usual picture ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Language-Development-8-to-12-Months.aspx)). Clinginess, bedtime resistance and night waking with separation anxiety, which usually peaks at 10–18 months ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-8-12-Months.aspx)).
- **Ask:** CDC's general list ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)). No gestures or no response to name by about 12 months is already in RESEARCH-2 §2.10. First dental visit is due around now (see screening section below). Instrument-based vision screening may begin at 12 months in the US ([AAP](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/periodicity_schedule.pdf)).
- **Play and talk:** narrate what you do; respond to pointing with the word; textured or flap books; pots and pans; stacking ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)). Offer household objects that differ slightly from what baby knows; babies engage most with those ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Cognitive-Development-8-to-12-Months.aspx)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Point, name, pause: name what baby points at, then wait ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)).
  2. Offer a brush, cup or spoon and be an enthusiastic audience for pretend ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Cognitive-Development-8-to-12-Months.aspx)).
  3. Send a comfort item along to a new carer ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)).
  4. Let baby push a sturdy toy or hold your hands to practise walking; avoid walkers ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)).
  5. Save "no" for dangerous things and redirect the rest ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html)).

#### 12–18 months
- **Social-emotional:** copies other children, shows a favourite object, hugs, claps ([CDC, 15 m](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html)); moves away but checks you are near, points to show you things ([CDC, 18 m](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)). Swings between independence and clinging, which AAP calls absolutely normal ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Emotional-Development-1-Year-Olds.aspx)).
- **Language:** one or two words besides mama/dada by 15 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html)), tries three or more by 18 months and follows one-step directions without gestures ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)); points to ask, shakes head and nods, knows at least one body part ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-13-to-18-months/)).
- **Thinking:** uses things correctly (phone, cup, book), stacks two objects ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html)); learns by imitation; does not yet foresee consequences ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Cognitive-Development-One-Year-Old.aspx)).
- **Movement:** a few steps alone by 15 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html)); walks without holding on, scribbles, cup without lid, tries a spoon, climbs on and off a chair by 18 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)).
- **Sleep:** 11–14 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** tantrums usually start around 18 months; hitting and biting are common and do not predict an aggressive child ([NHS, 2022](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/babys-development/behaviour/temper-tantrums/)). AAP calls sharing a meaningless term at this age and describes side-by-side play and competing for toys ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Social-Development-1-Year-Olds.aspx)). Sudden neediness after days of seeming mature ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Emotional-Development-1-Year-Olds.aspx)). Most are not ready for toilet training until 2–3 ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)).
- **Ask:** AAP's older list: cannot walk by 18 months; walks only on tiptoes; does not seem to understand common household objects by 15 months; fewer than 15 words by 18 months ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Developmental-Milestones-2-Year-Olds.aspx)). That word count conflicts with CDC's three (see language section). AAP's developmental and autism screens fall at 18 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html)).
- **Play and talk:** roll balls, fill and empty containers, blow bubbles, pretend-feed a toy ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)); action songs, offering choices, modelling the right word without correcting ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/help-your-baby-learn-to-talk/)); noisy pots-and-pans play ([NHS Start4Life](https://www.nhs.uk/start4life/baby/learning-to-talk/1-to-2-years)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Let your child fetch shoes or put things in a basket ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html)).
  2. Offer two choices and wait for a point or a word ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)).
  3. Bath a doll and name each body part you wash ([NHS Start4Life](https://www.nhs.uk/start4life/baby/learning-to-talk/1-to-2-years)).
  4. Set up a safe calm spot and stay nearby when your child is upset ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)).
  5. Hide a ball or cracker for your child to find ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Cognitive-Development-One-Year-Old.aspx)).

#### 18–24 months
- **Social-emotional:** notices when others are hurt or upset; looks at your face to decide how to react ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)); openly shows affection and a wide range of feelings, resists big routine changes ([AAP, 2022](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/emotional-development-2-year-olds.aspx)).
- **Language:** at least two words together, points to two body parts, gestures beyond waving ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)); ASHA expects at least 50 words, short phrases and two-step directions in this band ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-19-to-24-months/)).
- **Thinking:** holds something in one hand while using the other, tries switches and buttons, plays with more than one toy at a time ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)).
- **Movement:** kicks, runs, walks up a few stairs, eats with a spoon ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)).
- **Sleep:** 11–14 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)). Toddlers often go to one nap around 12–18 months (RESEARCH-2 §2.4).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** mood swings, testing limits, screaming or hitting when stopped ([AAP, 2022](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/emotional-development-2-year-olds.aspx)); refusing to share and poor peer play, which AAP says is usually a phase rather than a spoiled child ([AAP, 2022](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/social-development-2-year-olds.aspx)); behaving better with sitters, because a child feels safer pushing limits with parents ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/emotional-development-2-year-olds.aspx)); unclear pronunciation ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-19-to-24-months/)); quieter children may know as many words, and boys often talk later but even out by school age ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Language-Development-2-Year-Olds.aspx)).
- **Ask:** AAP: very passive or withdrawn, perpetually sad, or highly demanding and unsatisfied most of the time ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/emotional-development-2-year-olds.aspx)). AAP older list: no two-word sentences by 2, does not imitate or follow simple instructions by 2, cannot push a wheeled toy by 2 ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Developmental-Milestones-2-Year-Olds.aspx)). ASHA: fewer than 50 words with no word combinations by 24 months is a common late-talker criterion, with a hearing screen ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/late-language-emergence/)). AAP autism screen falls at 24 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html)).
- **Play and talk:** balls, button and lever toys, simple puzzles, dress-up, sand and water, "ready, set, go" on the swing ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)); follow the leader, freeze dance, sorting ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-18-to-36-month-olds.pdf)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Narrate your child's play, then ask "What will you do next?" ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-18-to-36-month-olds.pdf)).
  2. Play silly sorting: small shapes in the big bucket, big shapes in the small one ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-18-to-36-month-olds.pdf)).
  3. Say what you think they feel: "It looks like you are really angry right now" ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-18-to-36-month-olds.pdf)).
  4. Ask them to carry the napkins, then say thank you ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)).
  5. Repeat key words and keep instructions short ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/help-your-baby-learn-to-talk/)).

#### 2–2.5 years
- **Social-emotional:** plays beside others and sometimes joins; shows off skills; follows simple routines when reminded, such as tidying up ([CDC, 30 m](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html)).
- **Language:** about 50 words, two or more words with an action word, names things in a book, uses "I", "me", "we" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html)); asks why and how; speech is getting clearer but can still be hard for strangers ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-2-to-3-years/)).
- **Thinking:** pretend play, such as a block as food; steps on a stool to reach; two-step directions; knows one colour ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html)). May believe events happen because of something they did, and may feel responsible for illness or divorce; jokes can frighten them ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Cognitive-Development-Two-Year-Old.aspx)).
- **Movement:** twists lids and knobs, removes some clothing, jumps with both feet, turns pages one at a time ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html)).
- **Sleep:** 11–14 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** playing beside rather than with others ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html)); crying and clinging at goodbyes, which should be easier by 3 ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/emotional-development-2-year-olds.aspx)); repeating words and "uh", which is common from about 1.5 to 5 ([Stuttering Foundation](https://www.stutteringhelp.org/if-you-think-your-child-stuttering)); saying "no" often and strong reactions to changed routine ([ZERO TO THREE, 2025](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/understanding-toddler-behavior-whats-typical-and-when-to-seek-help/)). Toilet readiness: RESEARCH-2 §2.11.
- **Ask:** ZERO TO THREE's prompts, which it says are not a diagnostic checklist: tantrums that are intense, frequent or hard to recover from; little interest in play or people; aggression that continues despite guidance; loss of skills in speech, sleep or toileting; persistent sadness, fear or irritability ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/understanding-toddler-behavior-whats-typical-and-when-to-seek-help/)). AAP's third developmental screening falls at 30 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html)). England's 2–2½ review uses a parent-completed ASQ-3 ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/babys-development/height-weight-and-reviews/baby-reviews/)). At-risk children should have a hearing test by 2–2½ ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/hearing-loss-children/screening/index.html)).
- **Play and talk:** free play with new uses for objects; puzzles; big-box pretend; who/what/where questions while reading ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html)); slightly longer replies than the child's, time to answer, no background TV ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/help-your-baby-learn-to-talk/)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Offer two or three simple choices ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html)).
  2. Build junk models from boxes and yoghurt pots ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/baby-and-toddler-play-ideas/)).
  3. Act out the day with a teddy: wake up, bath, bed ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Cognitive-Development-Two-Year-Old.aspx)).
  4. At a meal, offer "carrot or cheese?" and say the word as you hand it over ([NHS Start4Life](https://www.nhs.uk/start4life/baby/learning-to-talk/2-to-3-years)).
  5. Add a few words to what your child notices: "a little bird is flying" ([NHS Start4Life](https://www.nhs.uk/start4life/baby/learning-to-talk/2-to-3-years)).

#### 2.5–3 years
- **Social-emotional:** by 3 calms within 10 minutes after you leave, joins other children ([CDC, 3 y](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)).
- **Language:** back-and-forth talk of at least two exchanges, who/what/where/why questions, says first name, understood most of the time ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)); correct p, b, m, h, w, d, n in words ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-2-to-3-years/)).
- **Thinking:** draws a circle when shown, avoids hot things after a warning ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)); begins to understand own daily routine, but not longer time such as a year ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Cognitive-Development-In-Preschool-Children.aspx)).
- **Movement:** threads large beads, puts on loose clothes, uses a fork ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)); pedals a tricycle ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
- **Sleep:** 11–14 h until 3, then 10–13 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)); naps usually end around 3–5 y (RESEARCH-2 §2.4).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** moving freely between make-believe and reality; imaginary friends, which do not signal loneliness; pretend violence ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Emotional-Development-in-Preschoolers.aspx)). Touching own genitals, common at about 3–6 ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Sexual-Behaviors-Young-Children.aspx)). Fears of the dark, animals or storms, which usually fade ([NHS, 2026](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/children-and-young-adults/advice-for-parents/anxiety-in-children/)). Night terrors, most common in toddlers and preschoolers ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Nightmares-and-Night-Terrors.aspx)).
- **Ask:** ZERO TO THREE flags ongoing distress at transitions or separation especially beyond 3 ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/understanding-toddler-behavior-whats-typical-and-when-to-seek-help/)). Germany's U7a at 34–36 months focuses on speech and vision ([BMG](https://www.bundesgesundheitsministerium.de/themen/praevention/kindergesundheit/frueherkennungsuntersuchung-bei-kindern)). Vision: at least once at 3–5 ([USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/vision-in-children-ages-6-months-to-5-years-screening)).
- **Play and talk:** speak in longer sentences, give 2–3-step instructions, read and ask about pictures, counting and opposites games, playdough, help with cooking ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Teach one calming step: deep breaths or a quiet space ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)).
  2. Role-play a new place or person before the visit ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)).
  3. Set one or two simple rules, redirect, and praise when followed ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)).
  4. Let your child help with one cooking step ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)).
  5. Use real names for body parts in the bath, and teach which parts are private ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Talking-to-Your-Young-Child-About-Sex.aspx)).

#### 3–4 years
- **Social-emotional:** by 4 takes roles in pretend play, seeks playmates, comforts someone hurt, adjusts behaviour to the place ([CDC, 4 y](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html)); around 3 starts playing with rather than beside others and can take turns in small groups, though not always ([AAP, 2024](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Social-Development-in-Preschoolers.aspx)).
- **Language:** sentences of four or more words, describes the day, answers "what is it for" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html)); retells stories, pretends to read; by 4 talks smoothly most of the time and is understood, though l, r, sh, ch, s, th may still be off ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-3-to-4-years/)).
- **Thinking:** names several colours, predicts what comes next in a known story, draws a person with three body parts ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html)); tends to see problems from one viewpoint ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Developmental-Milestones-3-to-4-Year-Olds.aspx)).
- **Movement:** catches a large ball most of the time, pours with supervision, unbuttons, holds a crayon with fingers ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html)).
- **Sleep:** 10–13 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** sound errors in later sounds ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-3-to-4-years/)); monsters and fantasy mix-ups ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Developmental-Milestones-3-to-4-Year-Olds.aspx)); changing or daily imaginary friends ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Emotional-Development-in-Preschoolers.aspx)); phases of fears that come and go ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/children-and-young-adults/advice-for-parents/anxiety-in-children/)).
- **Ask:** AAP's list, with its caveat that a slightly different timeline is not necessarily a concern: cannot throw overhand or jump in place; struggles to scribble or copy a circle; still clings or cries whenever parents leave; no interest in interactive games or fantasy; ignores other children; resists dressing, sleep or toilet; uncontrolled outbursts; sentences no longer than three words; mixes up "me" and "you" ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Developmental-Milestones-3-to-4-Year-Olds.aspx)). Stuttering that began after 3½, or has lasted 6–12 months ([ASHA-backed CHSA](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/stuttering-and-cluttering)).
- **Play and talk:** imaginary play with props, storytelling, sorting by changing rules, freeze dance ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-3-to-5-year-olds.pdf)); open questions, rhymes, "I spy" ([NHS Start4Life](https://www.nhs.uk/start4life/baby/learning-to-talk/3-to-5-years)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Draw a play plan before playing: who you will be and what you will do ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-3-to-5-year-olds.pdf)).
  2. Sort toys by colour, then switch the rule to shape ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-3-to-5-year-olds.pdf)).
  3. Tell a group story, each person adding a line ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-3-to-5-year-olds.pdf)).
  4. Model "Can I have a turn?" and praise when it is used ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html)).
  5. Give a small chore such as feeding a pet ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html)).

#### 4–5 years
- **Social-emotional:** follows rules and takes turns in games, sings or acts for you, does simple chores ([CDC, 5 y](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)); wants to please friends, elaborate pretend, may visit a next-door neighbour alone ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Developmental-Milestones-4-to-5-Year-Olds.aspx)).
- **Language:** tells a story with two events, keeps a conversation past three exchanges, recognises rhymes ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)); most consonants correct, understandable in conversation, names 10 or more letters, usually writes own name ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-4-to-5-years/)).
- **Thinking:** counts to 10, uses yesterday and tomorrow, pays attention for 5–10 minutes not counting screens, writes some letters of own name ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)).
- **Movement:** hops on one foot, buttons some buttons ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)).
- **Sleep:** 10–13 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** not reading yet; AAP says pushing academic skills early gives no real advantage and can make a child resist learning ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Cognitive-Development-In-Preschool-Children.aspx)). Curiosity about bodies ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Emotional-Development-in-Preschoolers.aspx)). Tantrums should be far less common by now ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/babys-development/behaviour/temper-tantrums/)).
- **Ask:** AAP's list, same caveat: extreme fear, timidity or aggression; major protest at every separation; little interest in other children; frequent sadness; cannot focus on one activity for more than five minutes; rarely pretends; cannot say first and last name; does not talk about daily experiences; trouble eating, sleeping, using the toilet, or washing and drying hands ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Developmental-Milestones-4-to-5-Year-Olds.aspx)). Germany's U9 at 60–64 months checks motor skills, hearing, vision and speech before school ([BMG](https://www.bundesgesundheitsministerium.de/themen/praevention/kindergesundheit/frueherkennungsuntersuchung-bei-kindern)). England offers vision screening around 4–5 ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/eye-tests-in-children/)).
- **Play and talk:** rule games like Simon Says, memory games like I Spy, art, playdates, role-playing kindergarten, asking "what" and "why" about stories ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Let your child do a task alone even if imperfect ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)).
  2. Practise saying their full name and yours, and how to find "helpers" if lost ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)).
  3. Have them tell a story from the pictures ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)).
  4. Lay out clothes the night before, and break the morning into steps ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Growing-Independence-Tips-for-Parents-of-Young-Children.aspx)).
  5. Ask questions with many answers: "What do you want to do tomorrow?" ([NHS Start4Life](https://www.nhs.uk/start4life/baby/learning-to-talk/3-to-5-years)).

#### 5–6 years
- **Social-emotional and school:** games with rules become enjoyable, with widely varying interest and skill ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-5-to-7-year-olds.pdf)). Bright Futures topics: family rules and routines, concern for others, patience and control over anger, friends, after-school care ([Bright Futures, 2017](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
- **Language and thinking:** by the end of kindergarten, follows one or two directions, retells a story, prints first name, identifies rhymes and first sounds ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/kindergarten/)). At 6 it is common to begin reading and simple sums and to start separating fantasy from reality ([KidsHealth, 2024](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-6yrs.html)).
- **Movement:** at 6 it is common to walk heel-to-toe and tie shoes ([KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-6yrs.html)). 60 minutes of activity a day ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
- **Sleep:** 10–13 h at 5 and 9–12 h from 6 ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)); 9–11 h from 5 in Canada ([CSEP](https://csepguidelines.ca/guidelines/children-youth/)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** anxiety about starting school ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/children-and-young-adults/advice-for-parents/anxiety-in-children/)); being among the youngest in class, where early academic problems usually fade by third or fourth grade ([AAP, 2025](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Is-Your-Child-Ready-for-School.aspx)).
- **Ask:** Bright Futures' question: what concerns do you have about your child's ability to do well in school? ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)). NHS: anxiety that is severe, lasts a long time, or gets in the way of everyday life; start with a GP and talk to the school ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/children-and-young-adults/advice-for-parents/anxiety-in-children/)).
- **Play and talk:** Concentration, Go Fish, Snap, Simon Says, freeze dance, clapping games; reduce the adult's role as soon as children know the rules ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-5-to-7-year-olds.pdf)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Play Concentration with a real pack of cards ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-5-to-7-year-olds.pdf)).
  2. Flip a coin or draw straws to settle a game dispute ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-5-to-7-year-olds.pdf)).
  3. Set up a "launch pad" near the door for bag and shoes ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/Pages/Ten-Tips-for-Your-Childs-Success-in-School.aspx)).
  4. Solve a conflict by talking, going outside to play, or walking away ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).

#### 6–7 years
- **Social-emotional:** greater independence from family; a circle of friends, usually same-gender; admires role models; understands right from wrong ([KidsHealth, 7 y](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-7yrs.html)). Selman's model places children here as starting to see that people hold different views because they have different information ([Wikipedia, secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-taking_theory)).
- **Language and thinking:** by the end of first grade, follows 2–3-step directions, is understood by anyone, tells stories that make sense, recognises 100 common words by sight ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/firstgrade/)). Longer attention; more organised, logical problem-solving ([KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-7yrs.html)).
- **Movement:** better coordination for tasks like shooting a basketball ([KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-7yrs.html)); old enough for organised sport ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-5-to-7-year-olds.pdf)).
- **Sleep:** 9–12 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** preferring same-gender friends ([KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-7yrs.html)); shyness in social situations and test nerves ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/children-and-young-adults/advice-for-parents/anxiety-in-children/)).
- **Ask:** bullying; talk with parents or a trusted adult ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)). After 7 it becomes unlikely that stuttering will go away completely, though treatment still helps ([AAP, 2016](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Stuttering-in-Toddlers-Preschoolers.aspx)). Vision signs: holding books very close, losing place, unusual clumsiness, frequent eye rubbing ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/eye-tests-in-children/)).
- **Play and talk:** Uno and Crazy Eights for flexibility, Battleship and checkers for planning, 20 Questions, rounds and clapping rhythms ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-5-to-7-year-olds.pdf)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Play Guess My Rule with blocks of different colours and sizes ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-5-to-7-year-olds.pdf)).
  2. Play Packing a Suitcase, adding one item each turn ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-5-to-7-year-olds.pdf)).
  3. Praise effort and small progress, not only the finished goal ([AAP, 2024](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/Pages/Helping-Your-Child-Develop-A-Healthy-Sense-of-Self-Esteem.aspx)).
  4. Hand over one responsibility and do not check it constantly ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/Pages/Helping-Your-Child-Develop-A-Healthy-Sense-of-Self-Esteem.aspx)).

#### 7–8 years
- **Social-emotional:** friend group, independence, role models ([KidsHealth, 8 y](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-8yrs.html)). In plain language, Erikson's "industry vs inferiority" (about 7–10) means feeling capable by making and finishing things, and feeling less than others after repeated failure or ridicule ([Wikipedia, secondary, citing Erikson 1950](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erikson%27s_stages_of_psychosocial_development)). Empirical support for his non-adolescent stages is limited, and the same source notes a Western male bias.
- **Language and thinking:** by the end of second grade, follows 3–4 directions, gives directions, uses language to inform and persuade, reads independently ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/secondgrade/)). Solves basic maths, concentrates and cooperates for longer ([KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-8yrs.html)).
- **Movement:** more coordinated movement ([KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-8yrs.html)); jump rope and organised sports become popular ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-7-to-12-year-olds.pdf)).
- **Sleep:** 9–12 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** temper and conflict, which Bright Futures treats as a routine visit topic ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)); questions about bodies ([AAP, 2023](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Talking-to-Your-Young-Child-About-Sex.aspx)).
- **Ask:** if there are school concerns, ask the teacher about evaluation for extra help; help with bullying ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)). USPSTF supports anxiety screening from 8 (RESEARCH-2 §2.9).
- **Play and talk:** rummy, hearts, chess, an instrument, singing in rounds ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-7-to-12-year-olds.pdf)); use everyday tasks like cooking to practise maths ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/Pages/Ten-Tips-for-Your-Childs-Success-in-School.aspx)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Ask: "Who do you talk with about your worries and things that make you mad?" ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
  2. Teach one strategy game and steadily raise the challenge ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-7-to-12-year-olds.pdf)).
  3. Discuss a rule and its consequence together ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
  4. Share how you recovered from your own mistake ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/Pages/Helping-Your-Child-Develop-A-Healthy-Sense-of-Self-Esteem.aspx)).

#### 8–9 years
- **Social-emotional:** prefers time with friends; Selman's model has children here anticipating how others might react to them ([Wikipedia, secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-taking_theory)). Appearance-based bullying often starts young: 54% of bullied UK 11–16-year-olds said it began by 10 ([Mental Health Foundation](https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/explore-mental-health/articles/body-image-report-executive-summary/body-image-childhood)).
- **Language and thinking:** by the end of third grade, uses subject words, summarises a story, plans and edits writing, explains what they learned ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/thirdgrade/)).
- **Movement and body:** at 9, early puberty signs such as oily skin, acne and body odour are common ([KidsHealth, 9 y](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-9yrs.html)). Breast budding can begin from 8 ([AAP, 2014](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Physical-Development-of-School-Age-Children.aspx)).
- **Sleep:** 9–12 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** body odour and pubic or underarm hair from premature adrenarche, which generally needs no treatment, though AAP still suggests seeing the doctor about early signs ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/When-Puberty-Starts-Early.aspx)); friends being same-gender ([KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-9yrs.html)).
- **Ask:** puberty signs before 8 in girls or 9 in boys ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/When-Puberty-Starts-Early.aspx)); note NHS uses "before 8" for all ([NHS, 2026](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/early-or-delayed-puberty/)).
- **Play and talk:** games with imaginary worlds such as Minecraft or Dungeons & Dragons, and crosswords or sudoku ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-7-to-12-year-olds.pdf)). Be aware of pubertal changes; answer questions simply ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Ask: "What have you heard about how bodies change?" and answer simply, modelled on the Bright Futures question ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
  2. Use a TV show or a family pregnancy as a teachable moment ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Talking-to-Your-Young-Child-About-Sex.aspx)).
  3. Make a quiet, well-lit homework spot ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/Pages/Ten-Tips-for-Your-Childs-Success-in-School.aspx)).
  4. Avoid comments on weight or looks, your own included; parents' comments and attitudes influence children ([Mental Health Foundation](https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/explore-mental-health/articles/body-image-report-executive-summary/body-image-childhood)).

#### 9–10 years
- **Social-emotional:** prefers being with friends; making and keeping friends is an important life skill ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)). Temper, limits and peers are the visit topics; give personal space at home ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
- **Language and thinking:** by the end of fourth grade, argues, jokes, understands some figurative language, takes brief notes, makes inferences ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/fourthgrade/)). Reads to learn about an interest; organises information for a report ([KidsHealth, 10 y](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-10yrs.html)).
- **Movement and body:** puberty signs may begin ([KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-10yrs.html)); breast budding typically around 10, testes enlarging around 11 ([AAP, 2014](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Physical-Development-of-School-Age-Children.aspx)).
- **Sleep:** 9–12 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** starting earlier or later than classmates; being somewhat different from peers is rarely a concern ([AAP, 2015](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Physical-Development-Whats-Normal-Whats-Not.aspx)).
- **Ask:** Bright Futures raises disordered eating at this visit and asks "How do you feel about how you look?"; children are told to talk with the clinician before trying to lose weight ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)). Germany decided on a new U10 at this age, covering school development, media use and wellbeing (see screening section).
- **Play and talk:** instruments, team sport, strategy games ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-7-to-12-year-olds.pdf)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Teach the three rules: no adult should ask you to keep secrets from parents, show interest in your private parts, or ask for help with theirs ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
  2. Ask: "What do you do when friends pressure you?" ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
  3. Let them run one thing on their own, such as an errand or a meal, with an adult stepping back ([Let Grow](https://letgrow.org/program/experience/)).
  4. Ask "What are some things you are good at?" ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).

#### 10–12 years
- **Social-emotional:** wants to be independent and fit in; focuses on appearance; may not connect actions with future consequences; drawn to risk ([KidsHealth, 11 y](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-11yrs.html)). Early adolescence (11–14) brings concrete thinking, preoccupation with body changes and questioning independence ([Bright Futures](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)). Selman's model places mutual perspective-taking at about 10–12 ([Wikipedia, secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-taking_theory)).
- **Language and thinking:** by the end of fifth grade, draws conclusions from what they hear, summarises, analyses an author's style, revises drafts ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/fifthgrade/)).
- **Movement and body:** hands and feet often grow first, which can look clumsy; girls' growth spurt is usually around 11½ (range 8–14), boys about two years later ([AAP, 2015](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Physical-Development-Whats-Normal-Whats-Not.aspx)). Periods typically begin about two years after breast development ([KidsHealth, 12 y](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-12yrs.html)).
- **Sleep:** 9–12 h ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)); 9–11 h uninterrupted to 13 in Canada ([CSEP](https://csepguidelines.ca/guidelines/children-youth/)).
- **Worried about, usually normal:** clumsiness, oily skin and acne; children the same age can start or finish puberty years apart ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Physical-Development-Whats-Normal-Whats-Not.aspx)). Feeling shy or awkward; social anxiety becomes more common in teens ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/children-and-young-adults/advice-for-parents/anxiety-in-children/)).
- **Ask:** no signs of puberty by 13 in girls or 14 in boys ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Physical-Development-of-School-Age-Children.aspx)); NHS uses not started by 15 ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/early-or-delayed-puberty/)). AAP adds adolescent depression and suicide-risk screening (from 12 as I read the grid; confirm on the visual chart) and audiometry once at 11–14 ([AAP](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/periodicity_schedule.pdf)). Routing rules for self-harm talk are in RESEARCH-2 §2.9.
- **Play and talk:** Go, chess, mahjong, sports, singing in parts ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-7-to-12-year-olds.pdf)).
- **One thing to try:**
  1. Ask, then listen more than you talk ([AAP, 2013](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Talking-to-Your-Child-About-Sex.aspx)).
  2. Agree a code word your child can use to get help in an uncomfortable situation ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Talking-to-Your-Child-About-Sex.aspx)).
  3. Name another trusted adult your child could go to ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/puberty/Pages/Talking-to-Your-Child-About-Sex.aspx)).
  4. Keep family meals going; effects on eating were stronger in older children ([Hammons & Fiese 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3387875/)).

### Inferences
- Cards should use the CDC age as the *end* of a band and the WHO window as the visible range ("many babies do this somewhere between 8 and 18 months"), which meets the "ranges, never exact dates" rule.
- AAP's "red flag" pages date from 2009–2023 and mix ages with lists written before 2022. Paraphrase as prompts ("worth mentioning"), and do not show 2009 thresholds such as "fewer than 15 words by 18 months" beside CDC's.
- For 6–12 years, "Ask" prompts should be concern-led (school, friends, worries, body changes), since no retrieved body gives age-keyed warning lists.
- The "one thing to try" items are written close to source; each needs a rewrite into PlayNest's voice and a word-budget pass (RESEARCH-2 §3.3).

### Gaps
- Raising Children's age-by-age pages (the source most directly matching these cards) could not be read; the site refused automated access.
- CDC's middle-childhood tips are gone from the index: the 6–8 URL returned an empty page and the 9–11 URL a 404. CDC's archive URLs also 404'd.
- 6–12-year "worry" lists rest mostly on NHS, AAP and KidsHealth pages, thinner than the under-5 material.
- Selman and Erikson are cited from Wikipedia. Primary sources were not reachable, so treat both as background framing, not card facts.

## How do the main frameworks compare, including the 75% debate and the walking change?

### Takeaway
CDC (≥75%, surveillance aid), WHO windows (healthy-child motor range), GSED (population measure, no norms yet) and national handbooks do different jobs, so they should not be merged into one list. The 75% debate is unresolved: the rationale is public, but the authors state the checklists were never tested for improving early identification, and no outcome study was found.

**How CDC changed (Zubler 2022, all figures from [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9680195)):**
- Eight experts, 11 criteria; 1,027 articles screened, 34 included.
- 94 retained and 65 new milestones. About a third of retained ones moved age; 21 of 31 moved older.
- 127 of 159 (79.9%) had normative data. Social-emotional and cognitive had least. 22 (13.8%) were flagged as needing more research.
- 75% was chosen to discourage "wait and see"; the authors argued 85–90% would flag too few children.
- Authors' own limits: not tested for improving early identification; placement partly expert judgement; 58.3% of normative studies came from non-English-speaking countries.
- One author developed the ASQ and receives royalties.

**The criticism:**
- ASHA was not involved, and said some changes may slow or inhibit early-intervention referrals; language milestones may be too low and may not account for multilingual children. CDC met ASHA on 28 Feb 2022 and AAP on 7 Mar 2022. CDC replied that these are communication tools, not standards, and that it had not lowered standards; AAP planned no further edits ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice/asha-comments-on-aap-and-cdc-developmental-milestones-updates)).
- ASHA published its own birth-to-5 milestones in Nov 2023 ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice/asha-comments-on-aap-and-cdc-developmental-milestones-updates)).
- A commentary called 34 articles a very small base ([MDedge, via search summary](https://mdedge.com/content/what-can-be-new-about-developmental-milestones)). Speech and physical therapy groups raised concerns ([Disability Scoop, via search summary](https://www.disabilityscoop.com/2022/04/22/changes-cdcs-developmental-milestone-checklists-met-skepticism/29818/)).
- Pandemic-masking claims were disputed: the changes were in progress before COVID ([Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/cdc-speech-children-masking/); [PolitiFact](https://www.politifact.com/article/2022/feb/28/what-know-about-cdcs-updated-developmental-milesto)).

**The walking change:**
- Current CDC wording: takes a few steps alone by 15 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html)), walks without holding on by 18 months ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)).
- Secondary sources disagree on the old position: 12 to 18 months ([PolitiFact, via search summary](https://www.politifact.com/article/2022/feb/28/what-know-about-cdcs-updated-developmental-milesto)); 15 to 18 months, also claiming 50% walk at 15 months ([unattributed magazine article](https://reader.mediawiremobile.com/accessibility/issues/208315/articles/63c6b9d4cc6bed54df67708d/reader)). That 50% figure sits badly with AAP's "many take first steps around their first birthday" ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-8-to-12-Months.aspx)).
- AAP's older parent page still says "cannot walk by eighteen months" is a flag ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Developmental-Milestones-2-Year-Olds.aspx)), so on walking, AAP's old and CDC's new positions land in the same place.

### Inferences
- The "≥75%" label is a floor, not a statement that a quarter of children have not done the thing. WHO's right bound for walking is 17.6 months in healthy children, so nearly all should be walking by CDC's 18-month line. PlayNest should say "most children", as CDC's public pages do, and not "75%".
- Since CDC's checklist can read as later than old lists, keep it as "Talk now if missing" and never as "normal if reached by this age".
- Use GSED as context only. It has no norms and no individual-use role in the sources read, and its licence is non-commercial.
- Do not reproduce AAP's chart or Bright Futures tables. Show visit ages as facts, link out, and paraphrase.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found testing whether the 75% lists delay or improve identification. Only one search was run before the budget ended, so this is "not found", not "does not exist".
- GSED status: the Household Form exists per a 2025 IADB-linked source, and revised norms were promised, but no release was confirmed ([IADB](https://blogs.iadb.org/desarrollo-infantil/en/?p=12699)).
- WHO's table labels the bounds only as left and right. They are commonly described as 1st and 99th percentiles, but this was not confirmed on the WHO pages read. The medians were not retrieved either.
- Iran: **visit timing, Sib workflows and any ASQ use could not be verified**. A Persian journal PDF on SIB failed to load, and no ministry guideline was reached. Do not show an Iran-specific schedule.
- India's MCP card itself was not retrievable (the MoHFW portal refused the connection), so card wording is unverified. A study-notes site describes green, blue and red zones for milestones, tips and warning signs, which is not an official source.
- Japan's Children and Families Agency page on check-ups returned no schedule text.

## Which well-child schedules can drive a generic "ask at your next visit" prompt, and when are screenings, hearing, vision and dental checks due?

### Takeaway
Schedules cluster around seven windows common to most countries, so a generic prompt can key off a window plus the country's own label. Screening policy genuinely disagrees: AAP screens everyone, while USPSTF finds evidence insufficient for autism, Canada recommends against routine developmental screening, and the UK does not screen for autism. Copy should therefore be "ask whether a developmental check is due here".

## What is typical for language and speech by age, and for bilinguals, late talkers, stuttering and speech sounds?

### Takeaway
Bodies disagree most on word counts, by up to a year on 50 words. Bilingual children match monolinguals when both languages are counted, and advice to drop a home language is explicitly rejected. About half to 70% of late talkers catch up, so "wait and see" is not the same as "fine", and hearing is always the first check.

**Later language (ASHA, end of grade):** kindergarten retells stories and prints first name ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/kindergarten/)); grade 1 is understood by anyone and knows 100 sight words ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/firstgrade/)); grade 2 persuades and reads independently ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/secondgrade/)); grade 3 summarises and uses subject vocabulary ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/thirdgrade/)); grade 4 uses figurative language and inference ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/fourthgrade/)); grade 5 summarises and analyses style ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/fifthgrade/)).

**Bilingual children**
- Hoff et al. followed children from about 22 to 31 months. Monolinguals were ahead in single-language English vocabulary and grammar; when both languages were counted, bilinguals matched them in total size and rate of gain. Word combining at 2;1 and 2;6 was near-universal in both groups. Development tracked relative exposure, and the authors say "it takes longer to acquire two languages than one", calling the single-language lag normal ([Hoff 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4323282/)).
- Bilingual children are not more likely to have delays or language disorders; mixing is normal and does not mean confusion; count across languages without double-counting translation equivalents; one-person-one-language is neither necessary nor sufficient; a minority language may need slightly more input; TV alone is not enough; use a language you are comfortable in; if concerned, see a speech-language pathologist with bilingual expertise ([Byers-Heinlein & Lew-Williams 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6168212/)).
- ASHA: two languages do not cause late language emergence; monolingualism should not be recommended; assess all languages; evaluate if there is difficulty in both ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/late-language-emergence/)).
- NHS agrees: use whichever language feels comfortable, and one parent per language works well ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/help-your-baby-learn-to-talk/)).
- Harvard's guides state bilingual children have better executive function ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-6-to-18-month-olds.pdf)).

**Late talkers** ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/late-language-emergence/))
- Prevalence: mostly 10–20% at 2 y; about 13.5% at 18–23 months and 16–17.5% at 30–36 months; boys about three times more likely.
- Outcomes: 50–70% catch up by late preschool or school age. At 7, 20% had language impairment vs 11% of controls. Receptive delay and smaller vocabulary predict poorer outcomes.
- Protective: daily book sharing, informal play, childcare centre attendance. Review language about every 6 months.
- AAP: tell the doctor if your child stops talking; if told they will catch up but you are still worried, seek a second opinion or contact early intervention; no formal diagnosis is needed for those services ([AAP, 2021](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Language-Delay.aspx)).

Normal disfluency: occasional repeats and "uh", most often at about 1.5–5 y ([Stuttering Foundation](https://www.stutteringhelp.org/if-you-think-your-child-stuttering)); filler sounds and whole-word or phrase repeats; stuttering looks like sound repeats, stretched sounds and blocks ([CHSA](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/stuttering-and-cluttering)).

**Speech sounds (McLeod & Crowe 2018)** ([AJSLP, RCSLT copy](https://rcslt.org/wp-content/uploads/media/Project/RCSLT/childrens-consonant-acquisition.pdf))
- Review of 64 studies, 26,007 children, 31 countries, 27 languages. Most consonants acquired by 5;0, with individual variability.
- Percent consonants correct: 63.50 at 2;0 and 93.80 at 5;0. Percent vowels correct: 88.20 and 98.02.
- Plosives and nasals come earlier than trills, flaps, fricatives and affricates.
- English, 90–100% criterion: **early (2;0–3;11)** p, b, m, d, n, h, t, k, g, w, ng, f, y; **middle (4;0–4;11)** l, j, ch, s, v, sh, z; **late (5;0–6;11)** r, zh, voiced th, voiceless th.
- Criterion matters: at 75–85%, 11 English consonants arrive by 2;0–2;11, and r comes at 4;0–4;11.
- Languages differ. Japanese: early m, t, p, g, k and others at 3;0–3;11; z at 5;0–5;11. The paper tells clinicians to find studies for the child's own language.
- ASHA's sound lists sit in the same range: p, b, m, h, w, d, n by 2–3 ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-2-to-3-years/)); t, k, g, f, y by 3–4, with l, r, sh, ch, s, th still possibly off ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-3-to-4-years/)).

### Inferences
- Show CDC as the "talk to your doctor" line and ASHA as the "what helps most children" line, with a note that the bodies differ. RESEARCH-2 §2.10's table should gain a source column so the 50-word gap (ASHA 19–24 m, CDC 30 m) is visible.
- A "words my child says" list (RESEARCH-2 §2.10) must count across languages from day one and must not double-count "water" and its translation.
- PlayNest's stuttering card can say "many children go through this; ask a speech therapist if it lasts about 6 months or longer, started after about 3½, or your child struggles or avoids talking". This is the common ground across bodies, and NHS would advise asking sooner.
- A speech-sound card should be per language, since English order does not transfer.

### Gaps
- Persian, Hindi, and others are absent from McLeod & Crowe's 27 languages, so PlayNest has no cross-checked sound order for them.
- Harvard's bilingual executive-function claim is, from memory, contested in later meta-analyses. Not verified, so do not use it as a benefit claim until checked.
- Recent Byers-Heinlein empirical work on bilingual milestones was not retrieved; only the 2013 review was.
- ASHA's bilingual consumer page returned 404; the Practice Portal covers the position.

## What do educators and health bodies actually recommend for school readiness?

### Takeaway
There is a real split. England's inspectorate pushed direct teaching of reading and maths in Reception in 2017, while AAP stresses social-emotional skills and warns against early academic pressure and readiness gatekeeping. The "Nordic play until 7" image is partly out of date: Sweden and Finland both now have a compulsory pre-school year at 6, before formal school at 7.

### Cited Findings
- **AAP (Nov 2025):** social, emotional and behavioural skills are as critical as academics; the idea of being ready at a fixed age is controversial; labelling a child "not ready" and delaying can keep them from the best learning environment; youngest-in-class problems usually fade by grade 3–4; being older than classmates carries greater risk of adolescent behaviour problems; readiness tests should guide teaching, not gatekeep ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Is-Your-Child-Ready-for-School.aspx)).
- **AAP on pressure:** pushing academics early gives no real advantage ([AAP, 2009](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Cognitive-Development-In-Preschool-Children.aspx)).
- **Bold Beginnings (Ofsted, 30 Nov 2017):** "the teaching of reading, including systematic synthetic phonics, is the core purpose of the Reception Year"; sufficient daily direct teaching of reading, writing and maths; correct pencil grip and table posture. It also says play was important in all schools visited, and heads knew what to teach directly and what through play ([Ofsted PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/663560/28933_Ofsted_-_Early_Years_Curriculum_Report_-_Accessible.pdf); [landing page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/reception-curriculum-in-good-and-outstanding-primary-schools-bold-beginnings)).
- **Hungry Little Minds:** the old campaign URL now redirects to an NHS Start4Life talking page for 3–5s, with chat, play and read tips such as open questions, pretend shops, "I spy", rhyme, and acting out stories ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/start4life/baby/learning-to-talk/3-to-5-years)). The NHS page names no campaign, so treat the campaign as folded in.
- **Ready4K:** an RCT of an eight-month text programme for preschool parents raised parental involvement by 0.15–0.29 SD and child early literacy by about 0.11 SD (York, Loeb & Doss, *J Human Resources* 2019) ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20659)).
- **Finland:** one compulsory pre-primary year, at least 700 hours, before comprehensive school starts at 7 ([Eurydice, Sep 2026](https://eurydice.eacea.ec.europa.eu/national-education-systems/finland/overview)).
- **Sweden:** preschool class compulsory since 2018 from the year a child turns 6; grundskola at 7 to 16; over 90% in preschool ([Eurydice, Sep 2026](https://eurydice.eacea.ec.europa.eu/national-education-systems/sweden/overview)).
- **Bright Futures:** ready to learn means regular bedtime and healthy breakfast; tour the school; attend events ([BF](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)). AAP tips: routine, launch pad, read daily, know the teacher ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/Pages/Ten-Tips-for-Your-Childs-Success-in-School.aspx)).
- **Everyday readiness items:** toilet independence, listening, social skills, running and using a crayon ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Is-Your-Child-Ready-for-School.aspx)); full name, knowing helpers, role-playing school ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html)).

### Inferences
- A "getting ready for school" card should lead with self-care, turn-taking and talk, and treat letters as play. This fits AAP, CDC and the NHS page; a UK locale can add that Reception teaches phonics directly.
- Ready4K supports PlayNest's short-nudge format, but effect sizes are small and the trial speaks to one programme's messages, not to nudges in general.
- Do not claim "Nordic children don't start until 7".

### Gaps
- NAEYC's readiness statement could not be read (403). Its position is unverified.
- Critiques of Bold Beginnings (e.g. early-years sector responses) were not retrieved.
- Later Ready4K trials with weaker or varied results, from memory, were not retrieved; check before citing.
- Reported Swedish plans for a 10-year compulsory school and Finland's two-year pre-primary pilot were not confirmed; the Eurydice pages read do not mention them.
- Evidence comparing play-based and formal early curricula on long-term outcomes was not retrieved.

## What is the evidence on routines, bedtime, family meals, school mornings and visual schedules?

### Takeaway
Routine evidence is strong in association and weak on causation. Mindell's 13-country dataset shows a dose-response with sleep; family meals show modest effects. Visual schedules are a strong evidence-based practice for autistic children, but evidence for typical children was not found.

### Cited Findings
- **Mindell 2015** (*Sleep* 38(5):717–722): 10,085 mothers; 13 countries and regions; children 0–5.11 y. More routine nights a week went with earlier bedtimes, shorter time to fall asleep, fewer and shorter wakings, more total sleep, and fewer daytime behaviour problems. Parent-reported sleep problems in infants and toddlers: 23.3% with a nightly routine vs 47.2% with none. Earlier start in infancy went with better later sleep. Routines ≥3 nights a week: 82.6% in predominantly Caucasian regions, 64.4% in predominantly Asian regions. Limits: parent report, routine content not assessed, online sample skewing educated and urban, correlational ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4402657/)). This resolves RESEARCH-2's [verify] note.
- **Hammons & Fiese 2011** (*Pediatrics* 127(6):e1565): 17 studies, 182,836 children. 3+ vs under 3 shared meals a week: overweight OR 0.88, unhealthy eating 0.80, healthy eating 1.24. 5+ vs ≤1: disordered eating 0.65. Mostly cross-sectional; longitudinal OR 0.93; substantial heterogeneity ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3387875/)).
- **School mornings:** AAP practice advice only: consistent schedule, launch pad, clothes laid out the night before, steps with reminders ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/Pages/Ten-Tips-for-Your-Childs-Success-in-School.aspx); [AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Growing-Independence-Tips-for-Parents-of-Young-Children.aspx)).
- **Consistency across carers:** keep bedtime routines and mealtimes the same for childminders and nurseries; wait until the child seems ready, and wait a few weeks if it does not work ([NHS, Mar 2026](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/teaching-your-child-everyday-skills/)).
- **Bedtime:** "brush, book, bed", routines that work anywhere, screens off 60 minutes before bed ([AAP, Aug 2026](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/sleep/Pages/healthy-sleep-habits-how-many-hours-does-your-child-need.aspx)). NHS: bath, night clothes, story, dim lights, cuddle ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/helping-your-baby-to-sleep/)).
- **Visual supports:** meets evidence-based practice criteria with 104 single-case and 2 group studies, effective from 0–2 y to 19–22 y on the spectrum, including adaptive and self-help outcomes (NCAEP 2020, AFIRM 2024 update) ([AFIRM](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Visual-Supports-Brief-Packet.pdf)).
- **Autism context:** changes in routine can cause distress ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/autism/signs/children/)). In typical toddlers, strong reactions to changed routine are also normal ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/understanding-toddler-behavior-whats-typical-and-when-to-seek-help/)), so copy must not imply a link.

### Inferences
- PlayNest's routine builder can honestly say "families with a regular bedtime routine tend to report better sleep" but not "routines cause better sleep". The dose-response supports a "more nights counts" design over streak-breaking, which also fits the §3.1 grow-only tally.
- Meal count: show frequency as encouragement, without a 3 or 5-meal target.
- Visual schedules can be offered to all families as a plain tool, with the evidence claim limited to autistic children.
- Mindell's Asian/Caucasian difference suggests routine content should not assume bath-and-book. Let parents define it.

### Gaps
- Fiese et al. 2002 could not be read (APA paywall redirect), so "50 years of routines research" is not cited here.
- No trial evidence on school-morning routines was found.
- No evidence for visual schedules in typically developing children was retrieved (search budget ended).

## What should "prepare for your visit" lists and a one-page visit summary contain?

### Takeaway
CDC and AAP give short, compatible prep lists: top concerns first, what the child does and enjoys, and lost skills. CDC's Milestone Tracker already models a shareable summary, which sets a floor for PlayNest's one-pager. A summary must carry observations, never scores or labels.

### Cited Findings
- **AAP:** list topics, narrow to your top 3–5, and raise them at the start of the visit ([AAP, Mar 2026](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/health-management/Pages/Well-Child-Care-A-Check-Up-for-Success.aspx)).
- **CDC, things to share:** what you do together, what your child enjoys, anything they do or do not do that concerns you, lost skills, special health needs or prematurity; if still concerned, ask for a specialist referral and contact early intervention ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)).
- **CDC Milestone Tracker:** 2 months to 5 years; iOS and Android; English and Spanish; corrected age; photos and videos of milestones; PDF or email **summary for doctors**; **well-visit snapshot**; appointment tracking and screening reminders; says it does not replace screening tools and does not collect identifying data ([CDC, Feb 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones-app/index.html)).
- **AAP on second opinions:** if told your child will catch up and you remain worried, ask for a referral, or contact early intervention (under 3) or the school district (3+) ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Language-Delay.aspx)).
- **AAP on emotional concerns:** write down day-to-day observations, since a routine visit may not show them ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Emotional-and-Social-Development-4-7-Months.aspx)).
- **Bright Futures sample questions:** "How are you getting along as a family?", "What concerns do you have about your child's ability to do well in school?", "How much do you know about your child's internet use?" ([BF](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)). By the 7 or 8-year visit it is appropriate to offer part of the visit without the parent present, and most clinicians excuse the parent for part of it by 12 ([BF](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
- **NHS:** parents complete an ASQ-3 before the 9–12-month and 2–2½-year reviews, with help if needed; premature babies' age is calculated from the due date until 2 ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/babys-development/height-weight-and-reviews/baby-reviews/)).
- **Germany's U10:** parents complete KIDSSCREEN before the visit ([KBV](https://www.kbv.de/praxis/tools-und-services/praxisnachrichten/2026/08-20/g-ba-beschliesst-neue-frueherkennungsuntersuchung-fuer-9-bis-10-jaehrige)).
- **eRedbook:** shows that a digital record can share read-only or read-write with carers ([eRedbook](https://www.eredbook.org.uk/)).

### Inferences
**One-page visit summary, suggested contents:**
1. Child's first name, age (and corrected age if premature), languages at home, country.
2. **Top 3 questions** (per AAP's 3–5 rule).
3. "What we've noticed": plain sentences with a date range, such as "since about June".
4. Any skills that seem to have gone; always first if present.
5. CDC milestones for this age marked "seen / not sure / not yet" with attribution (US-friendly; public domain), and no percent or score.
6. What the child enjoys and what you do together (CDC).
7. Sleep, eating and toileting patterns as ranges.
8. Words the child uses across languages (count without double-counting).
9. Growth trend link (RESEARCH-2 §2.5), vaccines from the region pack, hearing, vision and dental dates.
10. Past visits and screenings, with the source and date of each, and what was advised.
11. Prematurity, special needs, family history of speech, hearing or stuttering.
12. Space for notes and the next step.

Never include: scores, percentile verdicts, "delay", "risk", "autism" or the words "failed" or "behind". Footer: "Parent observations for discussion. Not a screening or diagnosis."

Pre-visit questions can be chosen from a short list per window, such as "Is a development check due here?", "When should the hearing or vision check be?", "When's the first or next dentist visit?", "We speak two languages; how should we count words?".

## What firsts should a Memories feature let parents log?

### Takeaway
No health body publishes a "firsts" list, but handbooks prove the habit is universal, from Japan's dated notes to eRedbook's photo timeline. Keep Memories strictly separate from milestone tracking, so a date logged for joy is never read as a developmental score.

### Cited Findings
- CDC's definition of a milestone uses the same language: a first step, a first smile, waving bye-bye ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html)).
- Japanese mothers wrote dated notes in handbooks, such as "Stood for the first time. So happy!" ([Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-topics/g02028/)).
- eRedbook offers a personal photo record of developmental milestones, described by a parent as a timeline of "firsts" ([eRedbook](https://www.eredbook.org.uk/)).
- First-tooth timing is about 6 months, and teething notes are in RESEARCH-2 §2.6. AAP says first steps come around the first birthday, earlier or later being normal ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/Movement-8-to-12-Months.aspx)).

### Inferences
Suggested starter list (editorial, drawn from milestones and life events above):
- **0–12 m:** first real smile, laugh, roll, tooth, solid food, sit, crawl or scoot, wave, word-like sound, bath at home, trip outside, night in own space, visit with grandparents.
- **1–3 y:** first steps, word, two-word phrase, song, friend, joke, drawing, haircut, day at childcare, "I did it myself", bed without a cot, dry day.
- **3–6 y:** first story told, name written, bike or scooter ride, swim, sleepover at family, day of school, lost tooth, best friend.
- **6–12 y:** first book read alone, sport or concert, walk to a friend's, meal cooked, money saved up, bike ride alone, chore owned, school trip, solo errand, brave moment.
- **Rules:** cultural and faith events (first fast, naming day, Nowruz, Eid, Diwali) should be free text or locale packs. Firsts should be parent-initiated rather than pushed by age, since prompts can hurt families of children developing differently or after a loss. Puberty firsts should be opt-in and never shared automatically. Log the date as a keepsake, but never display "early", "late" or a peer comparison, and do not feed Memories dates into milestone checklists without the parent's explicit choice.

### Gaps
- No authoritative or peer-reviewed list of "firsts" was found, and the commercial baby-book apps' lists were not retrieved after the search budget ended.

## Licences, access problems and items to confirm before shipping

### Takeaway
Licences restrict more than expected: AAP's schedule needs written permission to reproduce, Stop It Now forbids derivatives, and MedlinePlus's encyclopedia forbids AI-related use. Facts can be cited and paraphrased, but tables and text cannot be lifted.

### Cited Findings
- CDC: public-domain reuse rules are in RESEARCH-2 §2.1; the milestone pages themselves state no licence ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html)).
- AAP periodicity: no reproduction without written permission, except one personal copy ([AAP](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/periodicity_schedule.pdf)). It also states that milestones in the pocket guide are not validated for screening ([BF](https://downloads.aap.org/AAP/PDF/Bright%20Futures/BF4_POCKETGUIDE.pdf)).
- WHO GSED: CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO ([WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-MSD-GSED-package-v1.0-2023.1)).
- Stop It Now tip sheet: CC BY-NC-ND 3.0 US ([Stop It Now](https://www.stopitnow.org/ohc-content/age-appropriate-sexual-behavior)).
- Rourke: "freely available" tool, but all rights reserved ([CPS](https://cps.ca/tools-outils/rourke-baby-record)). © Rourke, Leduc and Rourke 2017 on the guide site ([RBR](https://www.rourkebabyrecord.ca/rbr2020)).
- ZERO TO THREE: all rights reserved, with a reprints page ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/your-childs-development-age-based-tips-from-birth-to-36-months/)).
- Harvard: no licence stated on the guide page, with a suggested citation ([Harvard](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/activities-guide-enhancing-and-practicing-executive-function-skills-with-children-from-infancy-to-adolescence/)).
- Looksee: sold through a shop, with its own terms ([Looksee](https://lookseechecklist.com/en/)).
- MedlinePlus A.D.A.M. encyclopedia: personal non-commercial viewing only ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002017.htm)).
- Page review dates that have already passed at the time of writing: NHS tantrums (due Nov 2025), eye tests (Mar 2026), stammering (Mar 2026), speech and talking (Apr 2026), activity for babies (Mar 2026), each shown on the page.

### Inferences
