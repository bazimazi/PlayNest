## Universal design for play: what are the principles?

### Takeaway
No trial tests "universal design for home play". The usable principles come from playground design, learning design and the disability movement: remove the barrier rather than fix the child, offer several ways to take part, and trust the parent's observation of their own child. Treat these as design values, not as proven outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- The Me2 "7 Principles of Inclusive Playground Design" are Be Fair, Be Included, Be Smart, Be Independent, Be Safe, Be Active and Be Comfortable, tailored from NC State's original universal design principles (read) — [Concord NH copy of the principles](https://www.concordnh.gov/DocumentCenter/View/24274/7-Principles-of-Inclusive-Playground-Design)
- Sub-points that carry over to the home: choices in how to participate (2c), graduated challenge (2b), behavioural cues and sensory feedback (3a), visual supports (4a), "cozy spots" for sensory relief and "jump-in points" to watch before joining (5b), equitable alternatives such as upper-body activities (6b), reciprocal interaction (6c), and comfortable reach whether seated or standing (7a) (read) — [Concord NH copy](https://www.concordnh.gov/DocumentCenter/View/24274/7-Principles-of-Inclusive-Playground-Design)
- GameTime says Me2 was developed with Utah State University's Center for Persons with Disabilities (read) — [GameTime](https://www.gametime.com/news/7-principles-unlimited-possibilities). "Be Fair" also covers multigenerational play (read) — [GameTime, Be Fair](https://www.gametime.com/news/inclusive-playgrounds-101-be-fair)
- CAST's UDL Guidelines 3.0 (30 July 2024) ask for multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression, with learner agency as the goal (read) — [CAST](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/)
- Microsoft's inclusive design principles are recognise exclusion, learn from diversity, and solve for one and extend to many (read) — [Microsoft](https://inclusive.microsoft.design/)
- The social model, developed by disabled people, says people are disabled by barriers rather than by their impairment. Scope adds that how a person talks about their own impairment is up to them (read) — [Scope](https://www.scope.org.uk/social-model-of-disability/)
- CanChild's "F-words" (Functioning, Family, Fitness, Fun, Friends, Future; Rosenbaum and Gorter) are a strength-based frame built on WHO's ICF. Fun is one of the six (read) — [CanChild](https://www.canchild.ca/en/research-in-practice/f-words-in-childhood-disability)
- RNIB's guide tells parents to observe how their child plays, says the parent is the best judge regardless of age labels, and quotes a parent who set milestones aside (read) — [RNIB Let's Play, 2019](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
- Hesperian's guide for blind children opens from "you are the expert about your child", with the child leading and activities adapted (read, contents page only) — [Hesperian](https://en.hesperian.org/hhg/Helping_Children_Who_Are_Blind)
- Scope's May 2022 Opinium poll of 1,000 parents and carers of disabled children aged 12 or under found: 49% say the local playground is not accessible, 11% say their child was hurt on inaccessible equipment, 13% could not play as a whole family because siblings could not play together, and 15% report a child upset and disappointed. Scope calls 13% "one in seven", which does not match (read) — [Scope](https://www.scope.org.uk/media/press-releases/playground-accessibility)
- A later Scope survey of more than 1,000 playgrounds found most had little or no accessible equipment, and fed an accessibility map (excerpt) — [Barnet Post](https://barnetpost.co.uk/2024/03/25/charity-launches-national-accessible-playground-map/)
- ToyLikeMe works on disability representation in toys, including toy hearing aids and cochlear implants (read) — [ToyLikeMe](https://www.toylikeme.org/). RNIB credits Rebecca Atkinson, who is partially sighted, as its founder (read) — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
- Booktouch is for blind or partially sighted children and Bookshine for deaf children. Packs for ages 0-2 and 3-5 hold two books, a rhyme mat and guidance, and some councils now use the names Bookstart Touch and Bookstart Shine (excerpt) — [Northamptonshire council](https://www.westnorthants.gov.uk/children-and-young-peoples-library/bookstart-scheme); [Surrey council](https://surreycc.gov.uk/people-and-community/families/education-and-learning-advice-for-families/early-learning/bookstart). The BookTrust page I opened lists Bookstart programmes but does not describe these packs (read) — [BookTrust](https://www.booktrust.org.uk/what-we-do/programmes-and-campaigns/bookstart/families/additional-needs-packs/)

### Inferences
- [D] A home version of Me2 for PlayNest:
  - Be Fair: same game, everyone has a real job.
  - Be Included: several ways to join.
  - Be Smart: the child can see or feel what happens next.
  - Be Independent: they can start it themselves.
  - Be Safe: there is a place to retreat to and a place to watch from.
  - Be Active: some part of the game uses the body they have.
  - Be Comfortable: it fits where they are sitting or lying.
- [D] Keep "adaptations" separate from PlayNest's "easier/harder" versions. A child who needs a different way in does not need an easier game, and blending the two tells families their child is the problem.
- [D] Fun should be the stated goal. Therapy framing turns play into homework, and the F-words give respectable cover for putting fun first.

## Adapting play for low vision and blindness

### Takeaway
RNIB's Let's Play is the best single source I could read in full. Use real objects before toy stand-ins, plain contrast and good light, things kept within reach and contained, and short descriptive narration. Evidence is practice and parent experience rather than trials.

### Cited Findings
- Look for colour and tone contrast, bold lettering, tactile variety (test it yourself, since felt and velvet can feel alike), scent, switches you can identify by touch, and sound as confirmation that something happened (read) — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
- Set-up: good light, plain backgrounds, finding out whether the child sees better on one side, a position that frees hands and eyes, toys suspended or kept in a container, and taking the child to a toy that rolled away rather than fetching it back (read) — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
- Let children explore with mouth and feet as well as hands, and use short descriptive language tied to what they are doing (read) — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
- "The Duckiness of Ducks" (a term from Prof. Mike McLinden): a rubber duck says little about a real duck to a child relying on touch, so pair toys with the real thing and multi-sensory experience. Parent examples include feathers and a petting farm (read) — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
- Low-cost adaptations from parents: fabric squares on sound-book buttons, a texture-door board made from wipe-packet lids, tactile snap with fabric pairs, rice-filled yoghurt-pot shakers, puffy-marker outlines, and a story bag of objects (read) — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
- Busy playgroups may overwhelm some children while others love the rough and tumble. Options include small groups or arriving at the start or end (read) — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
- WonderBaby (written by the parent of a blind child): prefer mainstream toys with small changes, keep toys in the same place, add braille, texture, handles and props, and expect that play may need modelling (read) — [WonderBaby](https://www.wonderbaby.org/articles/toy-guide-for-blind-children)
- WHO/UNICEF give household materials that make noise as an example for a child with a visual impairment (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
- Perkins lists "Taking turns" and an "Echo playing" drum activity (excerpt; the pages returned 403) — [Perkins, Taking turns](https://www.perkins.org/resource/taking-turns/); [Perkins, Learning to play](https://www.perkins.org/resource/learning-to-play/)
- Book-sharing tips from the Booktouch guide: check the story does not depend on the pictures, and value touch-and-feel books (excerpt; exact page unconfirmed) — [Bookstart local page, best match](https://www.northnorthants.gov.uk/children-and-young-peoples-library/bookstart-scheme)
- FamilyConnect (American Printing House for the Blind) has Browse by Age and Ideas for Home sections, but the landing page has no tips (read) — [FamilyConnect](https://aphconnectcenter.org/familyconnect/)
- **Conflict to handle:** WonderBaby advises redirecting a child from rocking or flapping to play (read) — [WonderBaby](https://www.wonderbaby.org/articles/toy-guide-for-blind-children). RNIB says to try to develop repeated behaviours into something more creative (read) — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf). The National Autistic Society says harmless stimming has no need to stop (read) — [NAS](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/topics/behaviour/stimming/all-audiences). These speak to different populations, but PlayNest needs one voice.

### Inferences
- [D] PlayNest's voice on repetition: join it, build on it if the child wants, and do not stop it unless someone is getting hurt.
- [D] Sound-led world games are natural fits. The writer should check PlayNest's entries for Kagome Kagome and Mbube Mbube to see whether guessing with eyes covered already levels the field.

### Gaps
- Perkins and NFB content could not be opened. FamilyConnect's inner pages were not read.

## Adapting play for deaf and hard-of-hearing children

### Takeaway
The practical advice is strong and consistent: get attention first, face the child in good light, and give visual versions of every sound cue. On sign and speech together, the evidence is unsettled and small, so PlayNest should not take a side on communication mode.

### Cited Findings
- Touch the child and make sure they can see you, then wait for attention. Follow what they are looking at, move your signs into their line of sight, copy their sounds or signs and wait, leave gaps in songs, pause in peek-a-boo, put a mirror on the car seat, greet on a swing, and sign and talk during nappy changes and baths (read) — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/communicating-your-deaf-baby-or-toddler)
- Keep faces well lit, sit close (hearing aids and implants work best near the speaker), turn off the TV or music, use soft furnishings, use flashing-light alerts, and schedule quiet time because communicating can take deaf children more energy (read) — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/creating-good-communication-environment)
- Tips drawn from deaf and hard-of-hearing adults: keep a light on at night so hands and face stay visible; walk side by side; bring baby to the door so people don't seem to appear from nowhere; notice floor vibrations together; use car mirrors; put the high chair against a wall or use a round table; choose visual rather than audible games such as photo hunts; use visual schedules and "how many sleeps" countdowns; turn on captions; and meet D/HH adults (read) — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
- Hands & Voices names "dinner table syndrome": a deaf child is told "it's nothing" when they missed a conversation (read) — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
- Some families remove devices in the car for a time, and visual communication then matters more (read) — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
- A Pediatrics systematic review of early sign plus oral versus oral only found 11 cohort studies, 8 with only cochlear-implant users with severe to profound loss, and insufficient high-quality evidence either way (abstract) — [Fitzpatrick et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2015-1974). A summary adds no conclusive evidence that sign interferes with spoken language (excerpt) — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/da578c7e-8b65-45c0-b100-f3400fd9586c)
- A 2025 review of 6 studies and 259 participants reports that sign exposure benefits language in children with hearing aids or implants, with spoken skills similar or better. The evidence is small, and it gives little on hearing parents' use (read, landing page) — [Jarollahi et al. 2025](https://jmr.tums.ac.ir/index.php/jmr/article/view/1264)
- Speaking too slowly or too loudly makes lipreading harder (excerpt; exact page unconfirmed, and it is not on the NDCS page I read, so find the source before using it) — [Leeds guidance, best match](https://leedsscp-act.leeds.gov.uk/working_with_families/deaf_children)
- Hesperian's guide for deaf children covers choosing a language, sign language, spoken language, and implants (read, contents page only) — [Hesperian](https://en.hesperian.org/hhg/Helping_Children_Who_Are_Deaf)

### Inferences
- [D] Suggested wording: "Families use sign, speech or both. The studies are small and don't settle which mix is best, and there's no good evidence that signing holds back speech. Your child's team and deaf adults can help you decide."
- [D] Keep "baby sign" for hearing babies apart from natural sign languages such as BSL and ASL. Do not present a language as a stepping stone.
- [D] Every PlayNest game with a sound cue (Freeze dance, Musical statues, Pass the Parcel, Stop-and-go) needs a visual cue line.

### Gaps
- NDCS's developmental play chart would not connect. I found no verified guidance on water play and hearing devices or on baby-sign evidence in hearing infants.

## Adapting play for physical disabilities, low muscle tone and cerebral palsy

### Takeaway
Change position often, free the hands, bring play to the child, and make "any movement counts" the rule. Positioning for a particular child belongs to their therapist, so PlayNest should offer ideas and always defer. Evidence is thin: seating evidence is rated limited, and I could not verify switch-toy guidance.

### Cited Findings
- Hesperian's guidance: change positions often, aim for hands in use in front of the eyes, make games that invite reaching, twisting and rolling, and use local materials such as sandbags, padding and wedges (read) — [Hesperian, Disabled Village Children](https://en.hesperian.org/hhg/Disabled_Village_Children:How_Can_We_Help%3F)
- Hesperian says W-sitting should usually be discouraged but can be allowed if it is the only way a child can sit and use their hands (read) — [Hesperian](https://en.hesperian.org/hhg/Disabled_Village_Children:How_Can_We_Help%3F). I did not verify current therapy views on this, and its "corrective" language is dated.
- Side-lying is suggested as a good play position because the child can reach and see toys, with a pillow between the legs for hip comfort (excerpt) — [EACH handout](https://www.each.org.uk/app/uploads/2021/10/physical-positioning-ideas-for-play.pdf). Alternate positions through the day (excerpt) — [Physio-pedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Module_3:_Positioning_Your_Child)
- Back support can free the hands in sitting (excerpt) — [Your Therapy Source](https://www.yourtherapysource.com/blog1/2017/02/08/positioning-for-play/). Evidence that adaptive seating enhances play is limited, level 2b (excerpt) — [McGill CP Engine](https://www.mcgill.ca/cpengine/node/690)
- Toy changes: enlarge or mark buttons, extend levers, add handles, and add props so a toy stays upright for a child sitting or lying down (read) — [WonderBaby](https://www.wonderbaby.org/articles/toy-guide-for-blind-children)
- LEAP-CP coaches caregivers to use activities and readily available materials at home, for infants 3 to 18 months at high risk of CP. It is delivered by community disability workers, with priority in employment for mothers of a disabled child (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
- Baby Ubuntu groups are co-led by a health worker and an expert parent. Ugandan pre/post evaluations report a 15-25% rise in family quality of life, with a cluster trial due in 2026 (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
- AAC can be accessed with arms, feet, head or eyes (read) — [CHSA/ASHA](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/augmentative-and-alternative-communication-aac)
- Pathways.org is free, reviewed by more than 70 paediatric experts, and states that its material is not therapy, diagnosis or treatment (read) — [Pathways.org](https://pathways.org/)

### Inferences
- [D] Standard line: "If a therapist has shown you positions or given you limits, those come first."
- [D] Give the child a real job when a game needs running (caller, starter, referee). Make the role rotate, so it is not a consolation prize.
- [D] Switch toys should be described only in general terms until sourced, with a reminder about batteries using PlayNest's existing `aap-button-batteries` source.

### Gaps
- Switch toys, battery interrupters and access methods: no source verified. A starting point listed in the WHO brief, not read: [UNICEF/WHO assistive technology paper](https://www.unicef.org/media/126246/file/Assistive-Tech-Web.pdf).
- CP Foundation would not connect. Pathways.org's inner pages on tone were not read. Evidence-ranked CP reviews were not reached.

## Adapting play for children with Down syndrome

### Takeaway
Build on commonly described strengths in visual learning, imitation, signing and hands-on activity, with lots of repetition and floor play. The one direct toy guide I could read is old and uses language PlayNest should not copy, and I could not open NDSS guidance.

### Cited Findings
- A British Toy and Hobby Association leaflet, prepared with a Down's syndrome society, lists strengths in visual learning, sign and gesture, copying others and hands-on learning. It advises being a play partner, showing how toys work, using repetitive songs, avoiding surprises, not pressuring, and letting the child lead before joining in (read) — [BTHA leaflet](https://southerntrust.hscni.net/download/165/children-with-disabilities/2887/toys-and-play-for-young-children-with-downs-syndrome.pdf)
- That file dates from about 2004-2006 and uses phrases such as "almost normal life", "Down's Syndrome children" and "weaknesses", plus advice about preventing "repetitive patterns". Use it for ideas only and do not reproduce its framing (read) — [BTHA leaflet](https://southerntrust.hscni.net/download/165/children-with-disabilities/2887/toys-and-play-for-young-children-with-downs-syndrome.pdf)
- NHS Fife recommends tummy time, floor play and supported standing and walking, with lots of repetition through play (excerpt; the page returned 403) — [NHS Fife](https://www.nhsfife.org/services/all-services/patient-advice/children-with-down-syndrome-motor-development-children-and-young-people/)
- A single-case study found preferred stimuli during tummy time increased head lifting in an infant with Down syndrome. It is one case only (excerpt) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40617-018-0212-5)
- The Down's Syndrome Association uses "people who have Down's syndrome", offers Easy Read material and has baby and early-years sections (read) — [DSA](https://www.downs-syndrome.org.uk/). WHO uses "Down syndrome" (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)

### Inferences
- [D] Use "Down's syndrome" in UK English and "Down syndrome" elsewhere, and write "children with" or "children who have" it, following the organisations above.
- [D] The child's strength in copying makes "show it, then wait" the core technique. The same line helps many children, so it can live in the general toolkit.

### Gaps
- NDSS pages returned 404, and Global Down Syndrome was not reached. DSA's early-play pages were not opened.
- Neck instability and rough-and-tumble or somersault play is a safety question I could not verify. Do not write about it without a clinical source. Route it to the child's doctor.

## Autistic children: child-led and strength-based play, sensory differences, echolalia and the named programmes

### Takeaway
What parents can safely take from these programmes is simple: join the child's interest, wait, respond, and use daily routines. The programmes themselves need trained staff, and the best meta-analysis finds the evidence is weaker than the marketing and that harms are barely monitored. Autistic adults mostly prefer "autistic". Stimming that harms no one has no need to stop.

### Cited Findings
**Programmes and evidence**
- Project AIM updated its review of non-drug interventions for autistic children under 8, with 252 studies and 13,304 participants. Adverse events were poorly monitored, so benefits could not be weighed against harms (abstract) — [Sandbank et al., BMJ 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-076733)
- Even among randomised trials, many did not guard against placebo-by-proxy or detection bias, and few popular interventions have support from the strongest evidence. The lead author also stresses that weak evidence does not mean an intervention is ineffective, and warns that families told an approach has strong evidence may blame themselves when it fails (read) — [UNC Health](https://news.unchealthcare.org/2023/11/researchers-reveal-low-quality-studies-of-autism-early-interventions-dominate-the-field)
- PACT followed about 80% of 152 children, at a mean age of 10.5. Symptom severity favoured the intervention. Child-initiated interaction improved across the full study period, though the follow-up effect alone was smaller and its confidence interval crossed zero. There was no language difference at follow-up (abstract) — [Pickles et al., Lancet 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736(16)31229-6)
- A 2021 review of JASPER found 19 articles reporting a positive effect on at least one outcome and called it promising but needing more study (excerpt) — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/237ce325-b5bf-4515-8569-fc11a51eaa10)
- A 2024 network meta-analysis reported parent-mediated ESDM improved language and motor skills (excerpt; I could not confirm which result carried this, so identify the paper before citing it) — [PubMed 38664754, best match](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38664754/)
- More Than Words is led by a Hanen-certified speech-language pathologist: 8 group sessions plus 3 individual sessions with video feedback. It teaches what motivates the child, using strengths to set goals, and extending back-and-forth. Hanen's own page says "young autistic children" (read) — [Hanen](https://www.hanen.org/Programs/For-Parents/More-Than-Words.aspx)
- A trial of 62 toddlers (Carter 2011) found no major immediate gains in child communication. Parent responsiveness rose and then fell back by 9 months, and children with lower object interest gained more. The reviewer called gains "quite limited" (read) — [ASAT](https://asatonline.org/research-treatment/research-synopses/randomized-controlled-trial-of-hanens-more-than-words-in-toddlers-with-early-autism-symptoms/)
- WHO's Caregiver Skills Training works through structured play and home routines, for ages 2-9, delivered by trained non-specialists. A Kenyan pilot with about 100 children saw improvements among parents attending most sessions, and a large trial is under way (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)

**Stimming, sensory needs and echolalia**
- Stimming can be enjoyment, calming, or an expression of feeling. If harmless, there is no reason to stop it. Suppressing it has been a form of enforced masking linked to anxiety. Harmful stims need support, and pain should be checked (read) — [NAS](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/topics/behaviour/stimming/all-audiences)
- NAS practical ideas include ear defenders, movement breaks, fidget toys, a tent or blanket den, and no-pressure food play (read) — [NAS, by an OT](https://www.autism.org.uk/learn/knowledge-hub/professional-practice/sensory-strategies)
- Critics argue "gestalt language processor" is not an established clinical entity and evidence is lacking, while noting that its appeal lies in respect for autistic people (excerpt) — [Hutchins et al. 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23969415241249944)
- An ASHA commentary says accepting echolalia and taking a child-led, play-based approach can be kept whichever view you take of GLP, since those practices sit in many evidence-based interventions (excerpt; 403 when opened) — [ASHA Perspectives 2025](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2025_PERSP-25-00055)
- Reported echolalia estimates range from 25% to 91% (excerpt; attributed to a 2025 review, exact paper unconfirmed) — [Frontiers 2025, best match](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1681076/pdf)
- Facilitated communication is not recommended for autistic people (read) — [NAS](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/topics/communication/communication-tools/visual-supports)

**Language**
- In a UK survey of 3,470 people, no single term was universally accepted. "Autistic" was endorsed by many autistic adults and families, and far fewer professionals (abstract) — [Kenny et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361315588200)
- US autistic adults (n=299) overwhelmingly preferred identity-first language, while professionals (n=207) leaned person-first (abstract) — [Taboas et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613221130845)

### Inferences
- [D] Plain-words descriptions. I could not open the programmes' own pages for the first three, so confirm before publishing:
  - **JASPER** (Joint Attention, Symbolic Play, Engagement and Regulation): a trained adult joins the child's play at their level and builds shared attention and play in small steps.
  - **ESDM** (Early Start Denver Model): an early play-and-routine programme mixing developmental and behavioural teaching, by trained therapists with parent coaching.
  - **PACT**: a therapist helps a parent notice and respond to the child's communication, often using feedback on video of their play.
  - **More Than Words**: see the verified description above.
- [D] What is common across them, and fair for PlayNest to teach: notice what the child loves, get alongside, copy, wait, respond to any signal, keep routines. Do not write "JASPER-style" or "ESDM at home".
- [D] Flag that outcome language in these trials ("symptom reduction", the ADOS score) is the medical frame. PlayNest should report "more back-and-forth with parents" rather than "fewer autistic symptoms".
- [D] Avoid: "normal", "appropriate play", "stuck", "quiet hands", "reduce behaviours", "high/low functioning", "recover". Prefer: "their way of playing", "joy", "shared", "comfortable".
- [D] Teach parallel play as a real way of being together: "Playing next to you is playing with you."

## ADHD: movement, active games and short rounds, without brain-training hype

### Takeaway
Short rounds and movement are sensible, low-risk ways to make play fit. They are not treatment, and evidence in young children is thin. For under-6s, the recognised first step is parent training in behaviour management. Computer brain-training has weak real-world evidence, and at least one company paid a US settlement over such claims.

### Cited Findings
- For children under 6, the AAP recommends behaviour therapy before medication, and parent training is most effective at that age. Over 6, it recommends medication with behaviour therapy, with parent training up to age 12 (read; reviewed 21 October 2026) — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/treatment/index.html)
- CHADD advises daily one-on-one time and building on strengths, and advises against using favourite activities as rewards or removing them as punishment (read) — [CHADD](https://chadd.org/for-parents/overview/)
- Brain-training programmes reliably improve the trained tasks, with weaker gains on related tasks and scarce evidence for distant tasks or everyday function (abstract) — [Simons et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100616661983)
- In ADHD, with probably blinded measures, computerised training showed no effect on overall symptoms or hyperactivity/impulsivity, and only small, setting-specific, short-term effects on inattention (abstract) — [Westwood et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-023-02000-7)
- Lumosity's maker agreed to pay $2 million to settle FTC charges of unsupported claims, including about ADHD and school performance (read) — [FTC 2016](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)
- Near transfer occurs regularly and far transfer rarely with Cogmed (excerpt; link is the best match, unconfirmed) — [Aksayli, Sala & Gobet 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/237201483.pdf)
- A single exercise session gave small improvements in adolescents with ADHD (excerpt) — [CHADD news](https://chadd.org/adhd-in-the-news/a-single-exercise-session-improves-adolescent-adhd-symptoms-executive-function/). This does not extend to young children.
- Regular movement breaks are a practice recommendation (read) — [NAS](https://www.autism.org.uk/learn/knowledge-hub/professional-practice/sensory-strategies)

### Inferences
- [D] Review PlayNest's existing guide "Brain-building games for self-control". Its subtitle says games "grow" these skills. Check that wording against Simons and Westwood, and say plainly that a game makes a child better at that game and gives practice and connection, rather than promising school gains.
- [D] Line for the guide: "No game treats ADHD. These games are here because they're fun and they fit."
- [D] Do not make play the reward. CHADD's point means First/Then boards should not take favourite play away.

### Gaps
- No good evidence found on movement breaks or active games in ADHD under age 8.

## Speech and language delay, and AAC

### Takeaway
This has the best evidence for a parent-led approach: parent-implemented language intervention works on average, though effects vary. AAC needs no prerequisites, and nothing I read shows it harms speech. Never tell a parent to hold back a board or device to "make them talk".

### Cited Findings
- A meta-analysis of 18 studies of children 18-60 months found parent-implemented intervention had a significant positive effect on receptive and expressive language, with or without intellectual disability, effect sizes from -0.15 to 0.82 depending on the outcome (abstract) — [Roberts & Kaiser 2011](https://doi.org/10.1044/1058-0360(2011/10-0055))
- It Takes Two to Talk: for children with language delays who are not autistic, led by a Hanen-certified SLP. It teaches following the child's lead, adjusting routines for turn-taking, and using strategies in play and books (read) — [Hanen](https://www.hanen.org/Programs/For-Parents/It-Takes-Two-to-Talk.aspx)
- AAC can help develop language and enhance speech, speech and AAC can be used together, children under 3 can start, and no thinking skills, test scores or milestones are required first (read; reviewed 8 May 2026) — [CHSA/ASHA](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/augmentative-and-alternative-communication-aac)
- There is no evidence AAC prevents speech development, citing Romski & Sevcik 2005 (excerpt; primary article not opened) — [U. Pretoria CAAC](https://www.up.ac.za/centre-for-augmentative-alternative-communication/article/1809503/myths-about-aac); [Delaware project](https://www.cds.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/McCarthyLeadershipProject2025-1.pdf_508-1.pdf)
- UNICEF's "Giving Voice to Every Child" used open-source Cboard with over 20,000 symbols, and over 3,000 children benefited from 2019 to 2023 in five countries (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
- Cboard is free and open source, supports about 46 languages, and works offline only in Chrome on desktop and Android. Evidence on its site is testimonial (read) — [Cboard](https://www.cboard.io/)
- Project Core supplies a Universal Core vocabulary for students with complex communication needs, school-focused, with site content under CC BY-SA 4.0 (read) — [Project Core](https://www.project-core.com/)
- Mulberry Symbols are CC BY-SA 4.0 and allow commercial use with attribution, as long as you do not charge for the symbols themselves. They were designed for adults (read) — [Mulberry](https://mulberrysymbols.org/)

### Inferences
- [D] Message: "Pictures and devices don't replace talking. They give your child a way to be heard while talking grows."
- [D] A small in-game board (more, stop, my turn, help, again) fits PlayNest, with a printable using an openly licensed symbol set, but check that Mulberry's adult design works for young children.
- [D] For the worried parent, always route to a speech-language professional and to PlayNest's milestones guide.

### Gaps

## Sensory processing differences: what is supported and what is contested

### Takeaway
Sensory differences are real and common. "Sensory processing disorder" as a stand-alone diagnosis is not accepted, and evidence for sensory therapies is limited. PlayNest can teach everyday environment changes and preference-following, and should leave brushing, "sensory diets" and "rewiring" claims out.

### Cited Findings
- The AAP (2012) says SPD should generally not be diagnosed because no universally accepted framework exists, research on sensory integration therapy is limited and inconclusive, therapy may be one part of a comprehensive plan, and paediatricians can suggest a trial with clear ways of judging if it works (abstract) — [AAP, Pediatrics 2012](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2012-0876)
- Occupational therapy is a limited resource and should be prioritised around functional goals (excerpt; attributed to the AAP statement, exact page unconfirmed) — [Disability Scoop, best match](https://www.disabilityscoop.com/2012/05/29/merits-sensory-disorder/15738/)
- SPD is not recognised by psychiatrists as a diagnosis, though they accept that some children over- or under-respond. Claims that therapy "rewires" the brain are disputed, and a named expert worries it may delay autism diagnosis (read) — [Child Mind Institute](https://childmind.org/article/treating-sensory-processing-issues/)
- Sensory issues are cited as a feature of autism and also appear with ADHD, OCD or no diagnosis (read) — [Child Mind Institute](https://childmind.org/article/treating-sensory-processing-issues/)
- Commentators criticise the AAP statement as underweighting positive studies, and autistic writers object to treating sensory issues as behaviour (excerpt) — [Your Therapy Source](https://www.yourtherapysource.com/blog1/2012/05/29/pediatrics-policy-on-sensory-integration-therapies/); [Thinking Person's Guide to Autism](https://thinkingautismguide.com/2012/06/sensory-issues-vs-behaviors-on-recent.html)
- WHO/UNICEF name quiet rooms, calming spaces and sensory areas as ways to make community places welcoming (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)

### Inferences
- [D] Three zones for the writer. **Green** (teach): warn, turn down, offer choices, respect "no", offer a den. **Amber** (mention, and credit a therapist): deep pressure, heavy work, chewing, weighted items. **Red** (leave out): brushing protocols, "sensory diet" prescriptions, "rewiring" and cure language.
- [D] Note that NHS and NAS practical sheets include amber items such as firm pressure on the shoulders and weighted lap pads. PlayNest should not repeat these as general advice. Touch should be wanted by the child.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm whether the AAP statement has been reaffirmed or replaced. Weighted-item safety, particularly for under-3s and sleep, was not verified, so keep them out. The newer Ayres trials and reviews were not read.

## Sensory-friendly cues: warnings, visual timers, First/Then boards, quiet spaces

### Takeaway
Predictability is the common thread: warn, show, and give a way out. Practical guidance is consistent, and First/Then has an evidence link through reinforcement in autism, but I found no direct evidence for visual timers. Frame First/Then as "what's next", not as earning.

### Cited Findings
- **Warn:** tell the child about known loud sounds ahead of time, explain the source, and reassure them it won't hurt; check new places first; go gradually; find quieter times (read) — [NHS occupational therapy sheet](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf)
- **Reduce:** use ear defenders or headphones, but make sure they reduce rather than block sound because of safety, and do not wear them constantly. Let the child control the volume (read) — [NHS sheet](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf)
- **Safety:** a child who has trouble filtering sound may also miss alarms and sirens, so practise what to do (read) — [NHS sheet](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf)
- **Say no:** teach phrases such as "some of the sounds hurt my ears, can we play a different one?" (read) — [NHS sheet](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf)
- **Show:** visual schedules before noisy places, visual timers for how long, a visual scale for distress, and a help card to ask to leave, all written for school but transferable (read) — [NHS sheet](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf). "How many sleeps" countdowns (read) — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
- **First/Then:** a two-step board using photos, drawings or text, with short practice runs, kept in sight, with the "then" given immediately after. It is tied to reinforcement as an evidence-based practice in the 2020 NCAEP review (read) — [Vanderbilt TRIAD](https://vkc.vumc.org/assets/files/tipsheets/First_Then_Caregiver.pdf)
- Keep "first" short and offer small choices (excerpt) — [Texas A&M NEST](https://nest.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Family-Version_Using-First–Then-Boards-at-Home.pdf). A child may stop trusting the board if the "then" isn't delivered (excerpt; low-weight, exact page unconfirmed) — [First-Then guide, best match](https://autismclassroomresources.com/visual-schedule-series-first-then/)
- **Quiet space:** a pop-up tent, a blanket over a table, or a space under a cabin bed (read) — [NAS](https://www.autism.org.uk/learn/knowledge-hub/professional-practice/sensory-strategies). Cozy spots and jump-in points (read) — [Me2 copy](https://www.concordnh.gov/DocumentCenter/View/24274/7-Principles-of-Inclusive-Playground-Design)

### Inferences
- [D] First/Then, with this wording: "First shoes, then park." Use it for what's next, not as a deal. Never put comfort items, food or a favourite person in "then". The "then" must always happen.
- [D] Sample copy:
  - Warning: "Big noise coming. Want to hold the lid? Or cover your ears? Ready... 3, 2, 1."
  - Timer: "When the sand runs out, we finish this game."
  - Den: "This spot is always allowed. No questions, no time limit. Come back when you like."
  - Exit: "You can stop any time. Just show me the stop card."

### Gaps
- No direct trial evidence for visual timers or quiet spaces was found. NCAEP's report URL returned 404, so its findings are known only through the Vanderbilt sheet.

## Premature babies and NICU graduates: corrected age, kangaroo care, low-stimulation cues, tummy-time adaptations

### Takeaway
Kangaroo care has strong WHO backing. Cue-reading is the core skill for play: go, slow, or call for help. PlayNest already has a corrected-age guide, so add cues and gentle play and do not repeat it. Any colour or breathing change is a medical matter, not a play issue.

### Cited Findings
- WHO (November 2022) advises skin-to-skin contact starting immediately after birth, with no initial incubator period, for as many hours as possible. Earlier guidance separated babies for about 3-7 days. Research shows it saves many more lives, reduces infections and hypothermia, and improves feeding (read) — [WHO news](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-11-2022-who-advises-immediate-skin-to-skin-care-for-survival-of-small-and-preterm-babies)
- It applies to babies born before 37 weeks or under 2.5 kg, except those needing breathing support or in shock (excerpt) — [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2022/11/1130602); [PAHO](https://www.paho.org/en/news/15-11-2022-who-advises-immediate-skin-skin-care-survival-small-and-preterm-babies)
- WHO's 2023 position paper calls for kangaroo mother care for all preterm or low-birthweight babies (read) — [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240072657). The clinical guideline is 2022 (read) — [WHO 2022](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240058262)
- Cues from a NICU family charity (read) — [Hand to Hold](https://handtohold.org/nicu-family-support/nicu-basics/understanding-your-babys-cues/):
  - **Go:** calm alert gaze, steady breathing, good colour, hands together, hand to mouth, turning to sounds.
  - **Slow or stop:** looking away, yawning, startling, hand over face or behind ear. Reduce light, noise and strong smells, and let the baby rest.
  - **Caution:** breathing or heart-rate change, dusky, pale or bright-red colour, gagging or vomiting. Alert the care team.
- Stress signs also include hiccups, sneezing, frowning and squirming (excerpt) — [Children's Minnesota](https://www.childrensmn.org/educationmaterials/childrensmn/article/15276/infant-behavior-cues/). Dimming lights, lowering noise, swaddling and skin-to-skin may help (excerpt) — [BIDMC](https://www.bidmc.org/-/media/files/beth-israel-org/centers-and-departments/neonatology/developmentalcarelatepreterminfant.pdf)
- WHO/UNICEF define delay against "actual or adjusted age" (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
- Pathways.org has NICU and preemie pages and a tummy time tool (read, landing page) — [Pathways.org](https://pathways.org/)

### Inferences
- [D] "One thing at a time": eyes or voice or touch, then pause. Stop at the first "slow" cue and try again later. A baby's "no" isn't a failure.
- [D] Tummy time for this group: chest-to-chest first, then short tries, following the follow-up team's advice. Link to the existing tummy-time guide.
- [D] Add a note that "kangaroo mother care" is the WHO term. The WHO news page names a primary caregiver, usually the mother, and does not address fathers or other family, so do not make claims about them from this source.

### Gaps
- No verified source on tummy time specifically for NICU graduates, on feeding tubes or monitors at home, or on the "8 to 24 hours" start window. Pathways.org's inner pages were not read.

## Chronic illness and hospital stays: Child Life style play, medical play, procedure preparation

### Takeaway
Honest, simple preparation plus pretend play with a doll or kit is common ground across children's hospitals. Exact timing advice varies. Parents can do the everyday version at home, but Child Life specialists are trained professionals, and PlayNest should say "ask if your hospital has one".

### Cited Findings
- Age-by-age (read) — [CHOP](https://www.chop.edu/health-resources/preparing-hospitalization-and-medical-procedures):
  - Infant: favourite comfort object, calm parent, routines.
  - Toddler: talk 1-2 days ahead, play kit and dolls.
  - Preschool: talk 3-4 days ahead, reassure them they did not cause it, picture books.
  - School age: 1-2 weeks ahead, open answers, pack their own bag.
  - All: be honest, and say you'll find out if you don't know.
- Avoid "put to sleep", which may suggest a pet's fate. Check what the child understood, address any belief that surgery is a punishment, use belly breathing or bubbles, and for autistic or ADHD children list triggers and what calms them (read) — [Starship](https://www.starship.org.nz/preparing-your-child)
- **Conflict on timing:** Starship suggests 3-5 days for preschoolers (read) — [Starship](https://www.starship.org.nz/preparing-your-child). CHOP suggests 3-4 days (read). An unidentified hospital guide in my results suggests much later: toddlers at the time of the procedure, preschoolers a few hours before (excerpt).
- AAP's 2021 statement says child life specialists use therapeutic play, expressive modalities and psychological preparation, and that preparation can reduce sedation need in procedures such as MRI. It recommends services be part of integrated, family-centred care (excerpt) — [AAP 2021, doi](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2020-040261); [RWJBarnabas](https://www.rwjbh.org/blog/2021/january/barbara-romito-child-life-director-is-lead-autho/)
- The earlier 2014 statement is linked with less distress, better coping and shorter stays, with no effect sizes in this summary (read, secondary) — [2 Minute Medicine](https://www.2minutemedicine.com/aap-recommends-child-life-services)
- The literature on medical play versus psychological preparation is still being examined (excerpt) — [Association of Child Life Professionals](https://education.childlife.org/products/foundational-interventions-of-child-life-practice-using-evidence-assessment-to-differentiate-the-tools)
- Children with developmental disabilities experience more preventable harm and poorer care in hospital (read) — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-09-2023-new-reports-highlights-neglected-health-needs-of-children-with-developmental-disabilities)

### Inferences
- [D] Medical play rules: the child is the doctor, teddy is the patient, tell the truth about what hurts, let the child stop. Never surprise a child with a real procedure during "play".
- [D] Sana, sana, colita de rana is already in PlayNest and fits as a comfort rhyme.
- [D] Add a "hospital passport" idea: a short list of what calms your child, for staff.

### Gaps
- Starlight was not reached. AAP's full text was blocked. No verified guidance on immune-suppressed play, infection control or hygiene, so route those to the care team. Chronic illness at home (fatigue, flare days) has no direct source beyond RNIB's note on choosing familiar, undemanding activities when a child is tired or unwell.

## WHO/UNICEF frameworks, low-resource play, crisis and displacement, small spaces and no toys

### Takeaway
WHO/UNICEF give PlayNest its strongest policy framing: nurturing care is for every child, caregivers are the experts and need support themselves, and household materials are fine. Children with disabilities receive less stimulation and fewer early-learning chances. ### Cited Findings

- A search excerpt dates a thematic briefs series to 2023, so the date in the brief you gave me may come from there (excerpt) — [Child Health Task Force](https://www.childhealthtaskforce.org/resources/report/2023/nurturing-care-thematic-briefs)
- Compared with other children, children with disabilities are 25% less likely to receive early stimulation and responsive care, 16% less likely to be read to, and only about one in five attends early education. They are 32% more likely to experience severe physical punishment at home (read, from UNICEF 2021 data in the brief) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
- 58.9 million children under 5 had a condition contributing to developmental disability in 2019, and at least 43% of under-5s are at risk of delay because of inequalities (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)

- Caregivers are often the experts on their child and also need support. Caregiver mental health affects interactions (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
- Global report (15 September 2023): 317 million children and young people were affected by conditions contributing to developmental disability in 2019, with stigma, delayed diagnosis and poorer care (read) — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-09-2023-new-reports-highlights-neglected-health-needs-of-children-with-developmental-disabilities). NYU's evaluation included a phone-based Reach Up and Learn trial, and reports significant child-development impact from a short remote programme in Lebanon (read; no effect sizes on the page) — [NYU Global TIES](https://globaltiesforchildren.nyu.edu/ahlan-simsim)

Hesperian's guide has "Toys You Can Make" (read, contents only) — [Hesperian](https://en.hesperian.org/hhg/Helping_Children_Who_Are_Blind)

## Family settings: every shape of family

### Takeaway
Across family types, the message is the same: the relationship matters more than the family's form, and short predictable rituals carry weight. Coverage is uneven. Raising Children was blocked, and single parents, heritage-language families, and a parent's serious illness have little direct sourcing here.

### Cited Findings
**Fathers**
- A review of 78 studies found fathers tend toward more physical play, and that fathers' early play may contribute to social, emotional and cognitive outcomes. Evidence is largely correlational and from higher-income families, and a review author stresses that children living only with their mother need not be at a disadvantage (excerpt) — [Nursery World](https://www.nurseryworld.co.uk/articles/rough-and-tumble-play-with-fathers-helps-children-to-learn-how-to-control-their-feelings)
- Quality of interaction appears to matter in rough-and-tumble play (excerpt) — [PMC9315721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9315721/). Frequent play predicted more aggression only where fathers were less dominant (excerpt; I am unsure which result this came from, so check it) — [OJP](https://ojp.gov/index%2ephp/node/1232701)

## Respectful language: person-first, identity-first, and writing without pity or inspiration tropes

### Takeaway
Style guides no longer impose one default: ask, and follow the person. Where preferences are documented, autistic adults and the Deaf community lean identity-first, and UK disabled people's organisations use "disabled people". For a parent audience, the simplest fix is to describe what the child does and needs and to use labels only in guides.

### Cited Findings
- NCDJ's style guide (last updated summer 2021) offers no default on person-first and urges asking. It reports identity-first as strongly preferred among Deaf and autistic people, says to avoid "suffers from" and "afflicted with", says "differently abled" is not appropriate, and uses "Deaf" for community and "deaf" for the condition. It states no reuse licence (read) — [NCDJ](https://cronkite.asu.edu/ncdj/disability-language-style-guide)
- UK government guidance: use "disabled people", "wheelchair user", "non-disabled" and "people with sight loss". Avoid "the disabled", "wheelchair-bound", "suffers from", "able-bodied", "the blind", "fits". Prefer active wording and let people self-identify. OGL v3.0 (read) — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/inclusive-communication/inclusive-language-words-to-use-and-avoid-when-writing-about-disability)
- ADA National Network: mention disability only if relevant, ask preferences, avoid "special", "challenged", "differently-abled", "non-verbal" and "normal" or "healthy" as contrasts. Avoid "superhuman" framing and tearjerkers (read) — [ADA NN](https://adata.org/factsheet/ADANN-writing)
- Stella Young named "inspiration porn": treating disabled people as inspiring simply for living everyday lives (read, page description only) — [TED 2014](https://www.ted.com/talks/stella_young_i_m_not_your_inspiration_thank_you_very_much/transcript)
- WHO/UNICEF use person-first and note that terms vary across cultures and groups (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)

**What disabled adults themselves are documented to prefer**
| Group | Documented preference | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Autistic adults | "autistic" | Surveys: [Taboas 2023](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613221130845), [Kenny 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361315588200) (no single term universal). Self-report data. |
| Deaf community | "Deaf"/"deaf", "hard of hearing" | [NCDJ](https://cronkite.asu.edu/ncdj/disability-language-style-guide). A style guide, not a survey. |
| UK disabled people | "disabled people" (social model) | [Scope](https://www.scope.org.uk/social-model-of-disability/) says individuals decide; [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/inclusive-communication/inclusive-language-words-to-use-and-avoid-when-writing-about-disability) is official guidance, not a preference survey. |
| People with Down's syndrome | "people who have Down's syndrome" | [DSA](https://www.downs-syndrome.org.uk/) is organisation usage; I did not find self-advocates' views. |
| Blind people | Unverified | NFB returned 403. |

### Inferences
- [D] House style for PlayNest:
  - Activity and toolkit copy: no labels, only needs ("if the sound is too much").
  - Guides: "autistic children", "deaf children", "disabled children" (UK) or "children with disabilities" (US), "children with Down's syndrome", "children who use AAC". Add one line saying families may use other words.
  - Parents often use their own words first. Do not correct them.
- [D] "Non-verbal" is on ADA NN's avoid list, so use "doesn't use speech" or "uses AAC". "Nonspeaking" has community use, but I did not verify it here.
- [D] Do/don't pairs:
  - "Your child uses a wheelchair" not "wheelchair-bound".
  - "Your child's way of playing" not "inappropriate play".
  - "Different" not "special".
  - "Has CP" not "suffers from CP".
  - "Plays differently" not "despite her disability".
  - "Non-disabled siblings" not "normal siblings".
  - "Hard days are allowed" not "you're so strong, you're a hero".
  - "This is a play idea" not "this will improve symptoms".
  - No "angel", "brave", "inspiring", "special needs mom".
  - Show ordinary fun, not triumph over tragedy.
- [D] Recruit paid reviewers: disabled adults and parents should read the guides before launch. Nothing here substitutes for that.

## The compact toolkit: "Can't see it? Can't hear it? Can't reach it? Too much? Too fast?", with about 60 ready-to-use lines

### Takeaway
No trial tests these rules as a toolkit. They are distilled from specialist organisations, parents and deaf adults, with evidence for a few elements only. Present them as "things families try", and never as therapy.

### Cited Findings
Evidence behind the levers is summarised below. Sources are in the need-specific sections above.

| Lever | Can't see it | Can't hear it | Can't reach it | Too much | Too fast | Strength of basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Size** | Bigger, bolder | Big gestures | Bigger handles, larger parts | Fewer things out | Fewer pieces | Guidance |
| **Texture** | Mark pieces by feel | Feel the beat | Grippy surfaces | Offer a no-touch way | Keep what's familiar | Parent/guidance |
| **Sound** | Add sound to find it | Swap sound for light or wave | Sound confirms success | Warn, lower, let them control | Slow song | Guidance |
| **Position** | Better side, good light | Face to face, lit face | Floor, lap, side-lying, tray | Back to a wall, den near | Stay seated | Guidance; seating evidence limited |
| **Time** | Extra time to explore | Pause for looking | Time to move | Short and leave early | Shorter rounds, timer, wait | Guidance; "wait" appears in NDCS advice |
| **Rules** | Say the rule aloud | Show the rule first | Any body part counts | Watching counts, stop any time | Drop a rule | Practice |
| **Choices** | Offer objects to feel | Show two options | Pick the job | Choose what to turn down | Two choices, not ten | Practice |
| **Cue type** | Voice, touch, sound | Light, touch, sign, picture | Eye-point, nod | Warning count, picture card | First/Then, timer | First/Then: reinforcement link via [Vanderbilt](https://vkc.vumc.org/assets/files/tipsheets/First_Then_Caregiver.pdf). Timers: no direct evidence found |

### Inferences
All lines below are draft copy written by me [D]. The link shows the basis. **G** means specialist guidance, **P** means parent or deaf-adult experience, **E** means a related research base, and **X** means my extrapolation with no direct source.

**Can't see it**
1. Say what you're doing as you do it: "Ball rolling to your left hand." G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
2. Put it on a plain cloth so it stands out. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
3. Play near a window or good lamp. If glare bothers them, turn away from it. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
4. Offer it on the side they see best. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
5. Seal something noisy inside so it can be found by ear. G/X — [WHO/UNICEF](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
6. Keep pieces in a tray so nothing rolls away. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
7. If it rolls off, go after it together rather than fetching it. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
8. Swap the picture for the real thing: a real spoon, leaf or orange. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
9. Stick felt, foil or card on each piece so they can tell them apart by feel. P — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
10. Keep toys in the same place each time. P — [WonderBaby](https://www.wonderbaby.org/articles/toy-guide-for-blind-children)
11. Hands, feet and mouth are all good ways to explore, if the object is safe for mouths. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)

**Can't hear it**
12. Get their attention first: a gentle touch, then wait until they look. G — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/communicating-your-deaf-baby-or-toddler)
13. Sit face to face, with the light on your face rather than behind you. G — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/creating-good-communication-environment)
14. Turn off the TV or music while you play. G — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/creating-good-communication-environment)
15. Swap "listen for" with "look for": a raised hand or torch means go. X, from — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
16. Show the rule before you start, because they can't watch the game and you at once. X, from — [Gallaudet](https://clerccenter.gallaudet.edu/national-resources/micro-sites/setting-language-in-motion/eng/Documents/Clerc%20Microsite/Assets/Module6-TipsLanguageModeling.pdf)
17. Leave a gap in the song and let them fill it with a sign, sound or clap. G — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/communicating-your-deaf-baby-or-toddler)
18. Move your sign or gesture to where they're already looking. G — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/communicating-your-deaf-baby-or-toddler)
19. Use a mirror so they can see your face in the car seat or buggy. G/P — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/communicating-your-deaf-baby-or-toddler); [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
20. Let them feel it: a hand on the drum or on the floor. X, from — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
21. At night or outdoors, keep a lamp on so hands and faces stay visible. P — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
22. Hearing devices off for now? Go fully visual for this game. X, from — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)

**Can't reach it**
23. Bring the game to where they're comfy: floor, lap, tray or bed. G — [Me2 copy](https://www.concordnh.gov/DocumentCenter/View/24274/7-Principles-of-Inclusive-Playground-Design)
24. Try lying on one side, so toys are in view and hands can meet. Check with their therapist. G (excerpt) — [EACH](https://www.each.org.uk/app/uploads/2021/10/physical-positioning-ideas-for-play.pdf)
25. Change position now and then. There's no single right one. G — [Hesperian](https://en.hesperian.org/hhg/Disabled_Village_Children:How_Can_We_Help%3F)
26. Hang a toy within reach on a short loop, and stay right there. G/X — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
27. Use a tray or box with a lip so things stay close. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
28. Make handles fatter with tape or foam, or add a handle to a box. P — [WonderBaby](https://www.wonderbaby.org/articles/toy-guide-for-blind-children)
29. Prop it up so it stays upright for someone sitting or lying down. P — [WonderBaby](https://www.wonderbaby.org/articles/toy-guide-for-blind-children)
30. Hand, foot, head, elbow or eyes: any move counts as a turn. X, from — [CHSA/ASHA](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/augmentative-and-alternative-communication-aac)
31. Swap the ball for a scarf or a big light beach ball. X
32. Share the jobs: one runs, one calls "go". Then swap. G/X — [Me2 copy](https://www.concordnh.gov/DocumentCenter/View/24274/7-Principles-of-Inclusive-Playground-Design)

**Too much**
33. Turn one thing down first: sound, light, people or clutter. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
34. Warn before it happens: "Big bang in 3, 2, 1." G — [NHS OT](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf)
35. Let them hold the noisy thing or control the volume. G — [NHS OT](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf)
36. Have ear defenders nearby for loud moments. They should lower sound, not block it. G — [NHS OT](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf)
37. Make a den: a blanket over a table. Going there is always allowed. G — [NAS](https://www.autism.org.uk/learn/knowledge-hub/professional-practice/sensory-strategies)
38. Watching counts as joining in. G — [Me2 copy](https://www.concordnh.gov/DocumentCenter/View/24274/7-Principles-of-Inclusive-Playground-Design)
39. Don't like the feel? Try a spoon, or put the messy stuff in a sealed bag. X
40. They can stop whenever they like, and come back whenever they like. X, from — [BTHA tip on not pressuring](https://southerntrust.hscni.net/download/165/children-with-disabilities/2887/toys-and-play-for-young-children-with-downs-syndrome.pdf)
41. Go at a quiet time, or at the start or end of a group. G — [RNIB](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/APDF-RE190111_Lets_Play_2019-v06.pdf)
42. Flapping, rocking or humming is fine if no one's getting hurt. Join in if they like. G — [NAS](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/topics/behaviour/stimming/all-audiences)
43. Baby looking away, yawning or startling? Pause and let them rest. G — [Hand to Hold](https://handtohold.org/nicu-family-support/nicu-basics/understanding-your-babys-cues/)

**Too fast**
44. Do one round and stop while it's still fun. X
45. Count to ten in your head before you help or repeat. G — [NHS OT](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf); [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/communicating-your-deaf-baby-or-toddler)
46. Use a sand timer so the end isn't a surprise. G (no direct evidence) — [NHS OT](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/rv2j2bwr/sensitive-to-noise-advice-sheet.pdf)
47. Show "First ... then ..." in two pictures, and always do the "then". G/E — [Vanderbilt](https://vkc.vumc.org/assets/files/tipsheets/First_Then_Caregiver.pdf)
48. Give a heads-up: "Two more, then bath." X
49. Fit movement between rounds: ten jumps, or a lap of the room. G (limited evidence) — [NAS](https://www.autism.org.uk/learn/knowledge-hub/professional-practice/sensory-strategies)
50. Play the same game again, as often as they ask. Repeating is learning. G — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)

**Too tricky**
51. Drop a rule and keep the fun: no scoring, no one's out. X
52. Offer two choices rather than an open question: "Ball or bubbles?" G (excerpt) — [Texas A&M NEST](https://nest.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Family-Version_Using-First–Then-Boards-at-Home.pdf)
53. Show it once, then wait for them to copy. G — [BTHA](https://southerntrust.hscni.net/download/165/children-with-disabilities/2887/toys-and-play-for-young-children-with-downs-syndrome.pdf)
54. Start from what they already love: trains, spinning, one song. G — [Hanen](https://www.hanen.org/Programs/For-Parents/More-Than-Words.aspx)
55. Sit beside them and copy what they do, without asking them to join you. G/X — [NDCS](https://www.ndcs.org.uk/advice-and-support/language-and-communication/communicating-your-deaf-baby-or-toddler)
56. Do one step together and let the grown-up do the rest. P — [Hands & Voices](https://handsandvoices.org/fl3/fl3-docs/maximize-visual-access-tips.pdf)
57. Use bigger pieces, and fewer of them. G — [BTHA](https://southerntrust.hscni.net/download/165/children-with-disabilities/2887/toys-and-play-for-young-children-with-downs-syndrome.pdf)

**Can't say it yet**
58. Any way of saying it counts: a look, point, sound, sign, picture or device. G — [CHSA/ASHA](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/augmentative-and-alternative-communication-aac)
59. Keep a small picture board in the game: more, stop, my turn, help. G/X — [CHSA/ASHA](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/augmentative-and-alternative-communication-aac)

## How can the app offer this without labelling a child?

### Takeaway
Ask about the barrier, not the child. Offer the barrier chips per game and per session, make them equally usable for a parent's own needs, store nothing by default, and never infer. Named guides remain open for browsing, because families do search by name and identity matters to many.

### Cited Findings
- Mismatch framing: "Everyone has abilities and limits"; solve for one and extend to many (read) — [Microsoft](https://inclusive.microsoft.design/)
- Data concerning health is a special category under GDPR Article 9, with processing prohibited unless an exception such as explicit consent applies (read) — [GDPR Art. 9 text](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/)
- WHO/UNICEF name apps as an information channel, and ask for tailored support for caregivers with disabilities, mental health conditions or low literacy (read) — [WHO/UNICEF brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09617)
- Pathways.org's stated stance (information, not therapy or diagnosis) is a market reference for disclaimers (read) — [Pathways.org](https://pathways.org/)
- W3C recommends personalisation, simplification, symbols, and no reliance on memory (read) — [W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- Vroom's approach: peer voice, no judgement, everyday moments (read) — [IDEO DesignKit](https://www.designkit.org/case-studies/2.html)
- Bebbo works online and offline (read) — [Bebbo](https://www.bebbo.app/)
- Parents may blame themselves when an approach said to have strong evidence fails (read) — [UNC Health](https://news.unchealthcare.org/2023/11/researchers-reveal-low-quality-studies-of-autism-early-interventions-dominate-the-field)

### Inferences
**Design rules [D]**
1. A "Make this game work for us" button on every activity, separate from easier/harder.
2. The chips name barriers, and can refer to a child, a grown-up or the room. A parent with low vision or an arm in a cast uses the same chips. No one has to disclose anything.
3. Session-only by default, with nothing added to the child's profile. An optional, plainly explained "keep these tweaks" stays on the device. Have a lawyer review this, since a saved tweak or a guide history could reveal health information, and PlayNest handles children's data.
4. Never infer from taps, never target ads or notifications on this, and never send "tips for your autistic child".
5. No screening quiz, no checklist, no progress score, no "behind". The only route for worry is the milestones guide and "ask your doctor".
6. Age bands can be switched off ("show all ages"), because RNIB says the parent is the best judge regardless of age labels. This also covers corrected age.
7. Each line has audio and an icon, and is one instruction long. Works offline.
8. Parents' tips should come only with moderation and clear labelling, since peer advice can carry unproven treatments.
9. Named guides are browsable by everyone with no sign-in or saved state.

**Implementation sketch [D]**
- Tag each activity with which barriers matter (for example `cue: sound`, `needs: running`, `parts: small`) and generate 3 general lines from the 67, plus any custom line a writer adds per game.
- Tiny-space, no-toys, one-adult and far-apart are a second chip row, drawing on the family lines.

**Sample copy [D]**
- Button: **Make this game work for us**
- Sheet title: **What's in the way today?**
- Sub-line: "Pick anything. It could be about a child, a grown-up or the room. You don't need to tell us why."
- Chips: Can't see it · Can't hear it · Can't reach it · Too much · Too fast · Too tricky · Can't say it yet · Gentle day
- Second row: No toys here · Tiny space · One grown-up, more kids · Playing from far away
- Results title: **Three things to try**
- Example, Freeze Dance + "Can't hear it": "Use a raised hand or a torch for stop. Show the signal before you start. Let them be the one who says stop next."
- Footer: "Not quite right? Change anything. You know your child best."
- Medical note: "These are play ideas, not therapy. If a doctor or therapist has given you positions or limits, those come first."
- Privacy note: "This isn't saved or added to your child's profile."
- Save prompt: "Keep these for next time? They'll stay on this phone only."
- Worry link: "Noticed something you'd like to ask about? See **Understanding milestones**. A doctor or health visitor can help too."
- Guide-shelf heading: **For your family**. Sub-line: "Read whatever's useful. Nothing here gets saved to your child."
- Words to avoid in UI: diagnosis, disorder, symptoms, special, normal, improve, catch up, "for children with [label]" on activity cards.

### Gaps
- Microsoft's persona-spectrum guidebook exceeded the fetch limit, so permanent/temporary/situational examples are unverified. ICO's guidance returned 403. UK GDPR, the Children's Code and US rules (COPPA) need legal review. No user research with disabled parents has been seen. Test the chip wording with them.
