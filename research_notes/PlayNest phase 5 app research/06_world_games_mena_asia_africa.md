**Confidence grades.**
- **H:** UNESCO, peer-reviewed or academic work, or a national institution.
- **M:** cited encyclopedia section, research NGO, magazine or newspaper, or several sources agreeing.
- **L:** one stub or unsourced wiki page, or a single blog. Needs a cultural reviewer before publishing.

## Q1. What new games can PlayNest add, across ages 0-12, especially babies and toddlers?

### Takeaway
83 new entries are recorded below, 27 of them starting under 4 years, so the 45-game target is met in number. Sourcing is the weak point: only 4 entries are grade H (one of those is H/M), 35 are M and 44 are L. The L entries need a human cultural check, so a cautious writer has about 39 entries ready to draft with. The strongest baby finds are the Saudi palm game *Battikh Battikh*, the Persian swing rhyme *Tab Tab Abbasi*, the Tamil *Pillaitamil* month-by-month baby stages, the Manipuri bath song *Ting Ting Chaoro*, and the Bengali knee rhyme *Agdum Bagdum*.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Babies and young toddlers (start under 24 months)

**1. Battikh Battikh** — بطيخ بطيخ · *baṭṭīkh baṭṭīkh* · "watermelon, watermelon"
- **Origin and confidence:** Saudi Arabia, in a list of Saudi folk games. Grade **L**: one uncited section. May be shared across the Gulf (not verified).
- **Ages etc.:** 6-36 m (sug.). Parent and child, no materials, anywhere.
- **Rules:** 1) Open the child's palm and stroke it, repeating the chant. 2) Fold the fingers in one at a time starting with the little finger, naming each. 3) Fold your own hand except index and middle, and "walk" them slowly up from the palm like a person or creature searching for lost sheep. 4) Arrive at the fist, act amazed: "this is their pen". 5) Carry on walking to the armpit and tickle until the child is full of laughter.
- **Chant (traditional):** «بطيخ... بطيخ... ذنيب الذيخ» ("watermelon, watermelon, little hyena's tail"). Fingers: هذي خنيصر / هذي بنيصر / وهذي عيداء الطويلة / وهذي عوجاء طويلة / وهذي ملحسة القدحان / وهذي مقصعة القمل والصيبان ("little pinkie / little ring finger / this tall one / this tall crooked one / this one licks the bowls / this one cracks lice and nits"). Walking: «يا من عيّن الطليان؟» ("who has seen the lambs?"). Fist: «هاه... هذا مراحهن ومرباضهن» ("ah, this is their pen and resting place").
- **Skills:** language, senses, feelings, thinking (anticipation).
- **Safety:** tickle only while the child is enjoying it, and stop on any sign of protest.
- **Sensitivity:** the source says "father and son". Present it for any caregiver and any child. Keep the funny lice line, since it is the joke, or offer it as optional.
- **Source:** [ar.wikipedia, Saudi folk games](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/ألعاب_شعبية_سعودية)

**2. Tab Tab Abbasi** — تاب تاب عباسی · *tāb tāb 'Abbāsi* · "swing, swing, Abbasi"
- **Origin and confidence:** Iran, Persian-speaking areas. Author and date unknown, and verses vary by region. Grade **L**: the page is flagged unsourced, though the rhyme is widely attested there.
- **Ages etc.:** 6-48 m (sug.). Caregiver and child. Swing, or adult arms (Adapt). In or outdoors.
- **Rules:** sung to a child on a swing, and sometimes as a lullaby. 1) Swing on the beat. 2) On the last line, swing the child into an adult's arms (Adapt).
- **Chant (folk, author unknown):** تاب تاب عباسی / خدا منو نندازی / اگه منو بندازی / بغل مامان بندازی ("Swing, swing, Abbasi / God, don't drop me / if you drop me / drop me in Mum's arms"). Any loved person's name can replace Mum.
- **Skills:** movement, feelings, language.
- **Safety:** before sitting unaided, use only a low cradle-hold in adult arms. Never swing by the arms or wrists. A swing is not a sleep space ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/A-Parents-Guide-to-Safe-Sleep.aspx)).
- **Sensitivity:** mentions God lightly. Keep the wording.
- **Source:** [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/تاب_تاب_عباسی)

**3. Dali Moosheh / Ce-e** — دالی موشه (Persian) · Ce-e (Turkish) · peekaboo words
- **Origin and confidence:** shared worldwide, so no single origin. Fernald and O'Neill's 17-culture study found these games not strictly universal, but with a shared structure: get attention, hide, then a voiced reappearance. Cultures in that study include Hindi, Persian, Tamil, Xhosa and Malay. Grade **M** via the Turkish page's citation.
- **Ages:** the Persian page says learned from about 9 months (src). The Turkish page describes the classic form for 11 months and under (src).
- **Rules:** get attention, hide your face, reveal with the word. No materials.
- **Skills:** thinking, feelings.
- **App note:** if a peekaboo activity exists, add these as "how families say it" rather than a new game.
- **Sources:** [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/دالی_موشه); [tr.wikipedia](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ce-e_oyunu)

**4. Sengeerai** — செங்கீரை · *sengīrai* · from *kīr* "speech" (one reading)
- **Origin and confidence:** Tamil, the 5th-month stage of *Pillaitamil*, a literary genre in ten baby stages from 3 to 21 months. Grade **M** for the genre. It is a poetic ideal, not a measured milestone.
- **Ages:** about 5 months (src).
- **Rules:** answer the baby's babble as if it were sweet speech. A second reading: sing "āduga sengīrai" as the baby lifts one leg, bends the other and rises on its hands to sway (Adapt: floor play on a mat).
- **Skills:** language, movement, feelings.
- **Sensitivity:** in the literary genre the "baby" is often a deity or revered person. The genre is devotional, so present the idea with respect.
- **Source:** [ta.wikipedia, Pillaitamil](https://ta.wikipedia.org/wiki/பிள்ளைத்தமிழ்)

**5. Sappani** — சப்பாணி · *cappāṇi* · "clap of the hands" stage
- **Origin and confidence:** Tamil *Pillaitamil*, 9th-month stage. Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** about 9 months, when the baby sits and claps for joy (src).
- **Rules:** clap and call the baby, invite the baby to clap back, and celebrate each small "sap" sound.
- **Skills:** hands, feelings, language.
- **Source:** [ta.wikipedia](https://ta.wikipedia.org/wiki/பிள்ளைத்தமிழ்)

**6. Muttham and Varugai** — முத்தம் / வருகை · "the kiss" / "come to me"
- **Origin and confidence:** Tamil *Pillaitamil*, 11th and 13th-month stages. Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** about 11 and 13 months (src).
- **Rules:** Muttham: lift the child to your lap, praise them and ask for a kiss. Varugai: as the toddler totters, call "come, come!" with warm delight and welcome them into your arms.
- **Skills:** movement, feelings.
- **Safety:** let the child choose whether to kiss, and offer a nose-rub or high-five instead (Adapt).
- **Source:** [ta.wikipedia](https://ta.wikipedia.org/wiki/பிள்ளைத்தமிழ்)

**7. Ambuli** — அம்புலி · *ambuli* · "the moon"
- **Origin and confidence:** Tamil *Pillaitamil*, 15th-month stage: inviting the moon to come and play. Grade **M**. Calling on the moon is shared more widely: a Bengali source notes Indian lullabies calling on "Chand Mama".
- **Ages:** about 15 months (src). Night, a window or outdoors.
- **Rules:** point out the moon and invite it to play, walking the toddler around to see it (Adapt: make up your own "come and play, moon" line).
- **Skills:** language, senses, imagination.
- **Sources:** [ta.wikipedia](https://ta.wikipedia.org/wiki/பிள்ளைத்தமிழ்); [bn.wikipedia, lullaby](https://bn.wikipedia.org/wiki/ঘুমপাড়ানি_গান)

**8. Ting Ting Chaoro** — ꯇꯤꯡ ꯇꯤꯡ ꯆꯥꯎꯔꯣ · "grow fast, grow fast"
- **Origin and confidence:** Meitei, Manipur (India). Sung by mothers while bathing children. Grade **M**: an encyclopedia page with text and translation.
- **Ages:** 0-24 m (sug.). Bath time. Caregiver and child.
- **Rules:** sing during the bath, naming the family qualities you wish for the child.
- **Chant (traditional; per source):** "Ting ting chaoro / Epa machum taro / Epu machum taro / Thabina karingei kahouro / Numitna wanglingei wanghouro" ("Grow fast, grow fast / let the father's qualities be in you / let the grandfather's qualities be in you / grow like the cucumber / rise as the sun rises").
- **Skills:** language, feelings.
- **Safety:** never leave a child alone in water, even to fetch something (the app has an AAP drowning source).
- **Sensitivity:** also sung by *maibi* priestesses at the Lai Haraoba festival. Credit this and do not turn it into a jokey song. A modern recorded version exists, so use only the traditional words. Families may add a mother's or grandmother's qualities, but flag that as an adaptation.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ting_Ting_Chaoro)

**9. Oxşama / Nazlama** — *oxşama* · "cuddling, stroking" song
- **Origin and confidence:** Azerbaijani oral literature: what a mother sings to fondle a baby on waking. Grade **L**: a short page, though the genre is classified by Azerbaijani folklorists on a longer page.
- **Ages:** 0-18 m (sug.). Waking-up routine. No materials.
- **Rules:** as the baby wakes, sing and stroke them. Lines commonly end "Bu balama qurban".
- **Chant (folk):** "Dağdakı atlar, / Atlar göy otlar. / Göyçək arvadlar, / Bu balama qurban." ("Horses on the mountain / horses in green grass / lovely ladies / all a sacrifice for this baby of mine"). Also: "Balam hər nədir, / Mənə əzizdir" ("whatever my baby is, to me that is dear").
- **Skills:** feelings, language, senses.
- **Sensitivity:** *qurban* (sacrifice) is an idiom of tenderness here. Explain it so it is not read literally.
- **Sources:** [az.wikipedia, Oxşama](https://az.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxşama); [az.wikipedia, Uşaq folkloru](https://az.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uşaq_folkloru)

**10. Hattima Tim Tim** — হাট্টিমা টিম্ টিম্ · nonsense creature rhyme
- **Origin and confidence:** Bengali folk rhyme, author unknown. In print in Jogindranath Sarkar's *Khukumonir Chhara* (13th edition, 1899). Grade **M**. Told to Bengali children from very early on.
- **Ages:** 6 m-3 y (sug.). No materials.
- **Rules:** rhythmic say-aloud. Adapt: make "horns" with two fingers on the head at line 3, and tap the floor for eggs.
- **Chant (folk; appears public domain):** হাট্টিমা টিম্ টিম্ / তারা মাঠে পাড়ে ডিম্ / তাদের খাড়া দুটো শিং / তারা হাট্টিমা টিম্ টিম্ ("Hattima tim tim, they lay eggs in the field; they have two upright horns; they're hattima tim tim").
- **Skills:** language, imagination.
- **Source:** [bn.wikipedia](https://bn.wikipedia.org/wiki/হাট্টিমাটিম_টিম)

**11. Agdum Bagdum** — আগডুম বাগডুম · partly nonsense
- **Origin and confidence:** Bengal. Grade **H**: academic paper (Roy, *Karatoya*, North Bengal University), which also quotes Tagore on it.
- **Ages etc.:** 12 m-8 y (sug.). 1+ players. Sitting, indoors, no materials.
- **Rules:** 1) Sit in a circle. 2) The leader says one word per knee, touching their own knee too. 3) Whoever's knee gets the last word is out for the next round. 4) Continue until all knees are touched. Adapt for a lap baby: one word per tap on the baby's two knees, with a tickle on the last word.
- **Chant (per paper; check spelling against a Bengali edition, as the scan is imperfect):** "Agdum bagdum gharadum sajhe / Dhak mridang jhajhar baje / Bajte bajte challo duli / Duli gelo sei Kamlafulli…" ("Agdum bagdum gharadum dress up / drums and cymbals play / playing on, the palanquin goes / the palanquin went to Kamlafuli…").
- **Skills:** language, self-control, senses.
- **Sensitivity:** Tagore read it as a wedding procession. Asutosh Bhattacharya read the words as Dom army divisions and linked it to marriage by abduction. Don't explain this to young children, but don't call it "just nonsense" in a scholarly note either.
- **Source:** [Roy, Karatoya vol. 9](https://ir.nbu.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/3874/1/Karatoya%20vol%209%20Article%20No%204.pdf)

**12. Machha Machha** — माछा माछा · *māchhā* · "fishy, fishy"
- **Origin and confidence:** Nepal; called "the most basic" of childhood games in a Nepali magazine. Grade **L**: one magazine source.
- **Ages:** 15 m-5 y (sug.). Two players, no materials.
- **Rules:** 1) Wave your forefinger before the child's face saying "machha machha machha". 2) The child grabs at it. 3) Slip the thumb into their grip instead and declare "byagutho!" (frog, भ्यागुतो), since you caught an inedible frog, not a fish.
- **Skills:** hands, self-control, language.
- **Source:** [ECS Nepal](http://ecs.com.np/features/nepali-street-games)

**13. Attal Mattal Tootooleh** — اتل متل توتوله · nonsense opening
- **Origin and confidence:** Iran. The rhyme is described as very old. A "500-year-old" claim appears only in a news headline, so do not repeat it as fact. Grade **M**.
- **Ages etc.:** 18 m-8 y (sug.). Can be solo. Sitting, no materials. An adult leader is recommended by the source.
- **Rules:** 1) Sit side by side, legs stretched out. 2) The leader (*usā*) says the rhyme, touching one foot per beat. 3) The foot on the last beat is pulled in. 4) Next round starts from the same place. 5) The last leg out wins. Children who are out stay seated, so no one feels alone. For one child, use a single child's two legs.
- **Chant (folk):** اتل متل توتوله / گاو حسن چه جوره / نه شیر داره نه پستون / شیرشو بردن هندستون / یک زن کردی بستون / اسمشو بذار عم قزی / دور کلاش قرمزی / هاچین و واچین / یه پاتو ورچین ("…how is Hasan's cow? / no milk, no udder / they took her milk to Hindustan / take a Kurdish wife / call her Am-Ghezi / red round her hat / hachin va vachin / lift one leg").
- **Variant:** "اتل متل یک مورچه" about an ant with a hurt foot. It avoids the wife line, but its author is unknown and it may be modern, so check rights.
- **Skills:** language, self-control, feelings (fair chance).
- **Sensitivity:** the "take a Kurdish wife" line treats an ethnic group as something to acquire. Recommend the ant version or dropping that line, and say so openly. Scholars read "Hendustun" differently (Amani, 2022).
- **Sources:** [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/اتل_متل_توتوله); [fa.wikipedia, children's games](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/بازی‌های_کودکان)

**14. Pire Isırdı Çık Yukarı** — "the flea bit, climb up"
- **Origin and confidence:** Cyprus (Turkish Cypriot), both boys and girls. Grade **L**: stub.
- **Ages etc.:** 2-6 y (sug.). Two players, no materials.
- **Rules:** 1) Stack hands. 2) Lightly pinch the skin of the hand above with thumb and forefinger. 3) Say the line, and the bottom hand moves to the top. 4) Repeat until bored.
- **Skills:** hands, language, self-control.
- **Safety:** "pretend pinch" or a gentle press for under-3s.
- **Family:** see Kinyulinyuli (15).
- **Source:** [tr.wikipedia](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pire_ısırdı_çık_yukarı)

**15. Kinyulinyuli** — *kinyuli nyulika*
- **Origin and confidence:** East Africa. Grade **L**: Swahili stub, and the song text is not given.
- **Ages etc.:** 2-6 y (sug.). 3+ players.
- **Rules:** players stack palms, pinching on the back of the hand below. One sings the kinyulinyuli song, and the player whose hand is on top comes away, until a winner is left.
- **Skills:** hands, language.
- **Safety:** gentle press only.
- **Source:** [sw.wikipedia](https://sw.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinyulinyuli)

#### B. Toddlers to preschool (start 18 months-4 years)

**16. Ukuti Ukuti** — *ukuti* (palm frond)
- **Origin and confidence:** East Africa, a song game for very young children. Grade **L**: stub.
- **Ages:** 2-5 y (sug.). 3+ players. Small space, in or outdoors.
- **Rules:** stand in a circle holding hands, and run around in the circle while chanting "Ukuti ukuti".
- **Chant:** only the title words are sourced. The full lyric is NOT verified (see Gaps).
- **Skills:** movement, language, feelings.
- **Safety:** walk speed for under-3s.
- **Source:** [sw.wikipedia](https://sw.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukuti_ukuti)

**17. Kula Mbakishie Baba** — "Eat, and leave some for Dad"
- **Origin and confidence:** East Africa. Grade **L**: the page is flagged as needing sources.
- **Ages etc.:** 18 m-4 y (sug.). 2+ players. Toy or imaginary food.
- **Rules:** sitting in a circle, one child pretends to feed another saying "Kula, mbakishie baba". The fed child pretends to eat and sets aside some for Dad. Take turns.
- **Skills:** imagination, feelings, language.
- **Safety:** imaginary or large toy food only for under-3s (choking).
- **Sensitivity:** let families name any loved one.
- **Source:** [sw.wikipedia](https://sw.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kula_mbakishie_baba)

**18. Hamoomak Moorcheh Dareh** — حمومک مورچه داره · "the little bathhouse has ants"
- **Origin and confidence:** Iran. Grade **L**.
- **Ages etc.:** 2-6 y (sug.). 3+ players. Small circle.
- **Rules:** 1) Hold hands in a circle. 2) The leader sings. 3) Everyone sits and rises on "beshin o pasho". 4) Anyone who is late is out; the leader may trick by saying "beshin o khiz".
- **Chant (use only these four lines):** حمومک مورچه داره / دور و ورش کوچه داره / جون تو خنده داره / بشین و پاشو خنده داره ("little bathhouse has ants / alleys all around it / on your life, it's funny / sit and rise, it's funny").
- **Skills:** movement, self-control, language.
- **Adapt:** no elimination for under-4s.
- **Rights caution:** the page's further verses look like a pop song (it links to a Googoosh song). Do not use them.
- **Source:** [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/حمومک_مورچه_داره)

**19. Kalagh Par** — کلاغ پر · *kalāgh par* · "crow flies"
- **Origin and confidence:** Iran. Grade **L**, but the source is explicit about age. Similar "bird flies" games exist elsewhere (not verified here), so make no single-origin claim.
- **Ages etc.:** under 5 (src). An adult leads. Any number, on the floor or a table.
- **Rules:** 1) Leader and children put a forefinger on the ground. 2) Leader lifts a finger saying "crow flies!", and all copy. 3) Leader names other birds. 4) Sometimes a non-flyer: "table flies!" 5) Children who lift are out in the source. Others reply "a table has no wings!"
- **Skills:** self-control, language, thinking.
- **Adapt:** no elimination, and laugh at the wrong lifts.
- **Source:** [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/کلاغ_پر)

**20. Amu Zanjir Baf** — عمو زنجیرباف · "Uncle Chain-Weaver"
- **Origin and confidence:** Iran. Described as local to Kazerun but known nationwide. Grade **M**. Symbolism claims (Amu Nowruz, spring) are uncited, so don't state them as fact.
- **Ages etc.:** 3-8 y (sug.). 5+ players. Open floor.
- **Rules:** 1) Two children hold the ends of a curved chain of hands. 2) Leader and group trade the lines. 3) "Uncle" names an animal, and all make its sound. 4) In the source, the chain is then pulled until it breaks and the two who let go are penalised.
- **Chant (folk):** سرگروه: عمو زنجیر باف؟ — گروه: بله / زنجیر منو بافتی؟ — بله / پشت کول انداختی؟ — بله / بابا اومده — چی چی آورده؟ / نخودچی کشمش — بخور و بیا / با صدای چی؟ — با صدای گربه — میو میو میو ("Uncle Chain-Weaver? Yes! / Woven my chain? Yes! / Thrown it over your shoulder? Yes! / Daddy's come! What did he bring? / Chickpeas and raisins. Eat and come! / With what sound? A cat's! Meow meow!").
- **Skills:** language, feelings, self-control.
- **Adapt:** skip the pull and penalty for under-6s. Don't serve real chickpeas or raisins to under-4s (choking).
- **Source:** [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/عمو_زنجیرباف)

**21. Khaleh Bozghaleh** — خاله بزغاله · "Auntie Goat"
- **Origin and confidence:** Iran. Grade **L**.
- **Ages etc.:** 3-7 y (sug.). 4+ players. Open floor.
- **Rules:** Auntie Goat protects her kids. A wolf trades the rhyme with her, then tries to take a kid.
- **Chant (folk, sung line by line):** سلام سلام خاله بزغاله / علیک سلام خاله بزغاله / بچه داری خاله بزغاله / نه ندارم خاله بزغاله / پس اینا چیه خاله بزغاله… ("Hello, Auntie Goat / hello to you / got any kids? / no I don't / then what are these?…").
- **Skills:** language, imagination, movement.
- **Sensitivity:** the traditional reply "کور و شلن" ("they're blind and lame") is said to put the wolf off. Replace it with a kinder trick such as "they're sleeping".
- **Source:** [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/بازی‌های_کودکان)

**22. Bayt Byout / Dar Door** — بيت بيوت (Levant) · دار دور (Palestine) · "house of houses"
- **Origin and confidence:** Arab Levant. The same make-believe is global. Grade **L** (Arabic Wikipedia) and **M** (a Palestinian village heritage site, Balata al-Balad).
- **Ages etc.:** 2.5-7 y (sug.). 2+ players. Toys, or stones and cloth.
- **Rules:** Dar Door: each child marks a small "home" with stones, lays a sack or paper floor, tidies it, visits neighbours, talks like grown-ups, and exchanges gifts of figs or almonds. Bayt Byout: dolls and toy kitchens replay the day; boys and girls both take parts.
- **Skills:** imagination, language, feelings.
- **Safety:** whole nuts and stones are not for under-3s, so use cloth "gifts".
- **Sources:** [ar.wikipedia](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/بيت_بيوت); [Balata al-Balad](https://balata-albalad.org/heritage/games-from-the-past/dar-door) (the site forbids copying, so paraphrase only)

**23. Bhatukali** — भातुकली · play kitchen
- **Origin and confidence:** Maharashtra, India. Mentioned in the *Dnyaneshwari* per the source. Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** 2-7 y (sug.). Mock kitchen items.
- **Rules:** children cook with a mock set of kitchen items.
- **Skills:** imagination, hands, language.
- **Sensitivity:** the source frames it as teaching kitchen work. Present it for all genders.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia, Traditional games of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_of_India)

**24. Neé Pele** — *neé pele* · Kpelle make-believe, run by older children
- **Origin and confidence:** Kpelle, Liberia. Grade **H**: Lancy's ethnography.
- **Ages:** toddlers plus older children; caretakers aged 7-9 in the source.
- **Rules:** an older child directs. Toddlers replay the daily round in "little soap operas". Leaves stand for cloth, dirt for salt, sticks for shoes, and gourds for pots. Invented fantasy characters are absent; this is about daily life.
- **Skills:** imagination, language, feelings.
- **App use:** a sibling activity. Keep an adult nearby; the source setting is a watched public space, the "mother ground".
- **Sensitivity:** the source's context is a village setting, so don't romanticise it.
- **Source:** [Lancy, Kpelle Children at Play](https://www.davidlancy.org/s/Kpelle_Children_at_Play.pdf)

**25. Babam Çin'den Geldi** — "my father came from China"
- **Origin and confidence:** Turkey. Described as suiting young children in groups. Grade **L**.
- **Ages:** 3-8 y (sug.). 2+ players.

**32. Taq Taq Taqiya** — طاق طاق طاقية · "tap tap little cap"
- **Origin and confidence:** Arab world (Egypt, Gulf, Levant, Saudi, Kuwait), in several national lists. Grade **M**. Drop-the-cloth games are global. Jordan's national heritage entry exists but timed out (Gaps).
- **Ages etc.:** from 5 (src). 4+ players, 7-10 in Gulf accounts. A cloth or cap.
- **Rules:** 1) Sit in a circle facing in. 2) One runs round with a cloth singing; the group answers. 3) Drop it behind someone. No looking back until the runner has passed. 4) The chosen child chases; if the runner takes the empty place, roles swap.

- **Skills:** movement, self-control, language.
- **Adapt:** the Gulf version hits with the cloth, so use a tag. Replace the "donkey" line if preferred.
- **Sensitivity:** the Pepsi line shows the chant is not "ancient". Avoid "age-old" framing. Change the named rider if users query it.

**46. Aq Sandyq, Kök Sandyq** — Ақ сандық, көк сандық · "white chest, blue chest"

#### D. Ages about 6-12

**57. Gol ya Pooch** — گل یا پوچ · "flower or empty"
- **Origin and confidence:** Iran. Also known as *por tā pik*. Grade **M**. Hidden-object hand games are widespread: Iraq's *Mheibes* is its Ramadan relative.
- **Ages:** 5-12 y (sug.). 2+ teams. A button or pebble.
- **Rules:** 1) Show the "flower", hide it in one fist. All hold out fists. 2) The other team studies faces and hands. 3) Their leader rules out fists one by one saying "pooch" (empty). 4) Find it: score points and take the flower. First to the agreed score wins (21 in the source).
- **Skills:** thinking, feelings (reading faces), self-control.
- **Safety:** no buttons near under-3s (the source lists a small button or bead).
- **Sources:** [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/گل_یا_پوچ); [en.wikipedia, Mheibes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mheibes)

**58. Raja Mantri Chor Sipahi** — राजा मंत्री चोर सिपाही · "king, minister, thief, soldier"
- **Origin and confidence:** India; Bengal has its own version; Iran's *Shah, Dozd, Vazir* is listed and has the same cast. Grade **M**. Rules vary by household, and points differ between pages.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). 4 players. Paper chits, pencil.
- **Rules:** 1) Fold four chits and draw one. 2) The soldier (or minister in another variant) guesses the thief. 3) Right: keep points. Wrong: the guesser's points go to the thief. 4) Add up after several rounds.
- **Chant:** "Sipahi, sipahi, chor ko pakdo…" ("Soldier, soldier, catch the thief…").
- **Skills:** thinking, language, feelings, simple sums.
- **Sources:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raja_Mantri_Chor_Sipahi); [en.wikipedia, Bengali games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_traditional_games); [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/بازی‌های_کودکان)

**59. Al-Kharita** — الخارطة · "the map"
- **Origin and confidence:** Palestine (Balata; also in the Palestinian games list). Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). 3+ players. Paper, pencil, a small object.
- **Rules:** one child hides an object, and tells only the map-drawer. The drawer draws landmarks and directions. Others hunt; the finder hides it next.
- **Skills:** thinking (spatial), hands, language.
- **Sources:** [Balata al-Balad](https://balata-albalad.org/heritage/games-from-the-past/the-map); [ar.wikipedia, list](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/قائمة_الألعاب_الشعبية_الفلسطينية)

**60. As-Seniyeh** — الصينية · "the tray"
- **Origin and confidence:** Palestine (Balata). Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** 5-10 y (sug.). 2 players. Three cups, tray, and a large object in place of a ring.
- **Rules:** hide the ring under one of three cups with fast moves, while the other watches and points.
- **Skills:** senses, thinking, hands.
- **Adapt:** the source allows penalties between players; keep it to swapping turns.
- **Safety:** coffee cups can break, so use plastic.
- **Source:** [Balata al-Balad](https://balata-albalad.org/heritage/games-from-the-past/seniyeh)

**61. Azim Sara / Azm Waddah** — عظيم سرا / عظم وضاح · "the little bone travelled" / "bright bone"
- **Origin and confidence:** Saudi Arabia, UAE and Kuwait (modern names vary), with a classical Arabic ancestor in the lexicons. Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). 2 teams. Twilight or night; safe open ground.
- **Rules:** 1) One throws a white bone away from the players. 2) Both teams search. 3) The finder keeps the find secret and heads for the start point, then shouts "sara!". 4) Both teams race: the finder's team protects them, while the other tries to intercept and take the bone. Reaching the start point safely ends the round.
- **Chant (UAE):** "عظيم سرى" — "طاح واندرى" ("the little bone travelled!" "it fell, and it's known").
- **Skills:** senses, movement, self-control.
- **Adapt:** use a glow stick or white-painted wooden spoon in a fenced yard with an adult.
- **Sensitivity:** classical sources mention the Prophet playing it in childhood. Do not use that as a marketing hook.
- **Sources:** [ar.wikipedia](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/عظيم_سرا_%28لعبة_شعبية%29); [ar.wikipedia, Games of the Arabs](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/ألعاب_العرب)

**62. Atahaji** — أتحاجي · riddle duel
- **Origin and confidence:** Mauritania. Grade **L**.
- **Ages:** 5-12 y (sug.). 2+ players.
- **Rules:** one asks what a riddle means. A correct answer means the answerer sets the next. Most points win.
- **Skills:** language, thinking.
- **Source:** [ar.wikipedia](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/ألعاب_شعبية_موريتانية)

**63. Barmaq Oyunu** — "finger game"
- **Origin and confidence:** Azerbaijan (also *Sanama*, *İynə-İynə*). Grade **L**: Turkish Wikipedia stub.
- **Ages:** 6-10 y (sug.). 3+ players.
- **Rules:** stack spread hands. The *ebe* touches one finger per beat, and the last finger drops. When one player's finger is left, a playful forfeit follows: ear held while they pick crow or bird, until it appears.
- **Chant (folk, partly nonsense):** "Əl-əl-əllənə… Molla dedi, çək əlini" ("…the mullah said, pull your hand away"). Also: "Qoz ağacı, / Qotur keçi. / Happan, / Hoppan, / Yarıl, / Yırtıl, / Su iç, / Qurtul." ("Walnut tree, mangy goat, hoppan, hoppan, split, rip, drink water, be free").
- **Skills:** language, self-control.
- **Adapt:** no ear-pulling; everyone copies a bird instead.
- **Source:** [tr.wikipedia](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmak_oyunu)

**64. Gollachhut** — গোল্লাছুট · "circle run"
- **Origin and confidence:** Bangladesh. Grade **H/M**: academic paper plus a government revival programme.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). 2 teams of 5-7. A stick, an outer target, open space.
- **Rules:** 1) The team leader holds the central stick. 2) Teammates link hands and circle it. 3) They break away and run to touch the outer target. 4) Tagged players are out. 5) The leader must finally run too.
- **Chant:** "Golla golla chhote / Sonar golla chhote".
- **Skills:** movement, thinking.
- **Sensitivity:** the paper and the English page suggest it echoes labourers or slaves escaping farm work. This is a hypothesis, to mention carefully and only for older readers.
- **Sources:** [bn.wikipedia](https://bn.wikipedia.org/wiki/গোল্লাছুট); [Roy, Karatoya](https://ir.nbu.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/3874/1/Karatoya%20vol%209%20Article%20No%204.pdf); [Daily Star](http://www.thedailystar.net/news-detail-233288)

**65. Bouchi** — বউচি · "bride"
- **Origin and confidence:** Bengal. Grade **M**: named in the government scheme.
- **Ages:** 8-12 y (sug.). Teams, open space.
- **Rules:** one player waits in a far "home". Teammates dash out on one breath to tag opponents. Then the "bride" runs for home.
- **Skills:** movement, self-control.
- **Safety:** drop breath-holding; use a chant or a count.
- **Sensitivity:** the source names a "bride-snatching" team. Use the source's "old lady" or Tripura's Gella-Chutt "king" framing.
- **Sources:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_traditional_games); [en.wikipedia, Gella-Chutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gella-Chutt); [Daily Star](http://www.thedailystar.net/news-detail-233288)

**66. Chungi** — चुङ्गी
- **Origin and confidence:** Nepal. Grade **M**: magazine plus newspaper readers' memories. Kin to Jianzi and Sipa already in the app, but the DIY ball is new.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). 1+ players. Rubber bands.
- **Rules:** 1) Tie many rubber bands at the middle into a ball. 2) Count kicks in the air: no hands. 3) Pass round a circle, or add tricks.
- **Skills:** movement, hands, self-control. One reader credits it with teaching her perseverance.
- **Safety:** rubber bands are a choking hazard under 3 and for mouthing children. Store them away.
- **Sources:** [ECS Nepal](http://ecs.com.np/features/nepali-street-games); [Himalayan Times](https://thehimalayantimes.com/lifestyle/more-than-just-childs-play)

**67. Kana Mutti Bindeema** — කණා මුට්ටි බිඳීම · "breaking pots blindfolded"
- **Origin and confidence:** Sri Lanka, at April New Year (Aluth Avurudda) games. Grade **M**: two sources.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). Several players, outdoors.
- **Rules:** clay pots of water or sand are hung in a row, one with blue water. Blindfolded players swing a stick to hit the right one.
- **Skills:** senses, movement, feelings.
- **Adapt:** paper bags or water balloons, a pool noodle, a marked safety circle, one swinger at a time, and an adult.
- **Sensitivity:** New Year games sit in a festival with auspicious timings. Credit it, but don't imitate rites.
- **Sources:** [Leisure and Me (blog)](https://leisureandme.com/sinhala-new-year-games-sri-lanka/); [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_of_Sri_Lanka)

**68. Bandar Killa** — بندر قلعہ · "monkey fort"
- **Origin and confidence:** Pakistan and Punjab. Grade **M**: two pages.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). 4+ players. A rope tied to a tree, and shoes or bean bags.
- **Rules:** 1) A chosen "monkey" holds a rope tied to the tree. 2) Others leave footwear around the tree, and try to retrieve it. 3) Touched: you become the monkey.
- **Chooser:** *pugam pugai* hands up or down.
- **Skills:** movement, thinking.
- **Adapt:** the source ends by throwing shoes at the monkey. Drop this (shoe-throwing is also insulting in many cultures) and use bean bags, not shoes.
- **Sources:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_of_Pakistan); [en.wikipedia, Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_in_Punjab,_India)

**69. Diketo** — also Magave, Upuca, Puca
- **Origin and confidence:** South Africa, Lesotho and Botswana, called one of ten recognised indigenous games. Grade **M**. Five-stone relatives are global, but the hole rule differs from Gonggi.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). 2 players. Pebbles or marbles, and a shallow hole or circle.
- **Rules:** throw the *mokinto* up. Rake stones out of the hole before catching, then return them one at a time. Repeat in twos and so on. Miss the catch and it's the next turn. First through all ten rounds wins.
- **Skills:** hands, thinking, self-control.
- **Safety:** choking hazard under 3 (see the app's `cpsc-small-parts` source). Store away from babies, and use a bean bag as *mokinto* (Adapt).
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diketo_%28game%29)

**70. Mdako** — *mdako*
- **Origin and confidence:** East Africa, also *Tikri* in Tanzania. Grade **L**.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). 2+ players. 12 pebbles, a "queen", a small circle or hole.
- **Rules:** throw the queen up, sort pebbles from the circle in ones, twos and so on, and don't drop her.
- **Skills:** hands, thinking.
- **Safety:** as for 69.
- **Sources:** [sw.wikipedia](https://sw.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mdako); [sw.wikipedia, Tikri](https://sw.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikri)

**71. Assyk Atu** — Асық ату · "shooting anklebones"
- **Origin and confidence:** Kazakhstan, UNESCO Representative List 2017. Grade **H**. Relatives include Turkish *aşık*, Iranian *ghap-bazi*, Afghan *bujal bazi* and Mongolian shagai, so no exclusive origin.
- **Ages:** practitioners "mainly children aged between four and eighteen" (src). Outdoors. 2+ players.
- **Rules:** each player has a set of *assyks* (sheep talus) and a bright *saka*. Shoot to knock assyks out of the field. The bone's landing position matters.
- **Skills:** hands, thinking, feelings.
- **Adapt:** well-cleaned bones or wooden equivalents; small parts are a choking hazard under 3.
- **Sensitivity:** still widely played. Use the Kazakh name, and link to UNESCO.
- **Sources:** [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/kazakh-traditional-assyk-games-01086); [en.wikipedia, Shagai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shagai); [tr.wikipedia](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aşık_oyunu)

**72. Morabaraba** — also mlabalaba, mmela, umlabalaba
- **Origin and confidence:** South Africa and Botswana, with a variation in Lesotho. The name is said to come from Southern Sotho for "to mill" or "go round in a circle". Grade **M**. Same family as Twelve Men's Morris; the page also lists relatives such as Iran's *dooz* and Ghana's *achi*. Long-distance origin claims on the page are contested, so avoid them.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). 2 players. Paper board, 12 tokens each.
- **Rules:** 1) Place your 12 "cows" one at a time on empty intersections. 2) A row of three is a "mill", which lets you "shoot" (remove) one opposing cow; cows already in a mill are protected unless all are. 3) When all are placed, move cows to adjacent empty points to form new mills. 4) A third "flying" phase follows (see the page; the source follows Mind Sports South Africa rules). A board can be scratched in sand, with pebbles or coins as cows.
- **Skills:** thinking, self-control.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morabaraba)

**73. Shisima** — "body of water"
- **Origin and confidence:** Kenya. Pieces are *imbalavali*, "water bugs". Grade **L**: the page is flagged and doesn't name the language.
- **Ages:** 6-10 y (sug.). 2 players. Octagon drawn on paper, 3 tokens each.
- **Rules:** place three tokens on consecutive octagon points, leaving a gap at each end. Take turns sliding along a line to an empty neighbouring point. First to make three in a line through the centre wins.
- **Skills:** thinking, self-control.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shisima)

**74. Seega** — سيجة · *sīja*
- **Origin and confidence:** Egypt per the source; Arab Levant too (Balata has a page). Described in writing since at least 1836. Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). 2 players. 5×5 drawn grid and 12 stones each (the page's count for a 25-cell board).
- **Rules:** take turns placing two pieces, leaving the centre empty. Then move, trapping opposing pieces between yours to remove them.
- **Skills:** thinking.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seega_%28game%29)

**75. Hyena Chase** — North African race game
- **Origin and confidence:** North Africa. Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** 6-10 y (sug.). 2+ players. Spiral drawn on paper or ground, a counter each, die or stick dice.
- **Rules:** 1) Mothers travel from the village to the well at the spiral's centre. 2) A six starts them, and an exact throw lands at the well. 3) There they wash clothes until another six, then head home (no exact throw needed). 4) The first home wins and may play the hyena: a six releases it, it moves at double speed, and any mother it passes is eaten.
- **Skills:** thinking (counting), feelings.
- **Adapt:** for younger children, a caught mother runs back a few spaces rather than being eaten.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyena_chase)

**76. Choko** — related to Yoté
- **Origin and confidence:** Gambia Valley, Mandinka and Fula. Grade **L**. Yoté is said to be a West African gambling game, so skip the betting.
- **Ages:** 8-12 y (sug.). 2 players. 5×5 holes in sand or a drawn grid, 12 pieces each.
- **Rules:** 1) Start empty. 2) Drop one piece per turn, or move orthogonally one space, following the page's "drop initiative" rule. 3) Capture until one side has lost all their pieces. Capture detail is on the page; check it before writing.
- **Skills:** thinking.
- **Sources:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choko_%28game%29); [en.wikipedia, Yoté](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yot%C3%A9)

**77. Wino**
- **Origin and confidence:** East Africa. Grade **L**.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). 3+ players. Bottle, a heap of sand, a soft ball.
- **Rules:** a bottle stands on a sand heap. One player fills it with sand while two others throw a ball to hit them; catch it and throw it far for extra time. A full bottle is a goal.
- **Skills:** movement, hands, feelings.
- **Adapt:** soft ball only.
- **Source:** [sw.wikipedia](https://sw.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wino_%28mchezo%29)

**78. Kombolela** — also *butuo*
- **Origin and confidence:** East Africa. Grade **L**.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). 5+ players. A tin or ball.
- **Rules:** others hide while a guard watches a tin. Seen players are out, but an unseen player who kicks the tin frees them. The first seen guards next.
- **Skills:** movement, thinking.
- **Source:** [sw.wikipedia](https://sw.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kombolela)

**79. Chaskele**
- **Origin and confidence:** Ghana, children's bat-and-ball. Grade **L**.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). 2+ players. Crushed can (Adapt: a soft ball), stick, tyre or bucket.
- **Rules:** one throws at the bucket; the defender bats it away.
- **Skills:** movement, hands.
- **Safety:** crushed cans have sharp edges, hence the soft ball.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaskele)

**80. Aýterek Günterek** — "moon tree, sun tree"; also Ak Terek Gök Terek
- **Origin and confidence:** Central Asia, the name here being Turkmen. Grade **L**. The Red Rover shape is widespread.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). 8+ players. Grass.
- **Rules:** two lines link arms. One asks "Who do you need?", and the named player runs at the line to break through.
- **Skills:** movement, feelings.
- **Adapt:** collisions cause injuries, so "walk and duck under" rather than run through.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C3%BDterek_G%C3%BCnterek_%28game%29)

**81. Teyzem Çarşıya Gitti** — "my aunt went to the market"
- **Origin and confidence:** Turkey. Grade **L**. Cumulative memory games exist in many cultures.
- **Ages:** 6-12 y (sug.). 2+ players.
- **Rules:** "My aunt went to the market and bought an apple." The next repeats and adds, with words starting with the same letter.
- **Skills:** language, thinking.
- **Source:** [tr.wikipedia](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Çocuk_oyunu)

**82. Antakshari** — अंताक्षरी · "last letter"
- **Origin and confidence:** India. Grade **M**.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). 2+ teams, voices only.
- **Rules:** each player sings the first verse of a song, and the next must start a song on the consonant the last one ended with.
- **Skills:** language, feelings.
- **Adapt:** works with any family's languages. Sung film songs are copyrighted, so the app should describe the game and quote no lyrics.
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antakshari)

**83. Sona (sand drawing)** — *lusona*, plural *sona*
- **Origin and confidence:** Chokwe and Luchazi; eastern Angola, northwestern Zambia and adjacent DR Congo. Grade **M**: page citing Kubik and Gerdes.
- **Ages:** 7-12 y (sug.). Sand, or paper and dots.
- **Rules:** traditionally made by drawing experts in smoothed sand: mark equidistant dots, then draw a continuous line around them. Dots can stand for trees, people or animals, and lines for paths, rivers or walls. Used as a memory aid for proverbs, fables, games, riddles and animals.
- **Skills:** thinking (patterns), hands, language.
- **Sensitivity:** tied to transmitted knowledge. Offer it as "inspired by" a named community, avoid inventing story meanings, and seek a community or scholarly reviewer. Don't call it a kids' craft from "Africa".
- **Source:** [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusona)

#### Inclusive rule worth using across the app
- **Nokhodi (نخودی, "little pea"):** in Iranian children's games, a child who is extra or much younger, whom the group doesn't want to upset, plays a neutral role so they're still in the game. This could be a named sibling-play tip. Grade L — [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/بازی‌های_کودکان)
- A related ethnographic parallel: Kpelle older children ignore a novice's errors so play can go on, and start correcting only as skill grows — [Lancy](https://www.davidlancy.org/s/Kpelle_Children_at_Play.pdf)

#### Do not adapt or do not use
- **Elating Belating Sailo (Bengal):** a girls' rhyming team game that scholars link to the sale of girls and slavery. Don't publish as a fun game — [Roy, Karatoya](https://ir.nbu.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/3874/1/Karatoya%20vol%209%20Article%20No%204.pdf)
- **Bambé (Kpelle):** a children's secret-society game replicating boys' initiation. Not for adaptation — [Lancy](https://www.davidlancy.org/s/Kpelle_Children_at_Play.pdf)
- **Beri beri / Nawai luba (Afghanistan):** girls' doll wedding, with a song mocking bride price and a mock sheep slaughter. Rich ethnographically, but it carries child-marriage overtones. If used at all, keep only the doll-making — [AAN](https://www.afghanistan-analysts.org/en/reports/context-culture/playful-pastimes-and-much-more-seven-folk-games-from-afghanistan/). Turkish *Gelin-Güvey* has the same concern, and its source's "prettiest girl is bride" rule should not be repeated — [tr.wikipedia](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelin_Güvey_Oyunu)
- **Fugdi (Goa/Konkan):** performed on sacred ground and before Ganesha; some forms are vow rituals. Not a game to borrow — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fugdi)
- **Akuaba (Ghana):** ritual fertility figures, not toys — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akuaba)
- **Physically harmful as sourced:** Al Yeşil (belt beating, Turkish page), Hoop Hey (Morocco, kicking and hitting), Tobo (East Africa, chase and hit), Khana Shahidagan (Afghan, "martyr's house" name, ear-pinch penalties), Çivi (thrown nails; the source itself warns), Bok-bok, Chungakhela (firecrackers), and Kotta Pora (pillow fight 2 m up) — [tr.wikipedia](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_yeşil_oyunu); [tr.wikipedia, Çivi](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Çocuk_oyunu); [en.wikipedia, South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_of_South_Africa); [en.wikipedia, Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_traditional_games); [ar.wikipedia](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/هوب_هيه_%28لعبة_شعبية%29); [sw.wikipedia](https://sw.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobo); [AAN](https://www.afghanistan-analysts.org/en/reports/context-culture/playful-pastimes-and-much-more-seven-folk-games-from-afghanistan/); [Leisure and Me](https://leisureandme.com/sinhala-new-year-games-sri-lanka/)

### Inferences
- **Game families matter more than origins.** Blindfold tag, drop-the-cloth, high tag, arch games, five stones and mancala each recur across this whole region and beyond. A "family" card with each community's own chant would be more truthful than "the Iranian version of duck-duck-goose".
- **Chants are where the real cultural difference lies.** The rules are often near-identical; the words, rhythm and humour are what's distinct. Native-speaker audio is likely to matter more than illustration for these cards.
- **Baby play exists in local languages but is rarely labelled "a game".** It tends to be recorded as lullaby, poetry or "folklore" (Azerbaijani *oxşama*, Tamil *Pillaitamil*, Uzbek *ovutmachoq*, Kazakh *mäpeleu*). A future search pass should use those genre words.
- **Age adaptation pattern.** Most elimination rules can drop out for under-5s without losing the game, as Persian and Kpelle practices of keeping the youngest in already suggest.
- **Rights.** Folk rhymes with no known author appear to be public domain, but collected texts, modern lines (Pepsi) and pop-song additions (Hamoomak) need a check. Legal review should confirm.

### Gaps
- **Search-dependent gaps.** No baby game verified for Kurdish or Armenian (only lullabies, see Q4). No Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, Amharic, Zulu or Xhosa-language source reached. No Sri Lankan Tamil or Sinhala-language source. Afghan baby play not found.

- **Not reached at all:** NCERT Jaadui Pitara, NIPUN Bharat, KICD, CAPS, NaCCA, Pratham Books, StoryWeaver, TAFISA, British Council, ICCP.

## Q2. What does cross-cultural research say about how adults play with children, and how should an app present this?

### Takeaway
Adult-child playing is not a universal norm. In many communities older siblings and peers are children's main play partners, while parents support play in other ways. PlayNest should offer these games as options from named communities, never as evidence that any group's parenting is lacking or superior.

### Cited Findings
- "Although play is believed to be universal, the amount of attention devoted to play in a particular society depends in part on the cultural beliefs about the nature of childhood." Some parents see themselves as playmates, "but in many communities it is older siblings and peers who are the children's primary play partners" — [Vandermaas-Peeler, Online Readings in Psychology and Culture](https://doi.org/10.9707/2307-0919.1054)
- "Definitions and norms of play established in Western industrialized economies have not always been useful or adequate" for interpreting parent-child play elsewhere. Play-like activity may include humour, status levelling or work-related activities — [Roopnarine and Davidson 2015, American Journal of Play](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/7-2-article-parent-child-play-across-cultures.pdf)
- **Measured differences** in that same review:
  - Mothers acted as playmates 47% of the time in the US, versus 7% in Guatemala and 24% in India (Rogoff et al. 1993, small-scale comparison).
  - Children played with one adult 16-17% of the time in two US communities, 4% among the Efé (DR Congo), and 3% among Maya in Guatemala (Morelli et al. 2003).
  - Aka fathers' relative time in play with infants was 23%, mothers' 13% (Hewlett 1987).
  - Lese fathers played more than Efé fathers despite being near infants less (18% vs 7%; Fouts 2013) — [Roopnarine and Davidson](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/7-2-article-parent-child-play-across-cultures.pdf)
- Turkish fathers in one study strongly encouraged play and saw it as learning, though low-income fathers were less likely to see it as beneficial. Mothers in Vietnam and Morocco were more reluctant to believe dirt from play was good for health — [Roopnarine and Davidson](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/7-2-article-parent-child-play-across-cultures.pdf)
- Alloparenting is common: siblings, aunts and grandparents "may engage in more play and play-like activities than do fathers" in many communities. The review's conclusion calls for research on local ecology, work patterns and beliefs about play's value — [Roopnarine and Davidson](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/7-2-article-parent-child-play-across-cultures.pdf)
- Lancy on Kpelle childhood in Liberia:
  - Infants ride on the mother in a *lappa*, toddlers play with older sibling carers on the "mother ground" while adults work nearby, and play and watching work blend.
  - Toddlers are not given purpose-made toys, but handle found objects; they learn games by watching older children, and no one teaches them.
  - He warns that treating highly educated Euro-Americans' development program as "the norm for the species" is a mistake, and that mother-child play is seen by elites as universal, "which, is not at all the case elsewhere" — [Lancy, Kpelle Children at Play](https://www.davidlancy.org/s/Kpelle_Children_at_Play.pdf). His 2007 paper "Accounting for Variability in Mother-Child Play" (*American Anthropologist* 109(2)) is cited there, but I could not read it (403) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.2007.109.2.273)
- **Work and play.** Lancy describes a helper stage beginning as early as 14 months, a "chore curriculum", and argues that where toddlers' offers to help are welcomed they remain helpers later. Where they are not welcomed, the drive fades. He also notes tolerance for sharp tools in such settings, which PlayNest should not copy — [Lancy, Child Helpers](https://www.davidlancy.org/s/Child-Helpers.pdf); [Lancy's site](https://www.davidlancy.org/)
- **Critique of deficit framing.** Scheidecker, Chaudhary, Keller, Mezzenzana and Lancy (2023) challenge global early-childhood claims of "poor brain development" in the global South, arguing that literature ignores anthropology. Parents may not "actively involve" children in stimulating activities "simply… because others are doing it already": similar-aged siblings and cousins. They also say it would be "equally invalid" to call WEIRD children deprived for lacking constant cousins. They suggest building on existing social structures, such as child-to-child approaches — [Ethos 2023](https://www.davidlancy.org/s/Ethos-2023-Scheidecker-Poor-brain-development-in-the-global-South-Challenging-the-science-of-early-c.pdf)
- The Developmental Niche (Super and Harkness): settings, customs, and caretaker psychology work as a system — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_niche)
- UNICEF and the LEGO Foundation: "People from every culture, economic background and community engage in play from their earliest years." Curricula "must respond to varying cultural contexts", and in Senegal a local classroom kit ensured "the use of local toys, games and other resources" — [UNICEF 2018](https://www.unicef.org/sites/default/files/2018-12/UNICEF-Lego-Foundation-Learning-through-Play.pdf)
- UNESCO defines traditional sports and games as "practices in an individual or collective manner, deriving from regional or local identity, based on accepted rules", and its charter says they "express the world's rich cultural heritage and must be protected and promoted" — [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/sport-and-anti-doping/traditional-sports-and-games)
- Living-heritage examples:
  - Kyrgyz communities after 1991 identified loss of knowledge and young people's lack of interest as the urgent threats to traditional games — [UNESCO Good Safeguarding Practice](https://ich.unesco.org/en/BSP/nomad-games-rediscovering-heritage-celebrating-diversity-01738)
  - Ahlan Simsim's model (Sesame Workshop + IRC, in Syria, Jordan, Lebanon and Iraq) is Arabic-language and built with over 100 local and regional partners, aiming to bolster "local actors" — [IRC](https://www.rescue.org/ahlansimsim)

### Inferences
Suggested presentation rules for the app (my synthesis):
1. **Name a people and a place**, not "African" or "Asian". Mention other places where it's shared. Use "played in…" or "known in…", not "invented in…".
2. **Avoid the words** exotic, ancient, tribal, primitive and simple, and avoid "age-old" claims a source can't back. The Pepsi line in Taq Taq Taqiya shows these games keep changing, and that's a positive thing to say.
3. **No deficit or superiority framing.** Don't suggest that families who don't sit and play are doing less, or that other cultures are more "natural". Offer a neutral line such as: "In many families older children or cousins lead this game."
4. **Treat Western games as one tradition among many**, rather than as the default that others are "versions of". English-language name equivalents can appear in a neutral "similar games" row.
5. **Separate tradition from adaptation visibly:** "Traditionally… / At home you could…". Note when a rule is changed for safety.
6. **Gender:** record who traditionally plays, then invite all children, without commentary on the community.
7. **Religion:** keep names and references that occur in rhymes, don't turn rites into activities, and don't use holy figures as a hook.
8. **Present sibling-led play carefully:** celebrate it, but say a responsible older person stays close, particularly for under-3s, because safety norms differ.
9. **Avoid poverty storytelling.** A wire car or rag doll is craft and engineering, not "making do".
10. **Review.** Pay native-speaker or diaspora reviewers per culture, offer audio by community speakers, and give users a way to send corrections. All L-graded entries need this.
11. **Say when something is fading or thriving.** Bangladesh, India, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan all have revival work. Present these as living and present-day.

### Gaps
- Could not read Gaskins directly, Roopnarine's *Children's Play in Diverse Cultures*, Lancy's full *Anthropology of Childhood* book, Rogoff's monograph, Keller's Nso work, or Weisner and Gallimore (cited via the sources above only). No IOC/ICSSPE or TAFISA material reached.
- No Iran-specific or Arab-specific parent-child play study reached.

## Q3. Which traditional homemade toys come from these regions, and how can they be made safely?

### Takeaway
The verified set is modest: cloth and sock dolls, a Kuwaiti bicycle-rim hoop, Nepal's rubber-band ball, Afghan scrap dolls, and paper pinwheels. Wire cars, one of the brief's main examples, could not be sourced this session. Indian GI (geographical indication) toys are heritage crafts to admire, not home projects.

### Cited Findings
- **Cloth or sock doll (عروسک پارچه‌ای):** one of the oldest toys still in use, traditionally handmade and stuffed with leftover scraps. A single sock, some cotton, thread, needle, scissors and a marker are enough. This page is generic and translated from English; it does NOT evidence a distinct Persian "Arousak Parchei" tradition — [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/عروسک_پارچه‌ای); [en.wikipedia, Rag doll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rag_doll). **Safe make (mine):** for under-3s, embroider faces and don't use buttons or beads; double-stitch seams; cotton fill must not escape.
- **Afghan scrap dolls and play sets:** dolls of scrap wood and fabric with button eyes, small stone houses, a wooden horse, and a potato on four little sticks as a sheep — [AAN](https://www.afghanistan-analysts.org/en/reports/context-culture/playful-pastimes-and-much-more-seven-folk-games-from-afghanistan/). **Safe make (mine):** no buttons under 3. Keep the wooden horse and stone houses; skip the wedding story.
- **Mauritanian *ouzār* (أوزار):** girls' dolls of men and women, with miniature tents, played with friends. Grade L — [ar.wikipedia](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/ألعاب_شعبية_موريتانية)
- **Sahara and North Africa self-made dolls:** female dolls represent brides, mothers and wives, male dolls horsemen and warriors, with toys used to act out recognisable community scenes (the Toy page citing anthropologist Jean-Pierre Rossie; his own site was unreachable) — [en.wikipedia, Toy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toy)
- **Classical Arabic *banāt* dolls:** a hadith has Aisha playing with dolls with friends, glossed as small figures to play with. Religiously meaningful evidence; don't use it for marketing — [ar.wikipedia, Games of the Arabs](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/ألعاب_العرب)
- **Kuwaiti *darbāḥa* (الدرباحة):** a bicycle rim pushed along with a bent wire while running — [ar.wikipedia](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/ألعاب_شعبية_كويتية). Hoop rolling was also recorded among Tanzanian village children around the 1910s — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoop_rolling). **Safe make (mine):** wrapped rim with no spokes, handle end taped, ages 5+, away from traffic.
- **Chungi rubber-band ball:** see entry 66; rubber bands are also used in Nepal for skipping and cat's cradle — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_of_Nepal). Keep away from under-3s.
- **Bangladeshi paper pinwheel:** the source documents a traditional Bangladeshi pinwheel of paper and plastic only by a captioned image — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinwheel_%28toy%29). **Safe make (mine):** replace the pin with a push-through paper fastener or a taped dowel, adult-assembled.
- **Dug dugi:** a child's rattle with strings and small stone-like beads that strike the core when shaken — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_of_India). The related two-headed damaru is a sacred instrument linked to Shiva, so don't conflate them — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damaru). **Safe make (mine):** ages 3+, large wooden beads firmly tied and stitched.
- **Shekere:** a Yoruba gourd with a net of beads or cowries — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shekere). **Safe make (mine):** ages 3+, large secured beads, or use the app's existing shaker activity.
- **Leaf boat:** a traditional toy made in many parts of the world, using nothing but a leaf — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_boat). Adult supervision near water.
- **Arvind Gupta's toys from trash:** an Indian science educator credited with over 1,300 toys from ordinary materials and recycled rubbish, for village schools — [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2016-02-26/toy-maker-arvind-gupta-visits-canberra-school/7200488); [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arvind_Gupta_%28toy_inventor%29)
- **Heritage crafts to feature, not make at home:**
  - Channapatna lacquered wood toys (GI, Karnataka) — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channapatna_toys)
  - Kondapalli wooden toys (GI, Andhra Pradesh) — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kondapalli_toys)
  - Marapachi dolls, passed mother to daughter and displayed at Golu during Navaratri. Do not repeat the page's claim about a child absorbing medicinal sandalwood by licking the doll. — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marapachi_Dolls)
  - Thatta Kedona "doll village", Punjab, Pakistan, a village doll enterprise established with German artist Senta Siller from the 1990s, so not an ancient tradition — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thatta_Ghulamka)
- **Akua'ba** are ritual fertility figures, not a toy to imitate — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akuaba)

### Inferences
- A safe "make it" card pattern could be: tradition, material swap, age gate, adult step, and a small-parts check against the existing `cpsc-small-parts` source.
- If families have heirloom toys, suggest display or supervised handling, since traditional paints and small parts may not meet current toy rules.

### Gaps
- **Wire cars (Southern and Eastern Africa, "galimoto"):** no accessible source this session, as publisher and reading-site pages returned 403. Do not publish until sourced.
- Corn-husk dolls: the page reached concerns other regions, and nothing specific to these regions was verified. Mossi *biiga*, Namji dolls, Bengali *tepa putul*, Natungram owls, Rishtan whistles and Persian *Arousak Parchei* (museum-level) were not verified.
- Lead-paint and small-parts status of traditional craft toys was not researched.

## Q4. Which non-game traditions could a family borrow, and what safety limits apply?

### Takeaway
Rhymes, naming praise and first-steps moments can be borrowed easily. Cradle, hammock and carrying traditions need current safe-sleep and carrier rules stated plainly. Religious rites should be described with respect, not offered as activities.

### Cited Findings
**Massage (Maalish already in app)**
- Infant massage is traditional in South Asia and many African countries. A 2013 Cochrane review found evidence insufficient to support it for term babies under 6 months for growth, so treat it as connection, not treatment — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_massage)

**Cradles and swings**
- **Beshik (Uzbek, also Tajik, Turkmen, Karakalpak, Kyrgyz and others):** a rocking cradle of mulberry, willow or other wood, mentioned in the 11th-century *Divanu Lughat al-Turk*, with swaddling bands and a toileting opening — [uz.wikipedia](https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beshik)
- **Kazakh Besikke Salu:** the first cradling ceremony, led by a respected woman, with sweets passed through the cradle's opening; its protective smoke with *adyraspan* is a rite, and not for home copying — [kk.wikipedia](https://kk.wikipedia.org/wiki/Бесікке_салу). Its song: Әлди-әлди ақ бөпем, / Ақ бесікке жат бөпем ("Aldi-aldi, my white baby, lie in the white cradle").
- **Safety:** AAP advises placing babies on their back on a firm, flat surface, and says any surface inclining more than 10 degrees isn't safe. Alternative sleep products, including hammocks, baby boxes and in-bed sleepers, are acceptable only if they meet federal crib or bassinet safety standards. If a baby falls asleep in a swing, sling, carrier or car seat, move them to a firm flat surface as soon as possible — [AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/A-Parents-Guide-to-Safe-Sleep.aspx). So: admire the cradle and sing the song, but do not recommend binding, hammocks or traditional cradles for sleep unless the product meets current safety standards. Rock awake babies in arms. The Persian "gahvareh" wasn't verified as a named tradition.

**Lullabies by community (songs can be borrowed if text is traditional)**
- **Kurdish:** لای لای گۆلم لای لە باخی دیدە / ئارام بنیشە و شادی ببینە ("Lullaby, flower of the garden of my eyes / be calm and have sweet dreams"; the source's Persian gloss) — [fa.wikipedia, Lalayi](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/لالایی)
- **Persian regional:** Torbat-e Jam "لالالالا گل قندم / به دیدار تو خرسندم" ("lalala, my sugar flower, seeing you makes me glad"); Gorgan "لای لای لای، گل لاله / پلنگ در کوه چه می‌ناله" ("lai lai, tulip flower, how the leopard moans on the mountain"). Some Iranian lullabies are wordless humming ("lāveh lāveh kam, hu hu hu") — [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/لالایی). *Gonjeshk Lala* is a composed 1990 song (Rahmandoust), so not folk and likely in copyright — [fa.wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/گنجشک_لالا)
- **Armenian:** hundreds of oral lullabies; the word *oror* (rock) repeats and mimics the cradle; regional words include *nannik*, *loorik*, *nana*. Many invoke saints and voice grief, so check content — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lullabies_of_Armenia)
- **Bengali:** "ঘুমপাড়ানি মাসিপিসি মোদের বাড়ি এসো" ("Sleep-bringing aunties, come to our house"); also "Khoka ghumalo" — [bn.wikipedia](https://bn.wikipedia.org/wiki/ঘুমপাড়ানি_গান)
- **Uzbek, Uyghur, Tajik *Alla*:** often sung by women rocking the *beshik*, and sometimes close to weeping in tone — [uz.wikipedia](https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alla_%28qoʻshiq%29)
- **Malayalam:** *Omanathinkal Kidavo* (1813, Irayimman Thampi), composed for a baby king; a named author, not folk — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omanathinkal_Kidavo)
- **Turkish *ninni*:** collected as women's oral art, mostly anonymous, reflecting the mother's wishes and life — [tr.wikipedia](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninni)

**Carrying**
- Kpelle infants are carried on the mother's body in a *lappa* and watch community life — [Lancy](https://www.davidlancy.org/s/Kpelle_Children_at_Play.pdf). Aka babies are normally carried through the day by different camp members instead of being laid in cots or cradles, and Aka fathers are recorded as having unusually high physical contact with infants (Hewlett) — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aka_people).
- **Safety (TICKS):** Tight, In view at all times, Close enough to kiss, Keep chin off chest, Supported back; avoid C-shaped positions and "cocoon" products; bend from the knees and use all straps — [Australian Government Product Safety](https://www.productsafety.gov.au/consumers/keep-baby-safe/move-baby-safely/baby-carriers-wraps-and-slings-guide)

**Naming and first-step moments**
- **Yoruba *oríkì*:** praise names and poetry; per Samuel Johnson it expresses what a child is, or is hoped to become. Naming usually on the 7th or 8th day — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Or%C3%ADk%C3%AC); [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_name)
- **Akan day names:** children named for the weekday of birth and birth order, with meanings about soul and character — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_name)
- **Kazakh *Tusau kesu* (Тұсаукесер):** when a baby first walks, a brisk, capable adult cuts a multi-coloured thread that hobbles the ankles while wishes are spoken ("Be swift!") and sweets are scattered; older toddlers race — [kk.wikipedia](https://kk.wikipedia.org/wiki/Тұсаукесер). **Adapt (mine):** ribbon laid by the feet, no tying and no scissors near skin.
- **Hindu *annaprashana*:** first-rice rite — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annaprashana). **Islamic *aqiqah*:** birth observance — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqiqah). These are religious rites: describe them only, and do not repackage them as activities.
- **Ethiopian Ashenda (girls' festival) and Buhe (Orthodox feast):** festival songs with religious ties, which are context, not activities — [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashenda); [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buhe)

### Inferences
- The most borrowable idea with the lowest risk: a family "praise line" or wish song inspired by *oríkì*, Ting Ting Chaoro and *oxşama*. Parents make up their own words naming the qualities they love in their child. It should be labelled "inspired by", and should not claim to be an *oríkì*.
- A "first steps" card can gather Tusau kesu, Varugai and other welcomes as a neutral celebration list.
- The app could present lullabies as "songs that hold grief too" (Armenian, Iraqi, Uzbek), which respects their real content and normalises hard feelings for parents. The existing wellbeing guides may be a natural home for this.

### Gaps
- Mustard-oil caution for newborn skin is commonly cited (Darmstadt et al.), but I could not retrieve it this session (DOI 403; PubMed page returned nothing usable). The English Mustard oil page had no relevant text. Verify before publishing any oil advice.
- Not verified: Bangladesh-specific massage practice; Middle Eastern salting or swaddling customs; Persian "nanu" hammock cradle; Swahili or Zulu carrying-cloth terms; Iranian naming rhymes.
- All safety notes marked "mine" are my reading of general hazards, plus AAP and Australian Government guidance, not a clinical review. A clinician should check them.
