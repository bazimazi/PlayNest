# Folk Tales for Retelling (Ages 2–12) and the Evidence on Shared Reading and Oral Storytelling: PlayNest "Story time" Source Pack

Current as of November 2026. Prepared for a writer producing ORIGINAL retellings (150–500 words) in plain language, followed by conversation prompts.

## Which folk tales from around the world can a writer retell for ages 2–12, and what are the facts of each?

### Takeaway
This pack holds 104 tale records across every inhabited region: 100 retelling candidates and 4 status records (Nanabozho, Coyote, Sedna, Aboriginal Dreaming) that explain why no plot is offered for retelling. Most of the world's public-domain tales are safe for an original retelling on copyright grounds, but copyright is the wrong test for Indigenous and sacred material. An expired copyright does not end a community's cultural rights, so those entries are marked ASK or NO.

**Method limit that affects everything below.** The web search budget for this session was exhausted on the first attempt, before any result came back. I did not route around it. All findings come from direct fetches of documents I could name: Project Gutenberg book IDs, Internet Archive book IDs, D. L. Ashliman's folktext site, named Wikipedia articles, publisher pages, and exact-DOI lookups in Crossref. Consequences:
- A tale missing here is not evidence that no good source exists.
- Several items named in the brief could not be located and are listed under Gaps rather than guessed.
- Sacred Texts (sacred-texts.com), Te Ara, AIATSIS, IFLA and NZHistory returned HTTP 403 to automated fetches, so nothing here depends on them.

### Cited Findings

#### How to read a record
- **Check tag.** **[T]** means I opened the primary or translated text in this session and read the passage, at least its opening and ending unless the record says otherwise. **[S]** means I read only a secondary reference (usually Wikipedia, sometimes a publisher page), so the writer should treat it as a lead and open the named primary source before finalising.
- **Plot summaries** are in my own words. No modern retelling is quoted. Where a primary text is old enough to be public domain I still paraphrase, so the writer starts from facts, not phrasing.
- **Stance** is my judgement unless a community statement is cited:
  - **OPEN**: broad public circulation, no restriction found.
  - **CARE**: open, but with a specific sensitivity to handle.
  - **ASK**: get community or rights-holder input before publishing.
  - **NO**: do not write a retelling; link to community-authored work instead.
  - "No community statement found" is not the same as permission.
- **Age bands** are 2–3, 4–5, 6–8, 9–12. Age fit and softening advice are my editorial judgement.
- **Prompts** follow Whitehurst's CROWD types: **C** Completion, **R** Recall, **O** Open-ended, **W** Wh-, **D** Distancing. I wrote them for this pack.
- **Copyright note.** Project Gutenberg states its texts are free in the United States and that readers elsewhere must check local law ([Gutenberg header text](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4018)). Life-plus-70 countries can differ for 20th-century compilers. Have counsel confirm the position for any title you intend to credit by name.

#### A. Persian world and Central Asia

**1. Khāle Suske (خاله سوسکه, "Auntie Cockroach") — Iran; widely reprinted since the Qajar era. Attribution: high for Iranian tradition; exact origin uncertain. [S]**
- **Plot:** Her father is tired of having an unmarried daughter and sends her to Hamedan to marry a rich man, Mash Ramazoon, who is said to offer good bread and a crystal water-pipe. She sets off in a veil of onion skin and shoes of walnut wood. On the road suitors stop her: a butcher, a baker and others. To each she puts one question: if we quarrelled, how would you beat me? Each answers with his tools of trade, and she refuses him. Then gentle Āghā Mushe, Mr Mouse, says he would only dab kohl on her eyes with his soft tail. She marries him. Later she falls into a stream and he rescues her with a golden ladder. She falls ill, and while he stirs broth for her he tumbles into the pot and dies. She wears black for him.
- **Variants:** Marzolph classifies it as a chain tale. Khosrow Salehi's 400-page study (1387 SH) reviews about 150 published versions. It is related to the Spanish and Caribbean cockroach-and-mouse tale (record 88).
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition. Modern illustrated editions and Azaryazdi's retelling are protected.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8; choosing kindly, friendship, loss.
- **Cautions:** This is the main issue with the tale. Persian critics are divided: Shadi Sadr calls it a classic gender stereotype, Pourgiv says it normalises abuse, Lezgi reads it as a critique. A cockroach heroine may also amuse or repel. Stance: OPEN for Iranian families, CARE on content.
- **Softening:** Change the question to "If we quarrelled, how would you speak to me?" This keeps the point that she refuses cruelty and removes beating from a bedtime story. For the ending, either tell it plainly as a sad end for ages 6+, or let her fish him out of the pot and nurse him, and note in the author's note that the tradition ends sadly.
- **Prompts:** C: "'If we quarrel, how would you…?'" R: "Why did she say no to the butcher?" O: "What is happening on her road now?" W: "What did Mr Mouse say he would do?" D: "How do people in our family make up after a quarrel?"
- **Sources:** [Persian Wikipedia](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/خاله_سوسکه). A tool summary of that page rendered the title as "Aunt Spider", which is wrong: سوسک means cockroach or beetle.

**2. Bozak Zangoole-Pā / Shangul o Mangul (بزک زنگوله‌پا، شنگول و منگول) — Iran, Afghanistan. Attribution: high; local form of ATU 123. [S]**
- **Plot:** A mother goat must go out for food and warns her kids never to open the door until they have seen her hand. In the Tehran telling she is Bozboz Ghandi and the kids are Shangul, Mangul and little Habbe-ye Angur, "Grape-seed". The wolf comes claiming to be their mother. They ask to see his hand, it is black, and they refuse. He whitens it with flour and comes back. Now they open up, and he swallows the two older kids while the smallest hides. When the mother returns, the little one tells her everything. She goes straight to the wolf and challenges him, cuts open his belly, brings out her children alive and well, and puts ten stones in their place.
- **Variants:** In Herat the mother is Bozak Zangooleh-Pā ("little bell-foot goat") with Alil, Shalil and Khākestari. In Kabul there are four kids (Angak, Mangak, Golule-ye Sangak, Khākestari). Some versions use henna on the paw. The Grimm form is KHM 5 with seven kids. Armenian (Tumanyan), Egyptian ("The Nanny Goat with the Iron Horns"), Moroccan and Russian forms are listed on the English article.
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition; Grimm is public domain.
- **Age/themes:** 2–3 (softened), 4–5; safety, cleverness, mother's love, fear.
- **Cautions:** Swallowing and belly-cutting. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** The wolf stuffs the kids in his sack, or swallows them whole and unhurt, which is true to the tradition. The mother butts him with her horns until he lets them go. Keep the door rule, since it is the point of the tale. Avoid naming a real stranger-danger lesson as the "moral".
- **Prompts:** C: "'Show me your…!'" R: "What did the wolf do to his hand?" O: "What can you see through the crack in the door?" W: "Who hid?" D: "Who do we open our door for?"
- **Sources:** [شنگول و منگول](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/شنگول_و_منگول); [بزبز قندی](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/بزبز_قندی); [The Wolf and the Seven Young Goats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wolf_and_the_Seven_Young_Goats); [Ashliman's Grimm 5](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/grimm005.html).

**3. Zāl and the Simorgh (زال و سیمرغ) — Iran; Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** The hero Sām has a son, Zāl, born with hair as white as snow; the name itself points to this. Sām is ashamed and frightened, blames an evil spirit, and has the baby left on the Alborz mountain. The great bird Simorgh, who nests on the heights, carries the child up to feed him along with her own young. He grows strong and wise among the birds. Years later Sām is sorry for what he did and comes to the mountain to find his son. The Simorgh gives Zāl up with love and hands him one of her feathers: if ever he is in trouble, he has only to burn it and she will come. Father and son are reunited, Sām tries to make right what he did wrong, and the king honours the young man.
- **Variants:** The feather returns later in the *Shahnameh* at the birth of Rostam.
- **Copyright:** Ferdowsi's poem is public domain; modern translations (Davis) are not. [The Persian Literature, Gutenberg 10315](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10315) carries an old English version, located but not read.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; difference, belonging, forgiveness, nature.
- **Cautions:** Parental abandonment; the article links the name to albinism. Treat it as a tale that honours a child who looks different. Stance: OPEN. Iranian heritage text, not sacred scripture.
- **Softening:** Sām's fear can be stated gently: "He was afraid of what people would say, and he did a wrong thing." Keep the wrongness, since the repair is the story.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Burn my feather and I will…'" R: "Who looked after Zāl?" O: "How does Zāl feel flying from the nest?" W: "What did the Simorgh give him?" D: "Who would you call if you needed help?"
- **Sources:** [Zāl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C4%81l); [زال](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D8%B2%D8%A7%D9%84); [Simurgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simurgh).

**4. The Elephant in the Dark (پیل اندر خانهٔ تاریک) — Persian, Rumi's *Masnavi*, earlier Sanai; older Buddhist Udana form. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** Some travellers bring an elephant to a town and keep it in a dark room. No one in the town has ever seen such an animal, so people go in one by one to find out what it is, and since they cannot see, they feel it with their hands. One touches the trunk and comes out saying an elephant is like a water pipe. One touches an ear and says it is a fan. One touches a leg and says it is a pillar. Another strokes the back and says it is a throne. They argue, each certain, each partly right. Rumi closes by wishing each of them had carried a candle, because a little light would have ended the quarrel (this closing line is from my memory of the *Masnavi*; confirm it).
- **Variants:** In the Pali Udana (68–69) a king has blind men feel the elephant. Sanai tells it before Rumi. Widely retold in Jain and Hindu settings.
- **Copyright:** Rumi and Sanai are public domain; recent translations are not.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; perspective, listening, humility.
- **Cautions:** Prefer the dark-room version. The "blind men" form makes disability the joke. Sufi teaching story, respect the source. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed. It suits the dim-screen setting well: parent and child can feel an object under a blanket first.
- **Prompts:** C: "'It's not a pillar, it's a…!'" R: "What did the ear feel like?" O: "What would you bring into the dark room?" W: "What did each person touch?" D: "When did you and a friend each see the same thing differently?"
- **Sources:** [Blind men and an elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_men_and_an_elephant).

**5. The Merchant and the Parrot (بازرگان و طوطی) — Persian, Rumi's *Masnavi* Book I. Attribution: high. [T, opening read; ending not re-read, confirm in the text]**
- **Plot:** A merchant keeps a clever parrot in a cage. Setting off to trade in India, he asks everyone in his household what gift he should bring back. The parrot asks for only one thing: when you see the free parrots of India, give them my greetings, and ask them whether it is fair that I sit caged while they fly in the trees. In India the merchant passes on the message, and one wild parrot trembles and drops as if dead. He is sorry he spoke. Back home he tells his own parrot what happened, and his parrot too falls down stiff. Grieving, he opens the cage and lifts it out. The bird at once flies up to a high branch and explains: the wild parrot sent a message, showing me how to become free.
- **Variants:** Told in many Persian, Turkish and Indian collections.
- **Copyright:** Rumi is public domain; [Redhouse's *The Mesnevi* (1881), Gutenberg 61724](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/61724).
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; freedom, cleverness, listening.
- **Cautions:** Rumi's frame is a spiritual reading on dying to the self; keep that out of the child's version. Household "slaves" appear in the old text; use "everyone in the house". Playing dead is safe here because the bird is fine. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Let the merchant wave goodbye, a little sadder and wiser.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Tell the parrots of India that I am…'" R: "What gift did the parrot ask for?" O: "What does the bird feel on the branch?" W: "Why did he open the cage?" D: "Is there something you love that is better free?"
- **Sources:** [The Mesnevi, Gutenberg 61724](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/61724).

**6. The Conference of the Birds (منطق‌الطیر, *Manṭiq-uṭ-Ṭayr*) — Persian, Farid ud-Din Attar. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** All the birds of the world gather because they have no king. The hoopoe, wisest of them, says they do have a king, the Simorgh, who lives far away, and offers to lead them there. Each bird has an excuse for staying at home, and each excuse shows a fault we all share. The hoopoe warns them of seven valleys they must cross. They bow their heads and many are afraid, but they set out. The way is hard and most fall behind from thirst, heat or fear. Only thirty birds arrive. When at last they are let in to see the great Simorgh, they find only their own reflection, since *si morgh* means "thirty birds" in Persian. What they were looking for was what they had become.
- **Variants:** Book-length poem; short children's versions usually keep three or four bird excuses.
- **Copyright:** Attar is public domain; modern translations (Wolpé, Darbandi and Davis) are not.
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; courage, patience, friendship, self-knowledge.
- **Cautions:** Sufi allegory of the soul's journey, so treat it respectfully. Many birds die in the source; birds may "turn back" instead. The pun only works if you explain it. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Those who cannot go on turn home with a blessing. The word-play is taught as a small secret at the end.
- **Prompts:** C: "'*Si* means thirty, and *morgh* means…'" R: "Who led the birds?" O: "What would your excuse be?" W: "What did they see at the end?" D: "What was a long hard thing you finished?"
- **Sources:** [The Conference of the Birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Conference_of_the_Birds).

**7. Eat, My Coat, Eat — Mulla Nasruddin / Nasreddin Hodja (Turkey, Persia, Arab world, Central Asia). Attribution: high for the cycle; in 2022 Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Turkey and Turkmenistan jointly inscribed the tradition with UNESCO. [T]**
- **Plot:** The Hodja goes to a banquet in his everyday clothes, a plain coat and old shoes, and nobody greets him. No one offers him a seat, and no one offers him food, and he stands by the door feeling foolish. So he slips home and puts on his finest coat. When he returns, the host bows, leads him to the head of the table, and sets the best dishes before him. The Hodja dips his sleeve in the soup and says kindly, "Eat, my coat, eat." The guests stop talking and stare at him. The host hurries over and asks what on earth he is doing. The Hodja replies that it must have been the coat that was invited, since he himself was not welcome until it arrived, so it had better have the dinner.
- **Variants:** Found as Juha in Arabic and Goha in Egypt; collected from Borrow 1884 onward. Idries Shah's versions are protected.
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition plus [Borrow's *The Turkish Jester* (1884)](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/hodja.html).
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; fairness, cleverness, kindness to strangers.
- **Cautions:** Nasruddin is sometimes presented as a Sufi teacher and a Muslim cleric, so don't mock the title. Ashliman's list also has crude tales and one mocking Kurds, so choose carefully. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Eat, my coat…!'" R: "Why did nobody greet him at first?" O: "What are the guests thinking?" W: "Where did he put his sleeve?" D: "Have you ever been treated differently because of your clothes?"
- **Sources:** [Ashliman, Nasreddin Hodja](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/hodja.html); [Nasreddin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasreddin).

**8. The Cauldron That Died — Nasreddin Hodja (as record 7; related ATU 1592B). [T]**
- **Plot:** The Hodja borrows a big copper cauldron from his neighbour. When he brings it back, there is a tiny pot sitting inside. "Good news," he says. "Your cauldron gave birth." The neighbour laughs at this, is pleased at the extra pot, and keeps both. Some time later the Hodja borrows the cauldron again. Days pass and it does not come back, so the neighbour knocks to ask. "I am so sorry," says the Hodja. "Your cauldron has died." "Died? A cauldron cannot die!" cries the neighbour. The Hodja raises one eyebrow. "You believed it could have a baby. If it can give birth, surely it can die." And the neighbour has nothing to say, and goes home without his cauldron.
- **Variants:** Very widespread; told about Juha, Goha and others.
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition; Borrow and other old collections.
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; cleverness, honesty, greed.
- **Cautions:** The Hodja effectively steals; let the child judge. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Optional coda in which he carries the cauldron home next morning, with a grin. This departs from the tradition, so flag it if used.
- **Prompts:** C: "'If it can have a baby, it can…'" R: "What was inside the cauldron?" O: "Was the Hodja fair?" W: "Why did the neighbour keep the little pot?" D: "When have you believed something because you liked it?"
- **Sources:** [Ashliman, Nasreddin Hodja](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/hodja.html).

**9. The Smell of Food and the Sound of Money — Nasreddin Hodja (as record 7). [T]**
- **Plot:** A poor traveller has only a piece of dry bread. He holds it over an innkeeper's steaming soup pot so that it soaks up the good smell, then eats it. The innkeeper sees this and demands money: you have taken the smell of my soup, so you must pay. The poor man has none, and they go before the Hodja, who is serving as judge. The Hodja listens carefully and asks the innkeeper if he is sure the smell has a price. He is sure. The Hodja then takes out two coins, holds them close to the innkeeper's ear, jingles them, and puts them back in his own pocket. "There," he says. "The smell of the soup is paid for with the sound of the money." And he sends them both away.
- **Variants:** Common across Eurasia, including Japanese and European forms.
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; fairness, cleverness, sharing.
- **Cautions:** None significant. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'The smell of the soup is paid with the sound of…'" R: "What did the Hodja do with the coins?" O: "What is the innkeeper's face doing now?" W: "Who paid in the end?" D: "What smells make you hungry?"
- **Sources:** [Ashliman, Nasreddin Hodja](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/hodja.html).

**10. The Ring in the Dark Room — Nasreddin (Persian-attributed form). Attribution: medium; the source describes it as undated and notes the modern "drunk under a streetlight" joke. [S]**
- **Plot:** Nasruddin is on his hands and knees in the yard outside his house, looking for something. A neighbour comes over and joins him, patting about in the dust. Eventually the neighbour asks what they are searching for, and Nasruddin says it is his ring. "Where exactly did you lose it?" the neighbour asks. "Oh, in the dark room inside the house," Nasruddin says. The neighbour sits back on his heels. "Then why on earth are we looking out here in the yard?" "Because," says Nasruddin, as if it were obvious, "there is much more light out here." In the Sufi use of the tale, it comments on people who look for answers where it is easy to look, rather than where the answer may be.
- **Variants:** A key under a lamp is the popular modern form; the [Ashliman page](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/hodja.html) does not include it. The "drunkard" version is not suitable, so use this one.
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition; avoid Idries Shah's wording.
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; cleverness, patience, thinking.
- **Cautions:** None. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Because there is much more…'" R: "Where did he lose the ring?" O: "What would you say to him?" W: "What was he looking for?" D: "Where do you look first when you lose something?"
- **Sources:** [Streetlight effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streetlight_effect).

#### B. Arab world, Turkey, Egypt and Jewish tradition

**11. The Fisherman and the Jinni — *One Thousand and One Nights*. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** An old, poor fisherman keeps a rule: he casts his net only four times a day. His first haul brings up the carcass of a donkey, his second a basket of rubbish, his third stones. His fourth brings up a sealed copper jar. He opens it to see what might be sold, and smoke pours out and becomes a terrifying jinni, who tells him he has sat in the jar so long that he swore to kill whoever freed him. The fisherman asks only one favour before he dies. "I cannot believe anyone so huge fitted inside this little jar. Show me." The vain jinni turns to smoke and goes back in. Quick as a blink, the fisherman slams on the lid and tells him what he thinks of such gratitude.
- **Variants:** The longer tale continues with nested stories and enchanted fish. Stop at the jar for children, or add the jinni's later promise to reward him. Similar genie tricks appear in Grimm ("The Spirit in the Bottle").
- **Copyright:** Public domain; [Lang's *Arabian Nights*, Gutenberg 128](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/128) is the text I read. Note that Lang says "genius" for jinni.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; cleverness, courage.
- **Cautions:** Jinn are part of Islamic belief for many Muslims, so don't portray them as a joke about faith. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** "I will be cross for a thousand years" can stand in for the death threat for ages under 6. Omit the frame tale (Shahrazad under threat).
- **Prompts:** C: "'Show me you can fit inside the…'" R: "What came up in the first net?" O: "What is it like inside the jar?" W: "Why did the jinni go back in?" D: "When did thinking help you more than being strong?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 128](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/128); [The Fisherman and the Jinni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fisherman_and_the_Jinni).

**12. Sindbad's First Voyage (the Whale Island) — *Nights*, Baghdad and Basra. Attribution: high. [T, opening and island section read]**
- **Plot:** Young Sindbad of Baghdad wastes his inheritance and, remembering that it is miserable to be old and poor, sells what is left and joins merchants sailing from Basra. One day the wind drops beside a small, low green island. The sailors go ashore, walk about and light a fire to cook. The ground begins to tremble. From the ship the captain shouts: it is no island but a whale, asleep on the sea, and the fire has woken it. Some reach the boat. The whale dives, and Sindbad is left clinging to a piece of firewood as the ship sails on without him. He floats all night and at dawn is washed against a real island, climbs up by tree roots, and in time finds his way back with his goods, wiser and glad to be home.
- **Variants:** Six more voyages follow; the later ones involve man-eaters and burial alive and should be left out. Whale-island stories occur in many traditions.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; Gutenberg 128.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; adventure, bravery, patience.
- **Cautions:** Orientalist framing in Victorian English; don't copy it. Only the first voyage is suggested. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** No one is shown drowning.
- **Prompts:** C: "'That is no island, it is a…!'" R: "What woke the whale?" O: "What can you hear on the water at night?" W: "What did he hold on to?" D: "Tell me about a time you were glad to get home."
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 128](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/128); [Sinbad the Sailor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinbad_the_Sailor).

**13. The Enchanted (Ebony) Horse — *Nights*, in Lang set in Persia at the New Year feast. Attribution: high for the tale's presence in *Nights* traditions. [T, opening and ending read; middle from memory, confirm in the text]**
- **Plot:** At the New Year feast in Shiraz, a stranger brings the King of Persia a horse made by art, which flies when a peg is turned, and asks a high price. The prince tries it, shoots into the sky and does not know how to come down. Searching the horse's neck, he finds a second peg, and lands by night on the roof of a palace in Bengal, where he meets the princess. They grow fond of each other, and she agrees to fly home with him. But the horse's maker steals her away, and she must rescue herself by her own wits, feigning illness to hold off a new suitor, until the prince comes disguised as a doctor. Together they ride home on the horse. They are married, and the King of Bengal gives his blessing.
- **Variants:** Named "The Ebony Horse" in Arabic editions; Chaucer's *Squire's Tale* is related. Aladdin and Ali Baba are "orphan tales" added by Galland from a Syrian storyteller, without an Arabic source ([Aladdin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aladdin)). I do not recommend Ali Baba for this app because of its killings and boiling oil.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; Gutenberg 128.
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; cleverness, bravery, invention.
- **Cautions:** The horse-maker is called "Indian" and is drawn as a villain, an ethnic stereotype to remove. Abduction and feigned madness also need softening. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Make the maker simply a greedy inventor, and make the princess the main problem-solver. Use "pretended to be too ill to marry".
- **Prompts:** C: "'Turn the peg and the horse will…'" R: "How did he learn to come down?" O: "Where would you fly?" W: "Who rescued whom?" D: "Who do you know that fixes things?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 128](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/128).

**14. Juha's Nail (مسمار جحا) — Arab world (Palestinian and wider Levantine use), proverb. Attribution: medium; the only page I could open is unsourced and carries maintenance tags. [S, weak]**
- **Plot:** A man has a house he must sell. The buyer is pleased with the price and agrees to everything, including one small condition that makes him smile: the sale covers the whole house except for one single nail in the wall, which stays the old owner's, and he may come and see it whenever he likes. At first the buyer thinks nothing of it. But the seller turns up each morning to look at his nail, and again each evening. He times his visits so that he arrives just as the family sits down to eat, and of course they must invite him to join them. The family grows more and more annoyed, until at last the buyer can bear no more. He leaves, and the house is the old owner's again. The tale has become a saying: "Juha's nail" is any small excuse used to get what you want.
- **Variants:** Told with Juha as the trickster; related cycles are Goha (Egypt) and Nasreddin. The page I read is thin, so look for a sourced Arabic collection before finalising.
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition.
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; cleverness, fairness, manners.
- **Cautions:** Politicised use of the proverb exists in the region, so keep it domestic. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** End with neighbours laughing, not eviction: Juha returns the money, or the buyer outwits him by moving the nail.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Everything except one little…'" R: "What did he keep?" O: "What would you have said at the door?" W: "When did he always come?" D: "Have you ever used a small excuse to get something?"
- **Sources:** [مسمار جحا, Arabic Wikipedia](https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/مسمار_جحا); [Goha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goha).

**15. The Man, the Boy and the Donkey — Aesop; Turkish forms; told of Juha, Goha and Nasreddin (ATU 1215). Attribution: high for the type. [S, contents page read; plot from the standard Aesop version]**
- **Plot:** A man and his son are walking to market, leading their donkey. Someone laughs: how silly to walk when you have a donkey! So the man puts his son on its back. Further on, people grumble that a healthy boy rides while his old father walks. They change places. Then the next people say what a selfish man, making his little boy walk in the sun. So both climb on. Then come people who scold them for crushing the poor animal. At last the two of them tie its legs to a pole and carry it between them, and everyone in the town falls about laughing. In Aesop the donkey kicks loose and falls in the river. The lesson: try to please everybody, and you will please nobody.
- **Variants:** Ashliman's set includes Turkish "It Is Difficult to Please Everyone", an English chapbook, a Swiss-German form and La Fontaine.
- **Copyright:** Public domain.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; thinking for yourself, kindness to animals.
- **Cautions:** The donkey's drowning is easily dropped. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** The donkey wriggles free and trots home, with father and son laughing behind it.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Try to please everyone and you please…'" R: "Who rode first?" O: "What would you do with the donkey?" W: "What did the last people say?" D: "When did someone's opinion make you change your mind?"
- **Sources:** [Ashliman, Type 1215](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type1215.html).

**16. The Shipwrecked Sailor — Ancient Egypt, Middle Kingdom; P. Leningrad 1115. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** An anxious official returns from a failed mission and fears facing the king. His attendant cheers him with a story from his own life. He sailed for the king's mines on a great ship with a brave crew, and a storm sank it. Only he survived, washed up on an island with plenty of food. He ate and made an offering to the gods. Then a giant serpent came and asked who had brought him there. Frightened, he told his tale. The serpent was gentle. It told him that it was the last of its family, that a star had fallen and destroyed them, and that he would be home in four months. A ship came just as promised. The serpent, Lord of Punt, loaded him with gifts and asked only to be spoken well of. The island sank from sight behind him.
- **Variants:** Interpretations vary. The serpent's lost family gives it surprising pathos. The ending warns against false confidence.
- **Copyright:** Ancient text, public domain; modern translations (Lichtheim, Simpson) are not, so paraphrase from facts.
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; bravery, loss, kindness, hope.
- **Cautions:** Egyptian religious context is historical; the source's "serfs" as reward should be left out. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Keep the serpent's sadness but state it briefly: "There were others like me once."
- **Prompts:** C: "'In four months a ship will…'" R: "What did he find to eat on the island?" O: "How would the serpent look to you?" W: "Who told this story, and why?" D: "What helps you when you are worried about something tomorrow?"
- **Sources:** [Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tale_of_the_Shipwrecked_Sailor).

**17. The Wise Men of Chelm Catch the Moon — Ashkenazi Jewish (Yiddish), Chelm, Poland. Attribution: high for the cycle; the moon episode is noted as widespread, with an early Jewish version attributed to Dik's *The Wisdom of a Certain Town*. [S]**

One night someone notices that their town is short of light, and the moon is far too useful to let it wander off each morning. The elders have an idea. On the next dark night the whole town comes to see the moon let out. They lift the lid and peer inside. No moon. The elders stroke their beards and think hard. (The article confirms the barrel-and-reflection episode; the closing joke is the common oral form, so confirm it in a Yiddish source.)

- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8; bedtime calm, humour, thinking.
- **Cautions:** Gentle humour within a community about itself. An outsider should write warmly and not use it to mock Jews as foolish. Stance: CARE.
- **Softening:** None needed. Excellent for a window-side bedtime.
- **Prompts:** C: "'We have caught the…!'" R: "What did they put the moon in?" O: "What would you do with a moon in a barrel?" W: "Where did the moon go?" D: "Can you see the moon from our window tonight?"
- **Sources:** [Wise Men of Chelm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wise_Men_of_Chelm).

**18. The Ring-Dove and Her Friends (الحمامة المطوقة) — *Kalila wa Dimna* (Ibn al-Muqaffa, 8th c.), from the Panchatantra. Attribution: high; the chapter is confirmed by title in the article I read. [S; plot from memory of the standard version, confirm in Ryder or Keith-Falconer]**
- **Plot:** A hunter spreads a net and scatters grain, and a flock of doves led by the ring-dove come down and are caught. They struggle separately and get nowhere, until she calls out: stop pulling apart, let us all rise together. They lift the net as one and fly with it, with the hunter running behind them until he gives up. She leads them to her friend the mouse, who gnaws the ropes, though she insists he free the others first and her last. A crow who sees it wants to be friends too, and despite the mouse's doubts they become friends. Together with a tortoise and a deer they form a small band of companions who help each other: when a hunter catches the deer or the tortoise, each friend uses their own skill to set them free.

- **Copyright:** Public domain originals; Ryder's 1925 English is out of US copyright but check elsewhere.
- **Age/themes:** 2–3 (the net section alone), 4–5, 6–8; teamwork, friendship, leadership.
- **Cautions:** None significant. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed. The first half alone works as a 150-word tale.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Let us all rise…'" R: "Who chewed the ropes?" O: "What would each friend be good at in our family?" W: "Why did she ask to be freed last?" D: "When did you need friends to do something hard?"
- **Sources:** [Kalila and Dimna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalila_and_Dimna); [Panchatantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panchatantra).

**19. The Lion and the Hare (Panchatantra) — India; versions from Pakistan, Tibet, Malaya, African American. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** A fierce lion, Bhāsuraka, kills without limit, so the animals of the forest make a bargain: one creature will come each day, if he will stop hunting them all. When the turn falls to a small hare, he takes his time and arrives late. The lion roars at him. The hare bows and says there was another lion on the road who stopped him and claimed to be the real king of the forest. Furious, the lion demands to be taken to this rival. The hare leads him to a deep well and says: he lives down there. The lion looks in and sees a great lion looking back. He roars, and a roar comes up. In rage he jumps in at his enemy, and the forest is free.
- **Variants:** Ashliman lists 12 forms: Bidpai; Santal; "The Tiger and the Shadow" (Malaya); "The Tiger and the Hare" and "The Tiger and the Fox" (Pakistan); O'Connor's Tibetan "The Hare and the Lions", where the lion does drown and the lioness is trapped in a hole; "Brother Rabbit Conquers Brother Lion" (Harris).
- **Copyright:** Public domain.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; cleverness, bravery, small beats big.
- **Cautions:** Lion dies; daily sacrifice. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** "Splash! He could not climb out for a long, long time, and when he did he had learned his lesson and went far away." Change "one a day to be eaten" to "one a day to serve him" only for under-5s.
- **Prompts:** C: "'He roared, and up came a…'" R: "Why was the hare late?" O: "What does the lion see?" W: "Who was in the well?" D: "When were you small but clever?"
- **Sources:** [Ashliman, Type 92](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type0092.html); [O'Connor, Folk Tales from Tibet, Gutenberg 75000](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/75000).

**20. The Monkey and the Crocodile (Sumsumāra Jātaka 208; Panchatantra IV) — India, with Swahili and Japanese descendants. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** A monkey lives in a fruit tree beside a river. A crocodile's wife wants to eat a monkey's heart. So the crocodile offers to carry the monkey across to an island where the fruit is ripe, since he cannot swim. Halfway across the crocodile begins to sink and admits the plan. The monkey keeps calm and says: what a pity you did not tell me earlier. We monkeys do not keep our hearts in our chests. Mine is hanging up in that fig tree. Take me back and I will fetch it. The crocodile, who is not clever, swims back. The monkey leaps to the highest branch and calls down that a heart does not hang on trees, and that the crocodile may be big but is not bright.
- **Variants:** In Lang's Swahili "The Heart of a Monkey" a shark needs a heart for a sick sultan (record 62). In Ozaki's Japanese "The Jelly Fish and the Monkey" the Dragon King's messenger is a jellyfish, whose bones are beaten out as punishment, which explains its softness. Also in Filipino and Tibetan collections.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; [Babbitt, Jataka Tales, Gutenberg 62514](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/62514); [Ashliman Jataka set](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/jataka.html).
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; cleverness, staying calm, friendship.
- **Cautions:** Buddhist birth tale: the monkey is the future Buddha in the frame. Keep the frame or drop it, but do not caricature it. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed; the crocodile goes hungry only.
- **Prompts:** C: "'My heart is hanging up in the…'" R: "Why did the monkey climb on his back?" O: "What is the river like?" W: "Who wanted the heart?" D: "What helps you stay calm when you are scared?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 62514](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/62514); [Ashliman](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/jataka.html); [Ozaki, Gutenberg 4018](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4018).

**21. The Talkative Tortoise (Kacchapa Jātaka 215) — India. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** A tortoise lives in a pond that is drying up in the hot season, and two geese who are his friends offer to take him to their lovely lake in the mountains. How can he fly? They will each take the end of a stick in their beaks, and he will bite on the middle and hang on. There is one condition: he must not open his mouth, whatever he hears. Off they go over a town. The children and people look up and point and shout: look, two geese carrying a tortoise on a stick! He longs to answer back. What business is it of theirs? He opens his mouth to say so, and falls. In the frame, a wise minister uses this event to teach a king who never stops talking.

- **Cautions:** He dies in the source. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** He lands with a bump in soft mud by the pond, shell cracked in patterns he keeps ever after, and from then on he thinks before he speaks. Note that this is a softened ending.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Whatever you hear, do not open your…'" R: "What did he hold on to?" O: "What would you shout from up there?" W: "Why did he open his mouth?" D: "When is it hard for you to stay quiet?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 7128](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7128); [Ashliman](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/jataka.html).

**22. The Banyan Deer (Nigrodhamiga Jātaka 12) — India, Buddhist. Attribution: high. [T, opening read; preface confirms the outcome]**
- **Plot:** There were two herds of deer, one led by a golden deer called the King of the Banyan Deer and one by the King of the Monkey Deer. A human king loved hunting, and the townspeople, tired of leaving their work to go with him, fenced the deer into a park so that he could hunt there. So many were being hurt in the chases that the two deer kings made an arrangement: each day one deer would go willingly to the block, one day from each herd. One day the turn fell to a mother with a young fawn, in the Monkey Deer's herd. She asked to wait until her baby could manage without her, but her king refused. She went to the Banyan King. **23. The Hare in the Moon (Sasa Jātaka 316) — India, Buddhist; the moon-rabbit motif across East Asia. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** A monkey, an otter, a jackal and a hare are friends who decide to practise kindness on the day of the full moon. An old man comes asking for food. The monkey gathers fruit from the trees, the otter catches fish, and the jackal brings a lizard and a pot of curds. The hare knows only how to find grass, which a man cannot eat. So he asks the old man to light a fire, and offers his own body, jumping in. The flames do not burn him. The old man reveals that he is Sakra (Indra) in disguise, testing them. And so that no one will ever forget such kindness, he draws the hare's picture on the face of the moon, where you can still see it.
- **Variants:** Chinese, Japanese and Korean moon rabbits, and a Mexican one (record 86), share the image.
- **Copyright:** Public domain.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8; generosity, sharing, bedtime calm, nature.

- **Softening:** Keep it clear the fire was a test and the hare was safe the whole time: "the flames felt as cool as morning". Finish by looking for the hare in the real moon.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Look up, and you can see the hare in the…'" R: "What did the otter bring?" O: "What do you see on the moon?" W: "Who was the old man?" D: "What can you give when you think you have nothing?"

**24. The Four Harmonious Friends / They Who Honour Age (Tittira Jātaka 37) — India; beloved in Bhutan and Tibet as *Thuenpa Puen Zhi*. Attribution: high for the Jataka; I could not open a Bhutan page, so the elephant-monkey-hare-bird form is from memory. [T for the Jataka opening]**
- **Plot:** Beside a great banyan on the slopes of the Himalaya live a partridge, a monkey and an elephant, and they are not getting along. The elephant remembers the tree as a small bush he could walk over, its top reaching his belly. The monkey remembers when he could sit on the ground and nibble its topmost sprouts. The partridge remembers eating a fruit and dropping the very seed from which it grew. So the partridge is the eldest, and from then on the others honour the bird, and the three live in peace and order. In the Himalayan picture form, four friends stand on each other's backs to reach the fruit.
- **Variants:** The four-animal form adds a hare and stacks elephant, monkey, hare, bird. It is painted on walls in Bhutan and Tibetan homes.

- **Softening:** None needed. The tall stack of animals is a lovely picture to act out.
- **Prompts:** C: "'When I was small, this tree was…'" R: "Who was the oldest?" O: "Who stands on top if we make a tower?" W: "How did they find out?" D: "What does Grandma remember from when she was little?"

**26. The Lambikin — Punjab, North India; Steel, then Jacobs. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** A tiny lamb sets off to visit his granny, skipping on his tottery legs. A jackal says, "Lambikin! I'll eat you!" But Lambikin gives a little frisk and says: "To Granny's house I go, where I shall fatter grow. Then you can eat me so." The jackal thinks that sensible. Then a vulture, a tiger, a wolf, a dog and an eagle all stop him, and each is told the same rhyme. He reaches Granny's house and asks to be put in the corn bin and fattened. "Fallen into the fire, and so will you, on little Drumikin, tum-pa, tum-too!" They let him by, until the jackal knows that voice.

**27. Dhon Cholechā (धों च्वलेचा) — Newar, Kathmandu Valley, Nepal. Attribution: high; described as the best-known children's story in Newar society. [S]**

One day, up in the fig tree, she is tricked down by two Lakhey demons disguised as an old couple, who carry her to their home and set her to making flatbreads. **28. Andare (අන්දරේ) the Court Jester — Sinhala, Sri Lanka, set at the court of Keerthi Sri Rajasinghe. Attribution: medium; semi-legendary historical figure. [S]**

- **Softening:** Skip hurting the frog; the king tests his hearing instead.

**29. Momotarō (桃太郎, Peach Boy) — Japan. Attribution: high; Ozaki 1908. [T]**
- **Plot:** An old woman washing clothes at a stream sees a huge peach bob along and takes it home. When she and her husband cut it, out jumps a baby boy. Momotarō frees the captives and goes home to care for his parents in comfort.

- **Softening:** The oni give back what they took, and promise to stop. In Ozaki the chief is tied up and given to the monkey to guard, not killed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'One dumpling, and I will come with…'" R: "What came down the stream?" O: "What would your dumpling taste like?" W: "Who flew in first?" D: "Who would you share your lunch with?"

**30. Urashima Tarō (浦島太郎) — Japan, from early written sources. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** Young fisherman Urashima sees boys teasing a turtle on the beach, buys its freedom and sets it back in the sea. Later a great turtle comes to thank him and carries him down to the Dragon King's palace beneath the waves, where the princess Otohime welcomes him. Everything is beautiful, and the seasons pass in rooms that open on spring, summer, autumn and winter. After what seems like three years he remembers his mother and father and asks to go home. The princess gives him a box, and tells him never to open it if he hopes to come back. On the shore nothing looks right. No one knows his name, and an old man tells him of a fisherman lost three hundred years ago. In his grief he opens the box. White smoke drifts out, and he becomes very old.
- **Variants:** Ozaki ends with his death and a moral on disobedience. Some versions turn him into a crane or end at old age. The article traces the legend to 8th-century texts: the Tango *Fudoki*, *Nihon Shoki* and *Man'yōshū*.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; Gutenberg 4018; Chamberlain and Hearn too.

**31. Bunbuku Chagama (分福茶釜, The Lucky Tea-Kettle) — Japan, Morin-ji temple, Tanuki tale. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** At Morinji temple there is an old tea-kettle. One day as the priest hangs it over the fire, it sprouts the head and tail of a badger, called a tanuki in Japanese, and flies about the room. The priest and his novices manage to catch it, and the priest, glad to be rid of it, sells it to a tinker for twenty coppers. That night the kettle grows fur and walks round the tinker's pillow. **32. Kaguya-hime (かぐや姫, The Moon Princess) — Japan, *Taketori Monogatari*, 9th–10th c. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** An old bamboo cutter finds a shining stalk with a tiny girl inside, and he and his wife raise her as their own. Gold begins appearing in the bamboo he cuts, and the girl grows into a young woman of great beauty, named Kaguya. Five noblemen come to marry her, and she gives each an impossible quest, such as bringing a jewel from a dragon's neck or a robe of fire-rat fur. They all fail or cheat. The emperor loves her too, but she refuses him gently. Each night she looks at the moon and weeps. At last she tells her parents she belongs there and her people are coming. On the night of the full moon, a thousand soldiers guard the roof, but no one can move. A chariot comes down from the moon. She sips the Elixir of Life, makes the moon-people wait while she writes to the emperor, and leaves the rest of the elixir with her letter. They put a coat of wings on her and she rises. The emperor has the letter and elixir burnt on the top of Mount Fuji, and people say the smoke still rises there.
- **Variants:** Considered the oldest surviving Japanese narrative. Ozaki calls her Princess Moonlight. In the classical text the robe also takes her earthly memories (from memory, confirm).
- **Copyright:** Public domain; Gutenberg 4018.
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; family, loss, bedtime calm.
- **Cautions:** Adults' courtship can be simplified; "she was grown" is fine. The parting is sad, but gentle. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Reduce suitors to two or three funny quests; keep the moon.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Every night she looked up at the…'" R: "What was in the bamboo?" O: "What would you ask for from a suitor?" W: "Who burned the letter, and where?" D: "Can we look at the moon and send her a wave?"

**33. The Tongue-Cut Sparrow (舌切り雀) — Japan. Attribution: high. [T]**

At last he finds the sparrow's home, and the sparrow family serves him a feast and dances. On leaving he is offered a gift: a small box or a big heavy one. **43. Cao Chong Weighs the Elephant (曹冲称象) — China, Three Kingdoms-era anecdote. Attribution: high; a real child, 196–208. [S]**

**46. Sükhe's White Horse: Origin of the Morin Khuur — Mongolia. Attribution: medium; one of two origin legends on the article. Also known in Japan through a picture book by Ōtsuka and Akaba (from memory, confirm). [S]**

**47. Tấm Cám — Vietnam; related to the Tày-Nùng "Khao and Đăm". Attribution: high. [S, English and Vietnamese articles]**
- **Plot:** Tấm ("broken rice") is an orphan who lives with her stepmother and half-sister Cám ("rice bran"). Sent to catch shrimp with the promise of a red bib for whoever catches most, Tấm fills her basket, and Cám tricks her and steals it. She cries, and Bụt, a kindly Buddha-like figure, tells her to raise the one small goby left, calling it with a rhyme. The stepmother learns the rhyme, sends her away, and eats the fish. Bụt tells her to bury its bones in four jars under her bed. From them come fine clothes, shoes and a horse, and she goes to the festival. She loses a slipper, and the king, finding it, marries her. Her family kills her repeatedly, and she keeps coming back as a golden oriole, a tree, a loom, then inside a fruit, until she returns as herself.
- **Variants:** **Grim ending:** Tấm has Cám jump in a pit, pours boiling water on her, makes her body into sauce and sends it to the stepmother as a gift from Cám. The stepmother's own death after eating it is from my memory of Vietnamese versions, so confirm it. Many modern Vietnamese versions soften this.
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition.
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12 (end softened); perseverance, grief, justice.
- **Cautions:** Repeated murder and revenge. Vietnamese readers know the ending and may notice silent removal. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Honest shortening: stop when she is restored. Tell parents in a note: "In the old telling the ending is very harsh; this version ends when she comes home." Bụt is religious, so keep him dignified.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Bống bống bang bang, come up and eat…'" R: "What did she bury?" O: "What shapes would you come back as?" W: "Who helped her?" D: "How is this like Cinderella?"
- **Sources:** [The Story of Tấm and Cám](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Story_of_T%E1%BA%A5m_and_C%C3%A1m); [Tấm Cám, Vietnamese](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%E1%BA%A5m_C%C3%A1m).

**48. Lang Liêu and the First Bánh Chưng (bánh chưng, bánh giầy) — Vietnam; *Lĩnh Nam chích quái*, 14th c. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** An old Hùng king must choose an heir among his many sons. He says that at Tết the prince who brings the finest dish for the ancestors' altar will rule after him. **49. The Monkey and the Turtle (Si Pagong at si Matsing) — Philippines; Rizal 1889, Ilocano form in Cole 1916. Attribution: high. [T for Cole's Ilocano version; S for Rizal]**
- **Plot:** A hungry monkey meets a turtle, and they dig up some banana plants to grow their own. The monkey plants his high in a tree, thinking this is clever. They threaten him with a hatchet, and he laughs: I like that, look at the black scars on my shell. Then one says, let us throw him in the water. He cries and begs them not to. - **Softening:** Monkeys splash home wet and hungry. Turtle offers bananas next time they promise to share.

**51. Timun Mas (Golden Cucumber) — Java, Indonesia. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** Mbok Srini, a lonely widow at the edge of the jungle, longs for a child. A golden cucumber grows, and inside is a baby girl, Timun Mas, who grows up kind and hard-working. When Buta Ijo comes, Timun Mas runs. **52. Sang Thong (สังข์ทอง, Prince of the Golden Conch) — Thailand; Paññāsa Jātaka, Chiang Mai, 15th–16th c. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** Queen Chantathevi gives birth to a conch shell, and because of the second wife's jealous plot she and it are driven from the palace. An old couple take them in. **53. Nine-in-One, Grr! Grr! — Hmong, Laos, as told by Blia Xiong. Attribution: high for the book's Hmong source; one modern telling; I could not open a scholarly collection. [S, publisher page]**
- **Plot:** Long ago, Tiger is lonely and asks the great god Shao how many cubs she will have. He tells her she will have nine every year, as long as she remembers his words. - **Prompts:** C: "'Nine in one, grr…'" R: "Who did Tiger ask?" O: "What is your not-to-forget song?" W: "Who mixed up the words?" D: "How do we remember important things?"

**55. How Wisdom Spread Through the World (Anansi's Pot) — Fanti, Ghana; Barker and Sinclair 1917. Attribution: high. [T]**

**65. Sundiata Learns to Stand — Mande, Mali; Epic of Sundiata. Attribution: high; history-based oral epic. [S]**
- **Plot:** A hunter tells King Naré Maghan that if he marries an ugly woman she will bear him a son who will be a mighty king. "We'll soon see," the old queen thinks, and she says nothing, but goes to the bedroom and takes off the bedding. She puts three small peas on the bedstead, then lays twenty mattresses over them, and twenty feather beds on top of those. In the morning they ask how she slept. "Terribly! I hardly closed my eyes. Something hard in the bed made me black and blue." Only a real princess could be so sensitive. The prince marries her, and the three peas are put in the cabinet of curiosities, where they can still be seen.
- **Variants:** This translation uses three peas in a cabinet of curiosities; many use one pea in a museum.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; Gutenberg 1597.
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5; humour, bedtime calm.
- **Cautions:** "Sensitivity as breeding" is a joke; don't make it a value. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed. Very good for bed-pile games.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Twenty mattresses and twenty…'" R: "What was under the mattress?" O: "How would you sleep up there?" W: "Who put the pea there?" D: "What makes your bed cosy?"

**78. The Gigantic Turnip (Репка, Repka) — Russia; Afanasyev no. 89, 1863. Attribution: high; ATU 2044. [S]**
- **Plot:** Grandfather plants a turnip. "Grow, grow, little turnip, grow sweet! Grow, grow, little turnip, grow strong!" It grows and grows until it is enormous. Grandfather pulls, and pulls, but can't pull it up. So he calls Grandmother. She holds on to him, and he holds on to the turnip. They pull and they pull, but they can't pull it up. So Grandmother calls Granddaughter. Then Granddaughter calls the dog, and the dog calls the cat. All together, they pull and they pull, but still it won't come up. Then the cat calls the mouse. The mouse holds on to the cat, the cat to the dog, the dog to the granddaughter, the granddaughter to Grandmother, Grandmother to Grandfather, and Grandfather to the turnip. They pull and they pull, and up comes the turnip, and everyone falls over.
- **Variants:** Cast varies. Ukraine's "The Mitten" and Russia's "Teremok" are sibling cumulative tales, but I could not open a usable source for them (see Gaps).

**79. The Baba Yaga — Russia; Afanasyev via Ralston 1873. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** A cruel stepmother sends a girl to "your aunt, my sister", to ask for a needle and thread, but that sister is Baba Yaga. The girl goes first to her real aunt, who tells her what to do: tie a ribbon round the birch that would hit you, oil the doors that bang, throw rolls to the dogs, and give bacon to the cat. At the hut Baba Yaga sits her at the loom while the bath is heated. The girl gives the servant a handkerchief, and asks her to go slowly. She gives the cat bacon and asks how to get away. It gives her a comb and a towel, and sits at the loom in her place. She runs, past dogs, doors and tree, all of which let her by. Baba Yaga comes after her, flying in a mortar and sweeping her trail. The towel becomes a river. The comb becomes a forest. She gets home and tells her father everything.
- **Variants:** In Ralston the father then shoots his wife. "Vasilisa the Beautiful" (Afanasyev) has a mother's doll and a skull lantern and is scarier. Baba Yaga is both fearsome and helpful in the wider tradition.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; [Gutenberg 22373](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22373).
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; kindness, courage, fear.
- **Cautions:** She intends to eat the girl; stepmother stereotype; the father's shooting. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Baba Yaga plans to "keep her for ever", and the father simply takes his daughter away. Keep the key idea: those she treated well helped her, because Baba Yaga never treated them well.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Long as I've served you, you've never…'" R: "What did the dogs get?" O: "What would you throw down?" W: "What became a river?" D: "Who has been kind back to you?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 22373](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22373); [Baba Yaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baba_Yaga).

**80. The Twelve Months (O dvanácti měsíčkách) — Czech/Slovak; in Chodzko's collection (the Němcová credit and Czech title are from my memory, confirm). Attribution: high for the type. [T]**
- **Plot:** Widow's daughter Helen is spoiled, while her stepsister Marouckla does all the work and grows prettier for it. In January the stepmother and Helen send Marouckla to the mountain to find violets. She walks through the snow until she sees a fire with twelve figures seated round it, the Twelve Months, and great January (Setchene in this translation) on the high seat. She asks politely. January gives his place and his wand to March for a moment, and the snow melts and violets bloom. Next she is sent for strawberries, and June takes the seat, and then for red apples, and September takes it. Helen and her mother are greedy and rude. Helen goes herself, won't say why she's come, and January waves his staff and a storm of snow falls. Her mother goes to look for her. Neither returns. Marouckla keeps the small house and cow, and in time marries an honest farmer.
- **Variants:** Samuil Marshak's Soviet play is protected. Greek and Italian "Months" tales are related.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; [Gutenberg 25555](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/25555).

**81. The Half-Chick (Medio Pollito) — Spain; in Lang's *Green Fairy Book*, 1892. Attribution: high; Lang marks it "Spanish Tradition". [T]**
- **Plot:** A black Spanish hen has twelve fine chicks and one that looks cut in half: one leg, one wing, one eye. His mother calls him Medio Pollito. He's bored in the farmyard and sets off for Madrid to see the king, hopping along, hoppity-kick. A stream choked with weeds asks him to clear it. "I can't be bothered," he says, "I'm going to see the king." A dying fire in a wood begs him for a few sticks. "Too busy." The wind caught in a chestnut tree asks for help. "Too busy." In Madrid the king's cook sees him and pops him into the pot. "Water, help me!" cries the chick. "You wouldn't help me." "Fire, don't burn me!" "You wouldn't help me." The cook throws him out of the window, and the wind blows him up to the top of the steeple. There he stands to this day, as a weathercock.
- **Variants:** This is why weathercocks stand on one leg, in the tale's own logic.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; [Gutenberg 7277](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7277).
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; helping, consequences, repetition.
- **Cautions:** The source has the fire "left by gipsies", a word to drop. A chick with one leg, wing and eye: a child with a limb difference might hear him as "punished". Make his body neutral and his rudeness the issue. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** Cooking is comic. The wind's choice leaves him up high with a good view.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Hoppity-kick, hoppity-…'" R: "Who asked first?" O: "What can he see from up there?" W: "Why did nobody help him?" D: "Who did you help today?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 7277](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7277).

**82. Stone Soup (Nail Broth, Sopa de Pedra) — pan-European; France, Sweden, Portugal, Ireland, USA. Attribution: high for the type (ATU 1548). [S, index read]**
- **Plot:** A hungry traveller reaches a village at the end of a hard winter. He knocks at door after door, and each house says there's no food to spare. So he sits down in the square, takes out a big pot, fills it with water, lights a fire, and drops in a smooth stone. An old woman comes to look. "What are you making?" Then come a few potatoes, onions, a bit of salt and some barley. Soon the whole village stands round with bowls, and everyone agrees they've never tasted such soup, made from nothing but a stone. He leaves in the morning, and gives them the stone.

- **Softening:** None needed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'It would be even better with a little…'" R: "Who brought cabbage?" O: "What would you put in?" W: "What was in the pot first?" D: "What soup should we make together?"
- **Sources:** [Ashliman, Type 1548](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type1548.html); [Stone Soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_Soup).

**83. The Hare and the Tortoise — Aesop. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** A hare is making fun of a tortoise for being so slow. "Do you ever get anywhere at all?" he says, laughing. "Yes," says the tortoise, "and sooner than you think. To show how silly the idea was, he lies down beside the course to wait for the tortoise to catch up. The tortoise keeps going, slowly and steadily, past the place where the hare is lying. The hare sleeps on peacefully. When he finally wakes, the tortoise is near the finish line. He runs as fast as he can, but he can't catch him. The race is not always to the swift.

**84. The Lion and the Mouse — Aesop. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** A lion lies sleeping in the forest with his great head on his paws. A timid little mouse, frightened and hurrying, runs across his nose. Unable to get free, he fills the forest with his angry roars. The mouse knows that voice. She runs to him and finds him struggling. She runs to one of the great ropes and gnaws it until it parts, and soon the lion is free. "You laughed when I said I'd repay you," she says. "Now you see that even a mouse can help a lion."

**85. The North Wind and the Sun — Aesop. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** The North Wind and the Sun are quarrelling about which is stronger, and while they are at it with much heat and bluster, a traveller comes along the road wrapped in a cloak. "Let's agree," says the Sun, "that whoever can get that traveller's cloak off him is the stronger." "Very well," growls the North Wind, and at once sends a cold, howling blast. With the first gust the ends of the cloak whip about the man's body, but he wraps it tight around himself. The harder the wind blows, the tighter he holds on. Then the Sun comes out. His beams are gentle at first, and the traveller loosens the cloak. They grow warmer, and he takes off his cap and wipes his head. Then he is so hot that he pulls off his cloak and goes to rest in the shade of a tree. Gentleness and kind persuasion win where force and bluster fail.
- **Variants:** Commonly used in phonetics as a standard sample. The same moral appears in Persian and Arabic teaching tales.

- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; gentleness, nature, bedtime calm.
- **Cautions:** None. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'The harder he blew, the tighter…'" R: "Who won?" O: "How does the warm sun feel?" W: "What did the traveller do first?" D: "What works better on you: a push or a kind word?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 19994](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19994).

**86. The Rabbit in the Moon (Mexico, Aztec/Nahua tradition). Attribution: conflict between sources, flagged. [S]**
- **Plot (popular form):** The god Quetzalcoatl, walking the earth as a man, travels a long way and grows hungry and weary, with no food or water anywhere. A rabbit grazing nearby offers herself as food to save him. Moved by her generous offer, he lifts her up to the moon, so high that her shape is printed on it, and sets her back on the ground. He tells her she may be just a rabbit, but everyone will remember her: there is your image in light, for all people and all times. That is why, on a clear full-moon night, you can look up and still see the rabbit in the moon, with her long ears, and tell your children who she is and what she did for a hungry stranger.
- **Plot (older mythic form):** After the sun and moon are made at Teotihuacan, the gods are annoyed that Tecciztecatl, who hesitated at the sacrificial fire, shines too brightly, so they throw a rabbit at his face. Its mark dims him and remains on the moon.
- **Conflict:** Wikipedia's *Moon rabbit* gives the Quetzalcoatl version as "an Aztec legend". Its *Tecciztecatl* article, citing Miller and Taube and López Austin's *The Rabbit on the Face of the Moon*, has the gods throw the rabbit, with no Quetzalcoatl. I could not confirm the age of the Quetzalcoatl form. Label it "a story told in Mexico" and don't call it an ancient Aztec text unless a specialist confirms it.
- **Copyright:** Oral and modern retellings; López Austin's scholarship and picture books are protected.
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; generosity, bedtime calm, nature.
- **Cautions:** Nahua peoples are living communities. The Teotihuacan creation includes self-sacrifice, which is too heavy for young children. Stance: CARE.
- **Softening:** Use the rabbit-and-traveller form. Parallel with record 23 makes a pair.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Everyone will remember you, for there is your image in…'" R: "What did the rabbit offer?" O: "What do you see in the moon?" W: "Who lifted her?" D: "Let's go and find her tonight."
- **Sources:** [Moon rabbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_rabbit); [Tecciztecatl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tecciztecatl).

**87. Juan Bobo and the Pig — Puerto Rico. Attribution: high for the cycle. [S]**
- **Plot:** Juan Bobo lives with his mother and tries hard to do just what she says, and it always comes out wrong. One Sunday she has to go to church and will take the family's pig to market afterwards. She tells him: clean up the pig while I'm gone, so she'll fetch a good price. Juan Bobo considers. How can a pig look her best? So he scrubs her and then fetches his mother's Sunday things. He puts the dress on her, the shawl, the necklace, a bit of lipstick and the high heels, and sends her off down the road to church, very proud of himself. When his mother comes home, she finds him beaming and no pig. Other tales have him carrying a jar of honey to feed the flies, or getting a day's pay in the wrong way for each task.
- **Variants:** The article says tales came through Spanish oral tradition and picaresque influence, and notes parallels from India, China, Turkey and Africa; Mason and Espinosa's Puerto Rican folklore papers are the scholarly path (from memory). Details beyond dress-up-the-pig are from memory.
- **Copyright:** Oral; hundreds of modern books are protected.
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; humour, listening, forgiving mistakes.
- **Cautions:** "Bobo" means fool, so don't let him be mocked for disability. Stance: OPEN.
- **Softening:** None needed. His mother laughs in the end.
- **Prompts:** C: "'And on her head she wore…'" R: "What did Mama ask?" O: "What would you dress the pig in?" W: "Where did the pig go?" D: "When did you mix up what someone told you?"
- **Sources:** [Juan Bobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Bobo).

**88. Pérez y Martina (Cucarachita Martina) — Puerto Rico, Cuba, Spain; Pura Belpré 1932. Attribution: medium for the tale's plot; the article gives only "a love story between a cockroach and a mouse". [S; plot from memory, check Belpré]**
- **Plot:** Martina is a tidy little cockroach who sweeps her doorstep and finds a coin. What shall she buy? Sweets would be gone soon, and so she buys a ribbon or powder and sits on her step looking fine. Animals come by to propose: a cat, a dog, a rooster and others. She asks each how he will sing to her at night, and each answers with a noise so loud she refuses. Then Ratón Pérez, a small and polite mouse, sings softly, and she marries him. One day she leaves him to watch the soup, and he leans over to smell it and falls in. She is heartbroken when she finds him. In some tellings she sings a lament, and the whole neighbourhood takes it up.
- **Variants:** This is the same family as Khāle Suske (record 1). It's a chain tale of the "Little Ant Finds a Penny" type. - **Softening:** She fishes him out with a spoon and they laugh, or tell it straight for 6+.

- **Sources:** [Pura Belpré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pura_Belpr%C3%A9); [خاله سوسکه](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/خاله_سوسکه).

**89. How Night Came (from the Sea) — Brazil, Tupi tradition as retold by Eells 1917; earlier Couto de Magalhães, from memory. Attribution: medium; Eells's preface and tribal specifics should be checked. [T]**
- **Plot:** At the beginning of time there is no night. It is always day: no sunset, starlight or moonbeams, no night birds, night beasts or night flowers. The daughter of the Great Sea Serpent, who lives in the shadowy deep, marries a son of Man. In the land of daylight her eyes grow weary and her beauty fades. "Oh, if only night would come," she moans. Her husband asks what night is, and she tells him of the heavy shadows of her father's kingdom. He sends three servants to fetch some. The Serpent gives them a big sealed bag and warns them not to open it until they are with his daughter. On the way they hear strange sounds inside: all the night beasts, birds and insects singing. They bite through the wax to see. Night bursts out at once. They run through the dark, and when morning comes, they creep home and are turned into monkeys, whose lips still carry the mark of the wax.
- **Variants:** Other versions use a nut rather than a bag (from memory, confirm).
- **Copyright:** Eells was published 1917; [Gutenberg 24714](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/24714). In life+70 countries check her death date.

- **Softening:** Good as is, with the sounds of evening as the climax.
- **Prompts:** C: "'And out of the bag came the…'" R: "Who asked for night?" O: "What sounds do you hear at night?" W: "What did the helpers do?" D: "Why is night good? What do you like about it?"

**90. Brer Rabbit, the Tar-Baby and the Briar Patch — African American, US South; Harris 1880. Attribution: high for African American origin; Harris was a white journalist writing it down. [S; Harris's dialect passage read]**
- **Plot:** Brer Fox is tired of being fooled, so he makes a doll out of tar and sets it by the road. Along comes Brer Rabbit, very polite. "Good morning!" The Tar-Baby says nothing. "Are you deaf? If you don't say good morning, I'll hit you." He hits it and his paw sticks. He hits it with the other paw and that sticks too. He kicks, and butts, until he's stuck all over. Out comes Brer Fox, rolling on the ground with laughter. Now he has him. He'll roast him, hang him or drown him. "Do what you like," pleads Brer Rabbit, "only please don't throw me in that briar patch!" So of course Brer Fox throws him in, and away he goes, calling back: I was born and bred in the briar patch!
- **Variants:** Ashliman has several, including Biloxi and Shasta versions. Similar sticky-figure tricks appear in Anansi (record 54), Kancil (record 50) and Africa. Julius Lester and Virginia Hamilton have Black-authored retellings (from memory; protected).

Name the tale's Black storytellers as its source. Stance: CARE; ask a Black American reader's input. A safe way is "the sticky doll", and to frame it as a survival tale of the enslaved.
- **Softening:** Threats kept mild: "I'll throw you somewhere terrible."
- **Prompts:** C: "'Please, please, don't throw me in the…'" R: "Why did he get stuck?" O: "Who's laughing now?" W: "Where did he grow up?" D: "Why might a small person need to be tricky?"
- **Sources:** [Ashliman, Types 175 and 1310A](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type0175.html); [Gutenberg 2306](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2306); [Tar-Baby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar-Baby); [Br'er Rabbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%27er_Rabbit).

**92. Iktomi's Blanket — Dakota; Zitkála-Šá, *Old Indian Legends*, 1901. Attribution: high; a Yankton Dakota author writing her own people's stories. [T]**
- **Plot:** Hungry Iktomi the trickster runs to Inyan, the great old rock on the hillside, and begs: "Grandfather, pity me. Give me meat." He spreads his worn-out blanket over the stone's cold shoulders as an offering, and his prayer is heard. As he walks off he finds a freshly killed deer and is delighted, and begins to cut it up. Then cold wind brings on a storm. Rain falls and he has no blanket. He goes running back up the hill and takes his blanket off Inyan. "You don't need this, you've sat in rain all these years." Wrapped up, he comes back in the rain to his meat, but the deer has gone, and only dry rib bones are left. He doesn't feel sorry for taking back his gift. He only says, if I had just eaten first! His tears are selfish tears, and do not move the Giver.

- **Copyright:** Public domain; [Gutenberg 338](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/338). **Copyright status doesn't answer the question of cultural permission.**
- **Age/themes:** 6–8, 9–12; gratitude, generosity.
- **Cautions:** Inyan and the Great Spirit are sacred to Dakota people. Reese warns against treating Native traditional stories as mere folklore (see the Representation section below). Name the Dakota nation and credit Zitkála-Šá by name. Stance: ASK. Consider linking to her own text, or to Dakota-authored reading rather than writing a new retelling.
- **Softening:** Do not paraphrase away the religious context. If kept, keep it short and respectful.
- **Prompts:** C: "'And his tears were…'" R: "What did he give to the rock?" O: "How does the rock feel?" W: "Why did he take it back?" D: "When have you wanted a gift back?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 338](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/338).

**93. The First Fire — Cherokee; Mooney 1900, from Swimmer and John Ax. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** In the beginning the world is cold. The Thunders send lightning to put fire in a hollow sycamore on an island, and the animals can see the smoke, but cannot reach it across the water. They hold a council. Raven goes first, is scorched black and comes back. Little Screech-owl goes, and hot air nearly burns his eyes, which stay red. The Hooting Owl and Horned Owl go, and get white rings of ash round their eyes. The little black racer snake swims over and comes back scorched. The great blacksnake climbs the tree and falls in. No one else will go. Then Water Spider says she will. She spins a thread from her body, and weaves it into a small bowl, a tusti, and fixes it on her back. She crosses the water and through the grass, puts in one small coal, and brings it back. And we've had fire since.
- **Variants:** Mooney's notes give others: Possum and Buzzard try first (Wahnenauhi); Dragonfly helps push; a Creek version has Rabbit, and a Jicarilla one has Fox.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; [Mooney, Gutenberg 45634](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45634).
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; bravery, small and clever, nature.
- **Cautions:** Cherokee nations are living sovereign governments. Traditional stories may be religious: treat as such, name the nation and cite the tellers Swimmer and John Ax. I found no statement from the Cherokee Nation, EBCI or UKB on outsider retellings. Stance: ASK.
- **Softening:** None needed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'And the little water spider said, I will…'" R: "Who came back with red eyes?" O: "What's your plan for carrying fire?" W: "What did she carry it in?" D: "Who is small but brave in our family?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 45634](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45634).

**94. The Origin of Strawberries — Cherokee; Mooney 1900, from Ta'gwadihi'. Attribution: high. [T]**
- **Plot:** When the first man and his wife had lived together happily for some time, they began to quarrel, until the woman left and walked off toward the Sun land in the east. The man followed behind, alone and grieving, but she went on ahead and never looked back. The great Apportioner, the Sun, took pity on him, asked if he was still angry, and he said no. Would he like her back? He eagerly said yes. So the Sun made a patch of the finest ripe huckleberries spring up on the path in front of her, and she walked by. Then a clump of blackberries; she didn't notice them. Other fruits came, one, two, three, and then trees covered with red service berries; still she went on, until there before her was a patch of ripe strawberries, the first ever known. She stopped to gather a few, and as she picked them she turned her face to the west. The memory of her husband came back to her, and she started home.
- **Variants:** Mooney's teller says "So some good came from the quarrel, anyhow." The Swimmer version is more detailed.
- **Copyright:** Public domain; Gutenberg 45634.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; making up, nature, food.
- **Cautions:** As record 93. Stance: ASK. The Sun is sacred.
- **Softening:** None needed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'And then she saw the first…'" R: "Which fruit came first?" O: "What fruit would stop you?" W: "Who helped him?" D: "How do we make up after a quarrel?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 45634](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45634).

**95. Raven Steals the Light — Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian and others; Pacific Northwest Coast. Attribution: high for the region; specific clan versions differ. [S]**
- **Plot:** In the beginning the world has no light. Raven hears that light can be found in a far-off house and sets off to steal it. An old man there keeps it hidden. Raven turns himself into a speck of dirt or a pine needle and drops into the water that the daughter of the house is about to drink, and so she becomes pregnant, and he is born as her son. The old man dotes on his grandchild, who cries and cries for the boxes hanging on the wall, until at last he is given them to play with. One by one he lets out the stars, then the moon, then the sun. He turns back into a raven and flies off through the smoke hole, and the world has light.
- **Variants:** Bill Reid's sculpture *The Raven and the First Men* is a related Haida work. The Tahltan, Tlingit and other peoples tell their own forms, and Swanton recorded them in 1905–1909 (from memory). Details of the transformation are partly from memory.
- **Copyright:** Swanton's Bureau of American Ethnology bulletins are public domain as US government works.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8; cleverness, bedtime (where light comes from).
- **Cautions:** **High.** On the Northwest Coast, stories can belong to specific clans and houses as owned property (this is a point from memory; I could not open a source on Tlingit *at.óow*). Stance: ASK/NO unless a clan or nation-approved version is licensed. Point instead to books by Haida and Tlingit authors.
- **Softening:** N/A.
- **Prompts:** (If licensed) C: "'And out of the box came…'" R: "What did he want?" O: "What was the dark like?" W: "How did he escape?" D: "Who tells stories in our family, and whose are they to tell?"
- **Sources:** [Ravens in Native American mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravens_in_Native_American_mythology).

**96. The Marmot and the Raven — Arctic Alaska and related peoples; Bayliss 1922 (non-Inuit reteller). Attribution: medium; the people are not specified in the record I read. [T]**
- **Plot:** Sea birds perched on the rocks insult a raven as he flies by: you eat dead things, you black one! He flies away, hurt. He crosses the water to a mountain and thinks: if it's shameful to eat dead animals, I shall eat live ones. He stands right in front of a marmot's hole and, when the marmot comes home, refuses to move. "They called me a carrion eater, so now I'll eat you." The marmot stays calm. "Very well. But you are such a graceful dancer, I've always heard. Won't you dance once before you eat me? I'll sing for you." The raven shuts his eyes and hops about while the marmot sings. "Do shut your eyes and dance your best once more." As he does, the marmot darts between his legs and into the hole, and laughs at him from inside, with the tip of his nose out, and the raven flies off in a rage.
- **Variants:** Raven tales run across the Arctic and into Northeast Asia, where Kutkh tales are told (Ravens article).
- **Copyright:** Public domain in the US; [Gutenberg 24569](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/24569). Bayliss used "Eskimo", a term many Inuit and Yup'ik people reject.
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; cleverness, teasing, bravery.
- **Cautions:** Bayliss's sources are unclear to me; the book is a popular retelling. Stance: CARE: use a people-specific name only if confirmed, and prefer [Inhabit Media](https://inhabitmedia.com) and Inuit-authored collections. The site describes Louise Flaherty as its co-founder and co-owner; the page doesn't state its policy.
- **Softening:** None needed.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Close your eyes and dance once…'" R: "What did the sea birds call him?" O: "What's Marmot's song?" W: "How did Marmot get away?" D: "What do you do when you're called names?"
- **Sources:** [Gutenberg 24569](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/24569).

**97. STATUS RECORD: Nanabozho (Nanabush, Wenabozho) — Anishinaabe (Ojibwe, Odawa, Potawatomi and others). [S]**
- **What is known:** Nanabozho is a spirit figure in Anishinaabe *aadizookaan* (traditional storytelling), a trickster, shape-shifter and culture hero, including in the world's creation ([Nanabozho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanabozho)). Speck's 1915 *Myths and Folk-lore of the Timiskaming Algonquin and Timagami Ojibwa* has public-domain texts such as "Nenebuc, the transformer" ([Gutenberg 66509](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/66509)).
- **Community position found:** [Ojibwe.net](https://ojibwe.net/stories/), whose listed team includes Alphonse Pitawanakwat and Margaret Noodin, publishes its stories by season "to reflect what is important to know in the dark winter time of rest", and says the team has "been careful to not capture the tales that should remain oral", suggesting that readers ask elders for those. It offers no reuse terms and says "Copyright 2025". I did not find an Anishinaabe statement inviting outside retelling. A rule that such stories are told only in winter is widely stated, but I could not source it here.
- **Recommendation:** **No retelling by PlayNest unless an Anishinaabe author or nation licenses it.** Do not summarise a plot here for that purpose. If used, license or link. No prompts are offered.
- **Sources:** as above.

**98. STATUS RECORD: Coyote tales — many Native nations of Western North America. [S]**
- **What is known:** The article shows Coyote is not a single character. Among the Navajo, Coyote is the tutelary spirit of "Coyoteway", a curing ceremony; in California peoples' stories he can be a sacred creator as well as a comic trickster ([Coyote (mythology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyote_%28mythology%29)). The public-domain routes include Judson's *Myths and Legends of California* ([Gutenberg 2503](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2503)) and Linderman's *Indian Why Stories* ([Gutenberg 606](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/606)). Both are by non-Native compilers, and I would not use them as authority. *The Adventures of Old Man Coyote* ([Gutenberg 46952](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46952)), which I recall as Thornton Burgess's American animal fiction, isn't a Native source.
- **Recommendation:** **Do not write a generic "Native American Coyote" tale.** Per Reese, a retelling with no named nation is "pan-Indian" and flattens difference ([AICL](https://americanindiansinchildrensliterature.blogspot.com/)). Fine for parents to learn of through Native-authored reading.
- **Sources:** as above.

**99. STATUS RECORD: Sedna / Sanna / Nuliajuk — Inuit. Sensitive; not recommended. [S]**
- **What is known:** Sedna, Sanna or Nuliajuk is the mother of the sea and sea animals in Inuit religion. Many of her names are avoidance speech, used because her true name is treated with respect or fear, and "Sedna" is itself a form popularised outside Inuit languages. Main plots: a young woman refuses to marry and ends up with a dog or a bird. When her father rescues her by boat, a storm comes, and he throws her overboard and cuts off her fingers as she clings on, and these become seals and whales. She lives on the sea bed and controls the animals ([Sedna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedna_%28mythology%29)).
- **Why not:** It's a religious story within a living faith and hunting tradition. Its content (bestiality, parental mutilation) can't be softened honestly for ages 2–12 without changing it. **Stance: NO.** Do not retell. For 9–12, point to Inuit-authored work. [Inhabit Media](https://inhabitmedia.com), which I recall as an Inuit-owned Iqaluit publisher, lists *The First Peoples* by Maika Harper on its home page, but I did not check that title's content.
- **Softening:** Removing the fingers removes the myth. Do not do this.
- **Sources:** as above; [Kiviuq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiviuq) is a related Inuit epic figure with its own community-led projects.

**100. Māui Fishes Up the Land (Te Ika-a-Māui) — Māori, Aotearoa New Zealand; with Polynesian relatives. Attribution: high; many iwi versions. [S]**
- **Plot:** Māui-tikitiki-a-Taranga is the youngest of the brothers, and they won't take him fishing, so he hides in the bottom of the canoe. He has a fish-hook made from the jawbone of his ancestress Muri-ranga-whenua. Far out, they find him. He says they must go further, past the good spots. They give him no bait, so he strikes his own nose and smears the hook with blood. He lets down his line and it catches something huge, so that the canoe heels over. He pulls and chants, and up comes a great fish, which is the North Island, Te Ika-a-Māui. His brothers, against his word, start hacking pieces off it, which made the valleys and mountains. In some traditions the canoe is the South Island, Te Waka a Māui. (The hook, the jawbone and both island names are confirmed on the page; the hiding, the bait and the hacking are from my memory of Grey, so confirm them.)
- **Variants:** Tongan, Hawaiian, Samoan and other tellings; Westervelt 1910 collects a wide range.
- **Copyright:** Grey's *Polynesian Mythology* (1855) is public domain ([NZETC](https://nzetc.victoria.ac.nz/tm/scholarly/tei-GrePoly.html)); [Westervelt, Gutenberg 32601](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/32601). The *Ko Aotearoa Tēnei* (Wai 262) report addresses law and policy affecting Māori culture and identity, and is a good starting point on taonga works ([Waitangi Tribunal](https://waitangitribunal.govt.nz/en/)). I could not open the report's text.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8, 9–12; youngest child, bravery, place.
- **Cautions:** **Attitude to retelling:** I found no single Māori position; these are ancestral stories with iwi variants, and from memory they are widely taught in New Zealand schools, which suggests acceptance of school-style sharing but says nothing about commercial use. Disney's Moana shows the risks: a Māui "skin suit" costume was withdrawn after complaints about insensitivity and brownface ([Moana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moana_%282016_film%29)). Use te reo names accurately, don't "Hawaiianise" them. Stance: ASK. Have a Māori reviewer, and credit iwi.
- **Softening:** Leave out birth detail (he is thrown in the sea). "Bleeds his nose" is fine for most kids.
- **Prompts:** C: "'He pulled and he pulled, and up came…'" R: "Who hid in the canoe?" O: "What does the great fish look like?" W: "What was the hook made of?" D: "What's the story of the land where you live?"
- **Sources:** [Māui (Māori mythology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ui_%28M%C4%81ori_mythology%29); [Polynesian Mythology, NZETC](https://nzetc.victoria.ac.nz/tm/scholarly/tei-GrePoly.html).

**101. Māui Slows the Sun — Māori; Hawaiian on Haleakalā; Tongan, Samoan and others. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** Long ago the sun rushes across the sky, leaving too little daylight to work or eat in. Māui says he'll catch it and slow it down. His brothers laugh, but they go with him. Carrying the jawbone of Muri-ranga-whenua and a great quantity of rope, in some tellings of flax, or from his sister Hina's hair, they journey east for many nights until they find the pit where Tama-nui-te-rā, the sun, sleeps. They set ropes as a noose round the pit and hide behind a wall of clay. At dawn the sun climbs out and they pull. Māui beats him with the jawbone until he cries out, then lets him go. He has moved slowly since, so people have time for their work.
- **Variants:** Westervelt notes Hawaiian, Fijian, Samoan and other forms, including Native North American ones. Hawaiian tellings place it on Haleakalā (from memory, confirm).
- **Copyright:** As record 100.
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; bravery, cooperation, time, bedtime.
- **Cautions:** As record 100. The Sun's beating is softened in many schoolbooks. Do not tell the Māui death story (Hine-nui-te-pō). Stance: ASK.
- **Softening:** He ties the sun up and makes a deal instead of beating it: "half the time for you, half for us".
- **Prompts:** C: "'Slow down, slow down, Sun, so we can…'" R: "What did they use to catch him?" O: "What would you do with a longer day?" W: "Where does the sun sleep?" D: "What do we do before the sun goes down?"
- **Sources:** [Māui (Māori mythology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ui_%28M%C4%81ori_mythology%29); [Gutenberg 32601](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/32601).

**102. Sina and the Eel (Sina ma le Tuna) — Samoa; also Tonga, Fiji, Māori forms. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** On the island of Savai'i lives Sina, a girl whose beauty is known across the Pacific. The Tui Fiti, a king of Fiji who is much older than she, hears of her, and he turns himself into an eel to go and see her. When Sina looks in the village pool, she sees an eel staring up at her and is cross: you stare at me with eyes like a demon! But she soon finds it a sweet creature and keeps it as a pet. Years go by. The Tui Fiti grows old and his magic grows weak. He tells her who he is, and that he knew he had no chance of winning her. He asks her to plant his head in the ground when he dies. She does, and a coconut tree grows. On every coconut are the eel's two eyes and mouth, and when you drink, you are giving him a kiss.
- **Variants:** Other versions have a pursuing eel who frightens her, or are more sexual. The kiss detail is from memory, and may vary; the article confirms the planted head. Disney references a different version.
- **Copyright:** Oral tradition.
- **Age/themes:** 4–5, 6–8; love, loss, nature, origin of food.
- **Cautions:** Older king pursuing a girl: make him an old friend or a lonely old creature and remove courtship. Samoan and Polynesian tradition with variants among iwi and islands. Stance: CARE; seek a Samoan reader.
- **Softening:** Make it a tale of friendship: Sina and her eel friend, with a farewell gift.
- **Prompts:** C: "'And on the coconut you can see two…'" R: "Where did she keep him?" O: "What would you say to him?" W: "What grew?" D: "What gift can we leave for someone we'll miss?"
- **Sources:** [Sina and the Eel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sina_and_the_Eel).

**103. Qat Brings the Night — Banks Islands, Vanuatu; Codrington 1891. Attribution: high. [S]**
- **Plot:** In the beginning, the islands have no night, and Qat and his brothers live in constant daylight. His brothers grow tired of it, with no rest and no way to stop. So Qat goes off in search of night, in the fuller telling sailing to Vava in the Torres Islands to get it, from a person named Night, who gives or sells it to him along with birds and sleep. He returns and teaches his brothers what to do: they lay down, and darkness creeps in. "What is this?" they ask, frightened by it. "It's night. Lie still, and close your eyes." They sleep. When they've slept enough, Qat takes a piece of sharp red obsidian and slices through the night, and a little red light shows, and that is the dawn. Everyone wakes up refreshed.
- **Variants:** Connected with Tagaro elsewhere in Vanuatu. The trip to buy night is from my memory of Codrington's *The Melanesians*; the article confirms that Qat made night, taught sleep and cut the dawn with obsidian. Pairs well with How Night Came (record 89).
- **Copyright:** Codrington is public domain; the Banks Islands peoples are living communities.
- **Age/themes:** 2–3, 4–5, 6–8; bedtime calm, siblings, nature.
- **Cautions:** The article says Qat was not formally worshipped but is central in local tradition. Name the islands. A secret-society connection is noted in linguistic work, so leave it out. Stance: CARE.
- **Softening:** None needed. The most bedtime-ready tale in the Pacific part of this pack.
- **Prompts:** C: "'Lie still, and close your…'" R: "What did Qat use to cut the night?" O: "What was the first night like?" W: "Why did they want night?" D: "What colour was the sky when you woke up?"
- **Sources:** [Qat (deity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qat_%28deity%29).

**104. STATUS RECORD: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Dreaming stories — Australia. Not free to retell. [S]**
- **Why not free:**
  - Dreaming narratives are not "folk tales" in the European sense. They hold sacred sites, law and custom, and ceremonies re-create those events ([The Dreaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dreaming)).
  - In Australia the concept of Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) holds that Indigenous cultural knowledge, including stories, is collectively owned. The 1993 Julayinbul Statement says such rights are best determined from within customary law ([Indigenous intellectual property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_intellectual_property)).
  - Creative Australia requires its funded artists working with First Nations cultural heritage to follow the *Protocols for using First Nations Cultural and Intellectual Property in the Arts* (Terri Janke). Dr Janke notes Australian law doesn't yet prevent "alteration, distortion or misuse" of traditional cultural material, so the protocols, not copyright, do the work ([Creative Australia](https://creative.gov.au/investment-and-development/protocols-and-resources/protocols-for-using-first-nations-cultural-and-intellectual-property-in-the-arts)).
  - Many stories have restricted or gendered knowledge. The Wawalag sisters' version of the Rainbow Serpent involves secret knowledge ([Rainbow Serpent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow_Serpent)).
  - K. Langloh Parker's 1896 *Australian Legendary Tales* is public domain on [Gutenberg 3833](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3833), and that's the trap: legally free, culturally not, and collected by a settler.
- **Recommendation:** **Stance: NO for PlayNest's own retelling.** Do not rewrite Tiddalick, the Rainbow Serpent or any Dreaming story. Instead, link to or licence works from Aboriginal-owned publishers such as [Magabala Books](https://www.magabala.com), "Australia's leading First Nations publisher", which is Aboriginal owned and led and based in Broome. Have the app say who owns each story.
- **Sources:** as above. Creative Spirits, by Jens Korff, has a useful [overview](https://www.creativespirits.info/aboriginalculture/spirituality/what-is-the-dreamtime-or-the-dreaming) that I did not fully read.

### Inferences
- **Launch shortlist.** Across the pack, the best first dozen for a mixed-age audience, with good bedtime fit, low cultural risk and strong repetition, are: Kasa Jizō (36), Sweet Porridge (70), Why the Sun and Moon Live in the Sky (56), The Gigantic Turnip (78), Stone Soup (82), The Tiger and the Dried Persimmon (38), Monkey and Crocodile (20), How Wisdom Spread (55), The Wise Men of Chelm (17, with care), Zāl and the Simorgh (3), Bozak Zangoole-Pā (2), North Wind and the Sun (85). All of them can be told from memory in under 500 words.
- **Pairing tales across cultures helps with representation.** Good pairs: Khāle Suske and Pérez y Martina (1, 88); Monkey's heart across India, Swahili and Japan (20, 62); Hare in the Moon and Rabbit in the Moon (23, 86); How Night Came and Qat (89, 103); Cinderella in China and Vietnam (45, 47); kind and unkind girls (33, 74, 79, 27).
- **Disclose softening in-app.** Where softening reverses an ending (Lambikin, Unanana, Tortoise), add one sentence for parents: "In the old telling, the ending is harsher." This keeps the app honest, and a parent who knows the tale won't feel misled.
- **Copyright is the easy question.** The hard question is standing. For Native American, First Nations, Inuit, Māori, Aboriginal, San and griot material, budget for paid cultural review or licensing, or leave them out and link instead. Don't publish a Native story just because Gutenberg has it.
- **Avoid "world tales" flattening.** Label each tale with a people and a place, not a continent. Add a line to say the people are living now, since Teaching for Change warns that folk tales alone can mislead children about contemporary life ([Derman-Sparks](https://www.teachingforchange.org/guide-for-selecting-anti-bias-childrens-books)).
- **Language.** The Persian, Arabic, Vietnamese, Korean and Spanish sources suggest that local-language versions are not translations of the English; have a native speaker write those retellings from the facts, instead of translating the English.

## What does the evidence say about shared reading and oral storytelling?

### Takeaway
Shared reading has solid support, strongest for language. Parent-training meta-analyses find small to moderate effects, and these depend on dose. MRI findings are correlational and from small samples, so they should not be presented as proof of cause. Oral storytelling is plausible and low-cost, but direct head-to-head evidence is thin: I could confirm only one named study's citation, not its results.

### Cited Findings

#### Policy
- The AAP's 2024 policy statement (Klass, Miller-Fitzwater, High and colleagues; *Pediatrics* 154(6):e2024069090) says reading together strengthens relationships "at a critical time", stimulates brain circuitry and early attachment, and supports social-emotional, cognitive, language and literacy development. It urges pediatricians to encourage routines and "conversations around books and stories" from infancy, and to continue literacy promotion at least through kindergarten — [AAP policy statement; I read the abstract via its Crossref record](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2024-069090).
- The accompanying technical report (Klass, Mendelsohn, Hutton and colleagues; e2024069091) says reading "supports early brain development and the neural 'reading network'", improves school readiness, that "all families benefit from high-quality and diverse books" and from guidance on "interactions around books and stories" — [technical report](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2024-069091). Note that "stories" are named alongside books.

#### Size of effect
- **Frequency and later outcomes.** Bus, van IJzendoorn and Pellegrini's 1995 meta-analysis found d = .59 for parent-preschooler reading on language growth, emergent literacy and reading achievement, about 8% of variance, unrelated to socioeconomic status, with the effect shrinking once children read on their own — [Bus et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543065001001).
- **Parent training RCTs.** Dowdall et al. pooled 19 RCTs (n = 2,594, ages 1–6): expressive language d = 0.41, receptive d = 0.26, caregiver book-sharing competence d = 1.01. Low dosage had minimal impact, and child age and **caregiver education were unrelated to child outcome** — [Dowdall et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13225).
- **Interactive reading in classrooms.** Mol, Bus and de Jong reviewed 31 experiments (n = 2,049): moderate effect on oral language, 7% of variance in kindergarten alphabet knowledge, and "findings with experimenters were simply not replicable in a natural classroom setting" — [Mol et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654309332561).
- **Dialogic reading.** Whitehurst et al. 1988 is the founding study — [Whitehurst et al. 1988](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.24.4.552). The What Works Clearinghouse rated Dialogic Reading as having positive effects on oral language and no discernible effects on phonological processing, with 6 of 7 eligible studies meeting standards, last updated February 2007 — [WWC evidence snapshot](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/EvidenceSnapshot/135).
- **Mechanism.** Sénéchal and LeFevre's 5-year study (n = 168, middle and upper-middle class): exposure to books built vocabulary and listening comprehension, which predicted grade 3 reading, while parents teaching letters and words built early literacy skills — [Sénéchal & LeFevre 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00417). A later study (n = 110, English-speaking children schooled in French) confirmed the Home Literacy Model, and 76% of parents adjusted their teaching to how their child was reading — [Sénéchal & LeFevre 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12222).
- **Exposure in words.** Logan et al. estimate that one picture book a day gives 78,000 words a year and that children from literacy-rich homes hear 1.4 million more book words by kindergarten than children never read to. This is a modelled estimate from 60 books, not an observed outcome. About 25% of caregivers never read with their children, per national US data the paper cites — [Logan et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1097/DBP.0000000000000657).
- **Print exposure and metadata only.** Mol & Bus 2011 — [citation](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021890); Mol et al. 2008 — [citation](https://doi.org/10.1080/10409280701838603); Flack, Field and Horst 2018 — [citation](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000512); Noble et al. 2019 — [citation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2019.100290). I confirmed that these exist but no abstract was available, so see Gaps.

#### Book-gifting programmes
- **Meta-analysis.** de Bondt, Willenberg and Bus reviewed 44 studies of Bookstart (n = 11), Reach Out and Read (n = 18) and Imagination Library (n = 15): home literacy environment d = 0.31, literacy-related behaviour and skills d = 0.29 — [de Bondt et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654320922140).
- **Reach Out and Read RCTs.**
  - Golova 1999: 135 low-income Hispanic families randomised; read 3+ days a week, 66% vs 24%; reading a top-three favourite activity, 43% vs 13%; OR 10.1 (95% CI 4.0–25.6) — [Golova et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.103.5.993).
  - High 2000: 205 randomised, 153 reinterviewed; child-centred orientation up 40% vs 16%; 4.3 vs 3.8 days a week. **Vocabulary was higher only in the older toddlers (18–25 months), not at 13–17 months** — [High et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.105.S3.927).
  - Mendelsohn 2001 is quasi-experimental (n = 122) — [Mendelsohn et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.107.1.130).
- **Books need guidance.** ROR's own evidence page reports Jimenez et al. 2023 (440 Latino families): the book alone had no significant effect, and effects appeared only with guidance, especially modelling — [ROR evidence page](https://www.reachoutandread.org/why-we-matter/the-evidence/). This is an advocate's summary; I did not read the original.
- **Bookstart.** BookTrust runs it across England, Wales and Northern Ireland from birth to 5, and cites Hines and Brooks (2009) that the pack has most effect on families who did not often read with their child — [BookTrust](https://www.booktrust.org.uk/what-we-do/programmes-and-campaigns/bookstart/). The pilot started in Birmingham in 1992 with 300 babies, with Wade and Moore researching it — [Bookstart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookstart).
- **Imagination Library.** The foundation's own review says studies are mostly local and formative, the programme is "extremely popular" and "shows promise". This is promising, not proof — [Imagination Library research page](https://www.imaginationlibrary.com/news-resources/research/). Wikipedia reports about 3 million books a month as of 2021 — [article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolly_Parton%27s_Imagination_Library).

#### Global evidence, including low-literate and low-income settings
- Vally et al. randomised 91 carer-infant pairs in Khayelitsha, South Africa, to 8 weeks of book-sharing training or control. Trained carers reported greater gains in words understood and said, and infants showed substantially greater sustained attention — [Vally et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12352).
- Cluster and randomised trials in Brazil and rural Kenya exist — [Weisleder et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2017-0723); [Knauer et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2019.01.002). I confirmed these citations only.

#### Brain imaging (Hutton and colleagues)
- Hutton 2015 (fMRI, n = 19, ages 3–5): greater home reading exposure correlated with activation in the left parietal-temporal-occipital "hub" for meaning while listening to stories, controlling for income — [Hutton et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2015-0359).
- Hutton 2020 (DTI, n = 47, mean 54 months): the home reading score correlated with white matter measures in language and literacy tracts, strongest for book-reading quantity — [Hutton et al. 2020, Acta Paediatrica](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.15124).
- Hutton 2020 (DTI, n = 47): higher screen use scores were associated with lower white matter integrity. It is cross-sectional; the authors say findings "are correlational in nature and do not identify causality". Cognitive associations lost significance once household income was included, and the sample was mostly high-SES — [Hutton et al. 2020, JAMA Pediatrics, full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6830442/).
- A further study (audio, illustrated, animated story formats) and a study on reading quality exist — [Hutton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11682-018-9985-y); [Hutton 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpeds.2017.08.037). Citations only.

#### Oral storytelling
- Isbell, Sobol, Lindauer and Lowrance 2004 compares storytelling and story reading on oral language complexity and comprehension — [citation](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:ECEJ.0000048967.94189.a3). I confirmed the citation but could not read it (see Gaps).
- A review of parent interventions found that parent training in book-reading, conversation and writing all helped, "with the effects being specific to the targeted skill" — [Reese, Sparks & Leyva 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/1468798409356987). This supports conversation, including reminiscing, as a distinct route from reading.

### Inferences
- Claim in-app that "reading together helps language", and say that frequent, warm, interactive sessions matter most. Don't claim that a Story time session "builds the brain".
- Dosage matters (Dowdall), so design for a low-friction daily habit rather than single long sessions.
- ROR and Dowdall suggest that a book alone isn't enough, and that coached interaction is the active part. That's what the prompts are for, so a parent-facing one-line tip probably adds more than an extra story.
- Telling from memory should be presented as a good alternative, not as proven superior to reading.

### Gaps
- **Isbell 2004:** from memory, about 38 children aged 3–5 over 12 weeks, both groups improved, with different strengths in comprehension and language complexity. Don't cite this until the paper is opened.
- **Riley & Burrell:** from memory this is "Assessing children's oral storytelling in their first year of school", *International Journal of Early Years Education*, 2007. If so, it is about assessment rather than a comparison with reading, which differs from the brief. Not verified.
- **Flack et al. 2018, Mol & Bus 2011, Noble 2019:** abstracts not available via the route I had. The effect sizes should be taken from the papers.
- **"Read Aloud Project", "Wong 2024", "Lukens", "Kids Lit Ethical guidelines":** I could not identify these without search. "Lukens" may be Rebecca Lukens's *A Critical Handbook of Children's Literature*, a criticism textbook rather than a trial (from memory). The brief should supply full citations.
- The AAP full text returned 403, so its exact wording on e-books and screens is unverified. O'Hare and Connolly's Bookstart+ RCT in Northern Ireland, which I recall as finding limited effects, could not be verified.

## Does reading from a phone count, what are the downsides, and how can they be reduced?

### Takeaway
Yes, it counts as reading aloud, and the child still hears the language, but the research suggests that a screen can cost some interaction and warmth. **I found no study of a parent reading aloud from a phone while the child does not look at it, which is exactly PlayNest's use case**. So the mitigations below are inferences from adjacent research, and must be presented as such. Telling from memory with the phone away has the fewest known downsides.

### Cited Findings
- **Less talk with toddlers.** In a lab study of 37 parent-toddler pairs, parents made more dialogic comments with print (11.9) than enhanced (6.2) or basic e-books (8.3), and made far fewer format-related comments (1.9 vs 10.0 and 8.3). Toddlers said more and collaborated more with print. Note that it compared children looking at tablet books, not parents reading off-screen — [Munzer et al. 2019, Pediatrics](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2012). A companion paper on social reciprocity exists — [Munzer et al. 2019, JAMA Pediatrics](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2019.3480).
- **Conflicting result.** In 102 toddlers aged 17–26 months, electronic books brought *more* child comments, attention (about 91% vs 83%) and positive affect, with no difference in parent content talk, although parents pointed less. The e-book had auto-narration and animation, and reading took about twice as long — [Strouse & Ganea 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00677/full). So the "print is always better" reading is too simple.
- **Warmth and posture.** In 24 mother-child pairs aged 7–9, warmth was lower on screen (3.10 vs 3.57 out of 5) and declined when children read on screen. Children on a tablet tended to bend their heads down with the mother behind, while with paper they sat close, though posture wasn't formally coded. **Comprehension did not differ.** The tablet weighed about 1,020 g against 196 g for the paperback — [Yuill & Martin 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01951/full).
- **Meta-analyses.** Across 39 studies (n = 1,812, ages 1–8), digital books that were only digitised gave lower comprehension than paper. Adults' mediation with paper beat enhancements in books read alone, but story-congruent enhancements beat paper — [Furenes, Kucirkova & Bus 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654321998074). Across 43 studies (n = 2,147), technology-enhanced stories had a small overall benefit over traditional listening (comprehension g = 0.17, expressive vocabulary g = 0.20). Multimedia such as animation, music and sound helped, while interactive hotspots, games and dictionaries distracted, especially for disadvantaged children — [Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314566989). A qualitative review reaches similar conclusions — [Reich, Yau & Warschauer 2016](https://doi.org/10.1097/DBP.0000000000000335).
- **Parent phone absorption.** Observed in 55 caregivers at meals, absorption in devices was the key theme, and highly absorbed caregivers often responded more harshly to misbehaviour — [Radesky et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2013-3703). Parent-reported tech interruptions correlated with child behaviour problems in 170 families (correlational; direction unknown) — [McDaniel & Radesky 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12822). In a still-face study with phones (n = 50, ages 7–24 months), infants showed more negative affect when mothers switched to devices — [Myruski et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12610). An interruption study also exists — [Reed et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000292).
- **Light at bedtime.**
  - In 36 children aged 3–4.9, 1 hour of evening light across 5–5,000 lux suppressed melatonin by 69–99% (mean 85%), with no clear dose-response, and even the lowest band, 5–40 lux, gave about 77.5% — [Hartstein et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12780). This was room-style exposure for an hour, not a dim phone for a few minutes, so it doesn't measure the app's case, but it's a warning that young children are very sensitive.
  - In adults, evening use of light-emitting e-readers prolonged the time to fall asleep, delayed the body clock and suppressed melatonin — [Chang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112).

### Inferences
These are my design conclusions, not findings. Each should be labelled as sensible practice, not as a tested result:
1. **Story text only.** No animation, sound effects, hotspots or games while reading. Furenes and Takacs suggest that extras can distract, and the parent's voice supplies mediation.
2. **Make the phone the parent's script, not the child's screen.** Hold it low, or angled to the parent, with the child looking at the parent or at the ceiling or a picture. This addresses the Yuill-and-Martin posture problem, but it hasn't been tested.
3. **Bedtime mode.** Dark background, warm tint, lowest usable brightness, and no notifications. Do Not Disturb should be prompted before starting because of the phone-absorption findings. The light dose to the child from a phone facing away is probably small, but I found no measurement.
4. **"Learn it, then put it down" flow.** Preview the story during the day, show a 5-beat outline as a memory aid, and encourage telling it with the phone face down. Fits an oral tradition and removes both light and absorption.
5. **Prompts as a final screen, or printed on a card, not interleaved.** Mid-story phone checks may break eye contact. Interrupting with prompts should be optional and few.
6. **A short warm script for parents.** "Sit close, slow down, do the voices, follow your child's interest." Warmth and closeness are the things screen reading appeared to reduce.

### Gaps
- No direct study was found on a parent reading aloud from a phone with the child not viewing, on dim-phone light exposure to a child at reading distance, or on "hold the phone low". Because search was unavailable, absence is not established.
- Munzer's and Strouse's conflict is not resolved. App differences, age and auto-narration may explain it.

## What practical advice can PlayNest give parents, by age and situation?

### Takeaway
Keep advice short and warm: read or tell something most days; repeat favourites; talk in the language you love most; you don't need to read well to share a story; tell your own family's stories too. Age bands are a guide, and they are my synthesis of the evidence above.

### Cited Findings
- **Re-reading works.** Sixteen 3-year-olds heard the same stories or different stories containing the same new words; retention was 0.59 and 0.72 vs 0.31 and 0.19 (chance = 0.25) — [Horst, Parsons & Bryan 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00017/full). The sample was small.
- **Keep reading after they can read.** A study of 220 Western Australian children aged 6–12 supports continuing to read aloud after independent reading, with concern about how rarely it occurs — [Ledger & Merga 2018](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ajte/vol43/iss3/8/).
- **Home language is fine.** In a study of 33 low-income preschoolers whose home language was Hmong or Spanish, home reading in the primary language was at least as effective as English for learning English vocabulary, and family participation rose from 50% to 80% — [Roberts 2008](https://doi.org/10.1598/RRQ.43.2.1). Golova's bilingual books and guidance also had strong effects on reading behaviour — [Golova et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.103.5.993).
- **Low literacy is no barrier to book-sharing.** Dowdall found caregiver education was unrelated to outcome — [Dowdall et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13225); Vally's South African training worked in a deprived community — [Vally et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12352).
- **Wordless books** have been studied — [Chaparro-Moreno et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2017.03.001) (title: wordless books boost preschoolers' language production in shared reading); [Grolig et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2019.11.002) (cluster-randomised narrative dialogic reading). I confirmed these citations only.
- **Family stories.** The 20-item "Do You Know?" scale asks, for example, how your parents met, the source of your name, and lessons your parents learned. Fivush links knowing more family stories to higher self-esteem and competence and fewer behaviour problems, and stresses that the value is in the sharing, not the facts — [Fivush, Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-stories-our-lives/201611/the-do-you-know-20-questions-about-family-stories); [Duke, Lazarus & Fivush 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-3204.45.2.268). Merrill & Fivush review intergenerational narratives and identity — [Merrill & Fivush 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2016.03.001). The evidence is correlational. Family history knowledge may reflect a family's warmth rather than cause it.
- **Teaching skills is separate.** Letter-and-word teaching and shared reading feed different skills — [Sénéchal & LeFevre 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00417). For Story time, enjoyment and language are the goal, and school-style drilling is not needed.

### Inferences
- **Ages 2–3:** 2–5 minute tales with a refrain (Sweet Porridge, Turnip, Goats Gruff). Let the child finish lines. Repeat for days. Mostly Completion and simple Wh- prompts. Choose sturdy board or picture books with clear pictures.
- **Ages 4–5:** Tricksters and "why" tales. Recall and Wh- prompts, one Open-ended question. Let the child act it out. Retell it together the next day.
- **Ages 6–8:** Longer tales and mixed feelings (Heungbu, Twelve Months). Open-ended and Distancing prompts. Encourage them to tell it back to someone else. Compare two versions.
- **Ages 9–12:** Keep reading aloud. Choose tales with ideas (Conference of the Birds, Mulan, Emperor's New Clothes). Discuss who tells a story and who owns it. Interview a grandparent.
- **Choosing books:** something your child wants, in a language you speak well, with good pictures for the youngest, and a mix of mirrors and windows.
- **Re-reading:** say yes. Repetition is useful for learning and comfort, not a waste of time.
- **Bilingual:** tell in whichever language feels warmest. Keep the same story in two languages if you like. Don't translate on the fly if it spoils the flow.
- **Low literacy or not confident:** tell from pictures, or from memory, or use wordless books. Audio narration in the app can model the story for the parent to retell. Make sure the app never implies the parent's reading isn't good enough.
- **Own family stories:** offer a weekly prompt: "Tell your child how you got their name." Pairs well with the "Do You Know?" items, without testing the child.
- **Prompts:** two or three at most at bedtime. Skip them if a child is settling. Enjoyment comes first.

### Gaps
- I have no direct evidence on the best number of prompts or on prompts delivered by an app at bedtime. Dialogic reading research largely involves daytime reading with trained adults, so an interrupted bedtime story could backfire. This merits a small test with real families.

## What guidance should PlayNest follow on cultural representation and retelling?

### Takeaway
Treat each story as coming from a named people, with living members. Offer both mirrors and windows. For Native American and First Nations stories, Debbie Reese's work suggests treating traditional stories with the respect given to scripture, and being tribally specific; in practice, that means Native-authored or licensed work rather than our own retelling.

### Cited Findings
- **Bishop.** Rudine Sims Bishop is known as the "mother of" multicultural children's literature ([article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudine_Sims_Bishop)). Her 1990 essay "Mirrors, Windows, and Sliding Glass Doors" is hosted [here](https://scenicregional.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Mirrors-Windows-and-Sliding-Glass-Doors.pdf). I opened the file but couldn't extract the text. The idea is well known: children need books that reflect their own lives and that open onto others'. I am giving this from memory.
- **Reese.** On [*American Indians in Children's Literature*](https://americanindiansinchildrensliterature.blogspot.com/), Debbie Reese warns that books with no tribal specifics are "pan-Indian (which means they're all the same)". Her [Tips for Teachers](https://americanindiansinchildrensliterature.blogspot.com/p/tips-for-teachers-developing.html) tell teachers not to treat creation and traditional stories as folklore, writing prompts or art projects, since they have religious significance, and to respect them as people respect Bible stories. She also advises naming a specific nation, using the present tense, and using that nation's website as a primary source. Her [Best Books](https://americanindiansinchildrensliterature.blogspot.com/p/best-books.html) lists are selected by Native people, and note "religious or spiritual content that is problematic to the people whose ways are being used by writers".
- **Anti-bias checklist.** Louise Derman-Sparks's guide, adapted from the 1980 Council on Interracial Books for Children's "Ten Quick Ways", asks about accuracy, tokenism, stereotypes, loaded words like "savage" and "primitive", and the creator's background. It warns that folk tales alone can mislead young readers about contemporary life — [Teaching for Change](https://www.teachingforchange.org/guide-for-selecting-anti-bias-childrens-books).
- **Community publishing caution.** Ojibwe.net's team says it was "careful to not capture the tales that should remain oral" — [Ojibwe.net](https://ojibwe.net/stories/).
- **Australia.** Creative Australia requires its funded artists to follow the First Nations Cultural and Intellectual Property Protocols — [Creative Australia](https://creative.gov.au/investment-and-development/protocols-and-resources/protocols-for-using-first-nations-cultural-and-intellectual-property-in-the-arts). Terri Janke's [True Tracks](https://www.terrijanke.com.au/true-tracks) covers relationships, contracts, and a 10-point checklist.
- **International.** UNDRIP was adopted in 2007, with 144 in favour and 4 against (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, United States), and those four later reversed their position — [Indigenous intellectual property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_intellectual_property). WIPO's term "traditional cultural expressions" includes stories and legends (same source).
- **Commercial risk example.** Disney pulled a Māui "skin suit" costume after complaints — [Moana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moana_%282016_film%29).
- **Internal critique inside a culture.** Iranian scholars have debated gender violence in Khāle Suske, showing that a community's relationship with its own tales isn't uniform — [خاله سوسکه](https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/خاله_سوسکه).

### Inferences
A practical checklist for each PlayNest story, my proposal:
1. **Named people and place**, in the present tense. "Told by Dakota people", not "an old Indian legend".
2. **Source line:** the collection, teller if known, and collector, noting colonial collectors honestly.
3. **Status:** OPEN, CARE, ASK or NO, as a required field. ASK and NO don't ship without review or a licence.
4. **Paid reader from the culture** for all CARE and ASK stories. Also ask the reader for the local-language title and spelling.
5. **Softening note** shown to parents when an ending changes.
6. **Language audit:** remove "slave", "gipsy", "savage", "Eskimo", "Bushman", "Hottentot" and "Kaffir" unless used historically in notes. Check caste and racial descriptions (Sang Thong's mask, Enchanted Horse's maker).
7. **Balance:** don't let most non-Western tales be about tricksters and most Western tales be about kings. Show heroes, carers and thinkers from everywhere.
8. **Sacred check:** does the tale have a god in it, is it scripture, or is it told only in season or ceremony? If it is, then it goes to ASK or NO.
9. **Way to complain:** a contact link so a community member can ask to change or remove a story, and a real process for acting on it.
