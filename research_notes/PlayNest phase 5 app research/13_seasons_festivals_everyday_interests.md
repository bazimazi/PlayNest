## 1. Weather, climate, time of day and seasons

### Takeaway
Weather suggestions can be safe without live data if a parent's tap ("rainy", "stormy", "very hot", "smoky") both changes what is offered and **removes** what becomes unsafe (for example, no bath in a thunderstorm). Season should be a parent-correctable guess from month and a climate profile, because four seasons are simply wrong for places like Singapore.

### Cited Findings

#### Safety by weather type

**Very hot days and desert climates**
- A heat index at or above 90°F can be dangerous, especially for babies, young children and children in sports. Exercise is especially dangerous above a 105°F heat index. Plan heavy activity for cooler parts of the day and plan indoor alternatives for runs of hot days — [AAP](https://healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/Protecting-Children-from-Extreme-Heat-Information-for-Parents.aspx).
- Heat stroke is a medical emergency. Warning signs include unusual sleepiness or being hard to wake, confusion, loss of coordination, severe headache or fever, intense thirst or no urine for many hours, vomiting, and fast or deep breathing — [AAP](https://healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/Protecting-Children-from-Extreme-Heat-Information-for-Parents.aspx).
- Offer water before children ask. Breastfed infants should get extra breast milk and formula-fed infants extra formula; infants "should not be given water, especially in the first 6 months of life" — [AAP](https://healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/Protecting-Children-from-Extreme-Heat-Information-for-Parents.aspx).
- Daily water guidance is 1 litre at ages 5–8 and 1.5 litres at ages 9–12. Keep children indoors during the hottest part of the day (10am–3pm), and do not use ice-cold water to cool them — [Queensland Health](https://www.qld.gov.au/health/staying-healthy/environmental/heatsafe/caring-for-children).
- Never cover a pram with a dry wrap, because it restricts airflow; a wet, breathable cloth kept wet is acceptable. Do not leave a baby to sleep in a pram in hot weather. Do not point a fan at a baby. Sponge with lukewarm water, "never cold water" — [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/environment/beattheheat/Pages/babies-children-hot-weather.aspx). WHO also says not to drape a pram with dry cloth, and to use a damp thin cloth plus a portable fan — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health).
- Fan thresholds differ. AAP says not to point a fan at yourself above 90°F, while WHO advises limiting fan use above 40°C — [AAP](https://healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/Protecting-Children-from-Extreme-Heat-Information-for-Parents.aspx); [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health).
- Children can be harmed quickly in parked vehicles, so never leave them there even briefly — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health). NSW Health says this applies "no matter what the weather" — [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/environment/beattheheat/Pages/babies-children-hot-weather.aspx).
- Concrete, asphalt and dark plastics and rubbers hold heat and can burn, even when the air feels cool, so an adult should touch surfaces first. Water play helps cooling, but drowning happens in shallow water such as buckets, play tables and bowls. Check play areas for snakes, spiders and stagnant water — [NSW Education](https://education.nsw.gov.au/early-childhood-education/ecec-resource-library/hot-weather-risks-for-children).
- Cool places to plan for include a library, a mall or a community cooling centre. At home, close blinds and curtains; lower floors tend to be cooler — [AAP](https://healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/Protecting-Children-from-Extreme-Heat-Information-for-Parents.aspx).

**Snow, freezing and cold rooms**
- Consider keeping children indoors at a temperature or wind chill of −27°C or lower. Dress in removable layers; use mittens rather than gloves; for young children use a neck warmer instead of a scarf, remove drawstrings, and use mitten clips rather than strings. Babies need extra bundling because they cannot say when they are cold — [Canadian Paediatric Society](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/winter_safety).
- Reduce outdoor time at −15°C or colder (attributed to the CPS), and babies and toddlers are at greater risk — [City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/community-people/health-wellness-care/health-programs-advice/staying-healthy-in-cold-weather/cold-weather-plans-for-groups-organizations/).
- AAP's line is to avoid outdoor play below −15°F, which is close to the CPS figure (−15°F is about −26°C). AAP also advises several thin layers and changing out of wet clothes right away. Hypothermia is an emergency; soak frostbitten areas in warm (not hot) water and do not rub them — [AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Winter-Safety.aspx).
- Sledding: supervise under-12s, go feet-first or sitting up, and use steerable sleds rather than disks or inner tubes. Supervise snow forts, keep an exit opening, and do not let children use sharp tools — [AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Winter-Safety.aspx). Under-5s should never go down a hill alone; never sled near roads. An adult should confirm ice is at least 10 cm thick for skating alone or 20 cm for games, and moving water may not be safe — [CPS](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/winter_safety).
- Baby sleep in cold homes: sleep space 16–20°C, hats and outdoor clothes off indoors, add a thin layer rather than more bedding, and check the chest or back of the neck rather than hands — [Lullaby Trust](https://www.lullabytrust.org.uk/baby-safety/travel-and-weather/cold-weather/). NHS also gives 16–20°C — [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/reduce-the-risk-of-sudden-infant-death-syndrome/).

**Nordic "friluftsliv" and all-weather outdoor play**
- The Norwegian public right of access (*allemannsretten*) is old customary law, codified in 1957, with equivalents in Sweden and Finland; campfires in forest areas are not allowed 15 April–15 September unless they clearly cannot cause a fire — [Wikipedia, Freedom to roam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_to_roam).
- Norway's early-years framework reportedly calls for children to have good experiences of friluftsliv all year round, without further guidance. A UK setting describes routine checks for wet or cold children, spare clothes and tarps — [Nursery World](https://nurseryworld.co.uk/content/features/outdoors-all-out).
- At a kindergarten in Bodø, the director said there is no academic basis for a set temperature limit for outdoor naps, and staff weigh wind and how the child looks — [High North News](https://en.highnorthnews.com/arctic-living/nordic-children-sleep-outside-in-sub-zero-temperatures/1090236). A popular-press piece reports a Finnish researcher's −5°C finding — [Galaxus](https://www.galaxus.ch/en/page/where-babies-are-left-to-nap-outdoors-in-sub-zero-temperatures-31257). Treat this as cultural context, not guidance.
- "No bad weather" parenting coverage is mostly popular press — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/11/12/the-164-year-old-nordic-secret-to-raising-happy-and-resilient-kids-says-swedish-mom-and-parenting-expert.html).
- Forest School's six principles cover long-term delivery, natural setting, learner-centred process, holistic development, supported risk and qualified practitioners. The page has **no all-weather statement and no weather guidance** — [Forest School Association](https://forestschoolassociation.org/what-is-forest-school/).

**Thunderstorm**
- "Do NOT bathe, shower, wash dishes, or have any other contact with water during a thunderstorm." Do not use anything connected to an outlet, stay away from windows, doors and porches, and do not lie on concrete floors or lean on concrete walls — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/lightning/safety/index.html).
- Do not resume outdoor activity for at least 30 minutes after the storm — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/lightning/safety/index.html); [NWS](https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-indoors).

**Rain, monsoon, puddles and floods**
- Leptospirosis spreads through water or soil containing animal urine, especially after flooding or heavy rain, so avoid such water, cover cuts and wear waterproof boots — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/leptospirosis/about/index.html).
- Floodwater can sweep people away and can carry contamination, electrical hazards and mosquitoes — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/floods/safety/index.html).
- For dengue, "cover, empty and clean domestic water storage containers on a weekly basis" and remove objects that hold water; the mosquitoes bite in the daytime; repellents with DEET, picaridin or IR3535 are listed — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dengue-and-severe-dengue).

**Poor air quality (wildfire smoke, urban smog)**
- Children are at particular risk from smoke because they breathe more air for their size and are more active — [PEHSU, adapted from HealthyChildren.org, June 2023](https://nyscheck.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/PEHSU-WildfireSmoke_2026.pdf).
- Activity by US AQI, from the same sheet. **150–200:** minimise outdoor activity and stop athletics and PE; children with asthma stay indoors in clean air. **201–300:** avoid all outdoor physical activity. **301–500:** avoid going outdoors. These may apply at lower AQI for younger or sensitive children, or when an event lasts beyond a few days — [PEHSU](https://nyscheck.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/PEHSU-WildfireSmoke_2026.pdf). A state sheet cites EPA guidance of 15-minute outdoor intervals for young children once AQI exceeds 100 — [Montana DPHHS](https://prod-dphhs.mt.gov/assets/publichealth/airquality/youngchildren.pdf).
- Indoors: close openings, recirculate air, use MERV13 or HEPA filtration, and never use an ozone-generating filter. A well-fitted N95 protects a child over 2 better than a medical mask. Humidifiers and wet washcloths do not stop smoke — [PEHSU](https://nyscheck.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/PEHSU-WildfireSmoke_2026.pdf).
- Indoor air can become as unhealthy as outdoor air during smoke, so put a HEPA cleaner in the room where children spend most time — [Montana DPHHS](https://prod-dphhs.mt.gov/assets/publichealth/airquality/youngchildren.pdf). The AAP/PEHSU fact sheet centres on a "clean room" and avoiding activities that add indoor pollution — [Oregon State](https://health.oregonstate.edu/aspire/resources/wildfire-smoke-protecting-children).
- WHO calls air pollution "one of the greatest environmental risks to child health", and its fact sheet is aimed at policy — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ambient-%28outdoor%29-air-quality-and-health). About 93% of the world's children live where pollution exceeds WHO guidelines — [WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/air-pollution-and-child-health).
- WHO experts class personal measures (respirators, portable filters, avoiding places, limiting activity, exercising less in polluted places) as "the least desirable choice in the hierarchy of interventions", behind emission-cutting policy — [WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240000278).

**Power cuts**
- Use flashlights or battery lights rather than candles where possible; any candle goes in a sturdy holder, away from anything flammable, never unattended — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/psa-toolkit/proper-use-of-candles-during-a-power-outage.html). The Red Cross is stricter and says not to use candles at all during an outage — [Red Cross checklist](https://www.redcross.org/content/dam/redcross/get-help/power-outage/EN_Power-Outage-Safety-Checklist.pdf).
- Never run generators, grills or gas- or charcoal-burning devices inside the home or garage, or near doors, windows or vents; carbon monoxide is odourless and colourless — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/psa-toolkit/avoiding-carbon-monoxide-poisoning.html).

**Drought-friendly water play**
- Greywater usually contains traces of human waste and is not pathogen-free; advice cited there is to avoid direct contact, and stored greywater should not be reused after 24 hours — [Wikipedia, Greywater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greywater).

#### Time of day
- **After school:** "restraint collapse" is not a clinical term; it is a "description of a phenomenon" every parent has seen, according to Child Mind Institute psychologist Dave Anderson. He suggests watching frequency, intensity, duration and impairment, and seeking help if any seems off — [TODAY](https://www.today.com/today/amp/rcna220901).
- Counsellor Andrea Loewen Nair coined the term. Suggested responses are decompression first, snack, hugs or blanket wraps, calm music or quiet space, delaying problem questions, and staying calm; talk to the teacher and health provider if frequent. It is more common in children with ADHD or autism but can happen to any child — [Understood.org](https://understood.org/en/articles/restraint-collapse-why-kids-fall-apart-after-school).
- **Pre-bed routine:** in mothers of 10,085 children aged 0–5 across 13 regions, a consistent routine was associated with earlier bedtimes, shorter sleep latency, fewer wakings and longer sleep, with a dose-dependent pattern. This is correlational, parent-report data — [Mindell et al., Sleep 2015](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/article/MED/25325483?resultType=core&format=json).
- **Evening light:** about 1000 lux for one hour before bedtime suppressed melatonin by 87.6% in 10 preschoolers (mean age 4.3), and levels stayed suppressed 50 minutes afterwards. The sample is very small — [Akacem et al., 2018](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/article/MED/29504270?resultType=core&format=json).

#### Season dates and non-temperate climates
- Astronomical seasons follow solstices and equinoxes (about 21 March, 21 June, 22 September, 22 December) and vary in length. Meteorological seasons are three full calendar months: Northern spring is March–May, summer June–August, fall September–November, winter December–February. Southern seasons are reversed — [NOAA NCEI](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/meteorological-versus-astronomical-seasons).
- Tropical rainforest climates have no wet or dry season. Some tropical areas have **two** wet seasons because the Intertropical Convergence Zone passes twice a year (southern West Africa, northern Guyana). Northern Australia's wet season runs roughly December–March. Mediterranean regions are wet in winter — [Wikipedia, Wet season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_season).
- India's southwest monsoon normally reaches Kerala's coast in early June, covers the country by mid-July, and withdraws by early October. The northeast monsoon sets in around 20 October for about 50 days and brings winter rain to Tamil Nadu — [Wikipedia, Monsoon of South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsoon_of_South_Asia).
- Singapore: "there is no distinct wet or dry season", with named monsoon phases: Northeast (December–early March), inter-monsoon (late March–May), Southwest (June–September), inter-monsoon (October–November) — [Meteorological Service Singapore](https://www.weather.gov.sg/climate-climate-of-singapore/).

### Inferences

#### How to model season without being wrong
- **Ask for a climate profile once, in plain words, and let parents change it:** "Four seasons (north)", "Four seasons (south)", "Wet and dry", "Two rainy seasons", "Hot most of the year / desert", "Fairly even all year", "Let me pick". No location permission is needed.
- **Use meteorological months, not equinox dates,** for the four-season profiles. They are simple and match NOAA's three-month blocks.
- **For tropical and desert profiles, drop the seasons altogether.** Use a 12-month tap grid ("Which months are rainy? Which are very hot?"). Offer starting presets only where a source supports them, and always editable.
- **Never assert.** Say "Rainy lately?" rather than "It's monsoon season". Today's tap always overrides the month guess, and "Not today" is one tap.
- **Keep two separate fields:** `conditionToday` (rainy, stormy, very hot, snowy, windy, smoky, power cut, cold room) and `seasonGuess`. Conditions drive safety; season only drives flavour.
- **Build conditions as filters, not only as suggestions.** "Stormy" hides bath, sink and water play and anything needing a plugged-in device. "Smoky" hides outdoor ideas at the parent's chosen level, and candle or incense activities. "Power cut" hides real-flame ideas. "Very hot" hides midday outdoor ideas. This is the most valuable safety behaviour the feature can offer, and it needs no live data.
- **Bad-air wording:** the app has no AQI feed, so offer a two-step tap ("Smoky or smoggy today" then "Very bad / Bad") and link the AAP levels as text. It should not give a medical verdict. Note that WHO ranks personal measures last, so copy should avoid implying families can solve this alone.
- **Bad-air caution:** skipping candles and incense on smoky days is my inference from the "avoid activities that add indoor pollution" point; the specific list was not retrieved.

#### Weather activity bank (72 ideas, each with an honest one-line reason)

*Rain and monsoon*
- W1. **Window raindrop race** (18–72 m). Pick two drops and guess the winner. *"Turns stuck indoors into a guessing game."*
- W2. **Listen to the rain** (0–12 m). Hold baby by a covered door and say "pit-pat" as it changes. *"A new sound, your voice, no setup."*
- W3. **Sill rain gauge** (48–144 m). Straight jar, tape marks, a daily log. Empty it daily because of mosquitoes. *"Real measuring with real numbers."*
- W4. **Rain-painted paper** (30–96 m). Washable marker dots, 30 seconds outside, then look. *"The rain does the art."*
- W5. **Umbrella drum** (12–48 m). Stand under an umbrella and listen, then pat along. *"Rain gets a rhythm you can copy."*
- W6. **Paper-boat regatta** (48–144 m). Fold boats and race them in a tub, never in a drain or flooded street. *"Careful folding, with racing as the reward."*
- W7. **Mud-pie bakery** (18–72 m). After light rain, in a clean garden patch; wash hands afterwards. *"Messy pretend play that costs nothing."*
- W8. **Worm rescue** (24–84 m). Move worms off the path on a leaf. *"Gentle hands and a small good deed."*
- W9. **Tomorrow's forecast** (60–144 m). Predict from the sky and score yourselves. *"Guessing, checking, and being fine with wrong."*
- W10. **Indoor monsoon den** (12–72 m). Sheet over chairs and a rain-sound game with fingertips on the sheet. *"Cosy when you can't go out."*

*Puddles (rain puddles only, never floodwater)*
- W11. **Splash scale** (18–72 m). Small, medium and giant jumps. Cover cuts and wear boots. *"Gets the wiggles out, with sizes built in."*
- W12. **Chalk the shrinking puddle** (48–96 m). Draw round it each hour. *"You can watch water disappear."*
- W13. **Puddle mirror** (24–72 m). Find the sky, then ripple it. *"A quiet look after the loud jumping."*
- W14. **Boot-print trail** (18–60 m). Wet boots on dry ground, follow the trail and watch it fade. *"Footprints that vanish are oddly thrilling."*

*Thunderstorm (indoors, away from windows, no water, nothing plugged in)*
- W15. **Flash-to-boom count** (48–144 m). Count from flash to thunder from the middle of the room. Is the gap growing? *"Gives a nervous child a job."*
- W16. **Storm den** (12–72 m). Blanket den with a battery torch, away from windows. *"A small place feels safer in a big noise."*
- W17. **Lap thunderstorm** (18–60 m). Tap fingers, then pat knees, then a quiet fade. *"Makes the scary noise predictable."*
- W18. **Name it and hold on** (0–24 m). "Boom. That was loud. I've got you." *"Your calm is what they read."*
- W19. **Dry bath** (6–36 m). Swap the bath for a towel rub and your bath song. *"Keeps the routine without the water."*
- W20. **Pass-the-story storm** (48–144 m). One sentence each, in turns. *"Fills the wait with no plugs or screens."*

*Very hot days and desert climates (before 10am or after sundown; indoors at midday)*
- W21. **Ice-block rescue** (24–72 m). Big toys frozen in a large block; free them with spoons and warm water. Use a block too big to mouth and no small toys. *"Cool hands and a long, absorbing job."*
- W22. **Damp-cloth dab** (0–12 m). Lukewarm flannel on arms and legs, naming each part. *"Cools them down and makes it a chat."*
- W23. **Early-bird walk** (0–144 m). Go at first light and look for what's awake. *"The cool hours are the outdoor hours."*
- W24. **After-dark walk** (36–144 m). Torch, closed shoes, listening, stars. Stay on paths and watch for snakes and scorpions. *"Desert evenings are the real playground."*
- W25. **Curtain-closed cave** (18–72 m). Blinds down, a torch and whispered animal sounds. *"Keeps the room cool and makes that the game."*
- W26. **Cool-floor animals** (24–96 m). Slow stretches on tile. *"Moving without overheating."*
- W27. **Slow-motion Olympics** (36–144 m). Every event at quarter speed. *"Silly, and it can't work up a sweat."*
- W28. **Cheers!** (12–72 m). A sip and a clink after each song or page. Not for under-6-month-olds. *"Drinks before they're thirsty."*
- W29. **Coolest-spot detective** (72–144 m). A room thermometer and a map of the home. An adult tests surfaces. *"They find today's best place to play."*
- W30. **Shade map** (60–144 m). Chalk the shade at 8am, then again at 5pm. *"You can see the sun move."*
- W31. **Ice-melt race** (36–96 m). Three spots in the shade; guess the winner. *"A real experiment, in minutes."*
- W32. **Hand-fan workshop** (36–120 m). Fold, decorate, compare. *"A craft that actually helps."*
- W33. **Cool-place colour hunt** (24–120 m). At the library or mall, find five blues. *"An outing that isn't outside."*
- W34. **Before-you-touch test** (36–96 m). An adult checks the slide or the sand first, and the child asks "safe or hot?" *"A habit worth having where surfaces burn."*

*Snow and freezing*
- W35. **Snow paint** (24–72 m). Coloured water in squeeze bottles. *"Art at a scale paper can't manage."*
- W36. **Track detective** (36–144 m). Find prints and draw them. *"Careful looking, with a mystery."*
- W37. **Snow on a tray** (12–36 m). Bring snow to the kitchen floor, for days too cold to go out. *"All the wonder and none of the windchill."*
- W38. **Ice lantern** (48–144 m). Freeze water in a bucket and light it with an LED, not a flame. *"A beautiful thing that only exists in winter."*
- W39. **Snow fort with a door** (48–144 m). Adult nearby, an exit opening, no sharp tools. *"Big building, with the safety rule built in."*
- W40. **Layer-up race** (30–72 m). Practise layers indoors first. *"Dressing practice before the cold."*
- W41. **Frozen bubbles** (60–144 m). Blow bubbles well below freezing and watch. *"Something you have to go outside to see."*
- W42. **All-weather ten** (0–144 m). Ten minutes out, properly dressed, in any weather you've judged safe. *"Short and regular beats long and miserable."*
- W43. **Finger check-in** (24–72 m). High-fives every 15 minutes to feel for cold or wet. *"Teaches them to notice cold early."*
- W44. **Sled like a scientist** (60–144 m). Compare slopes and steering, feet first, on clear slopes. *"Thrill with a reason to slow down."*

*Wind (only when branches aren't moving hard; go in if it's strengthening)*
- W45. **Streamer run** (18–72 m). A short ribbon on a stick. *"You can see the invisible."*
- W46. **Wind-direction flag** (48–120 m). A strip on a stick, a compass, a week's log. *"A pattern they discover themselves."*
- W47. **Paper kite** (60–144 m). Paper, not plastic bags; an open space away from wires. *"Patience pays off, up in the air."*
- W48. **Drop test** (36–96 m). Leaf, feather, sock: which lands first? *"Prediction, with instant answers."*
- W49. **Whoosh watch** (0–18 m). Hold baby and watch trees, saying "whoosh". *"Moving things hold their attention."*
- W50. **Pinwheel** (48–96 m). Make it and test it at different spots. *"Build it, then find where it works."*

*Autumn leaves*
- W51. **Leaf rainbow** (24–72 m). Sort by colour along a path. *"Sorting without calling it maths."*
- W52. **Leaf-pile leap** (18–84 m). An adult checks for sticks first. *"Pure joy, and it needs a rake."*
- W53. **Leaf crown** (36–96 m). Thread the stalks onto a card band. *"Fingers work, and they wear the result."*
- W54. **Leaf rubbings** (48–120 m). Crayon on paper. *"The veins appear like magic."*
- W55. **Big-leaf crunch** (6–18 m). A large, clean leaf in the hand, watching the mouth. *"A new texture and a great noise."*
- W56. **Seed hunt** (48–120 m). Collect, compare, plant one. Not for eating, and keep away from under-3s. *"Shows where trees come from."*

*Spring planting*
- W57. **Bean in a jar** (36–120 m). Wet paper, so roots show. Adult handles seeds with under-3s nearby. *"You can watch what's normally hidden."*
- W58. **Sprout graph** (48–144 m). Measure weekly and draw the line. *"Real data they care about."*
- W59. **Soil tray** (9–30 m). Hands in a tray of clean soil, then a wash. *"Texture, and practice at 'not in mouth'."*
- W60. **One-branch photo** (36–120 m). The same bud, once a week. *"Slow change is hard to see otherwise."*

*Cold rooms*
- W61. **Blanket burrito** (12–60 m). Roll up, tell a short story, unroll. Face clear, and not for sleep. *"Warm, giggly and a good deep press."*
- W62. **Thaw the ice cube** (24–96 m). Stand frozen, then melt slowly into a dance. *"Warms the body before the room."*
- W63. **Reading nest** (6–96 m). Layers and a pile of books, awake and supervised. Babies sleep with no extra bedding. *"Warmth and closeness in one."*
- W64. **Star-jump countdown** (48–144 m). Ten, then nine, down to one. *"Two minutes and everyone's warm."*

*Bad-air days (indoors, windows shut)*
- W65. **Base camp** (0–144 m). The filtered or clean room becomes "camp" for the day. *"Makes the restriction feel like a plan."*
- W66. **Sock-ball basket** (24–120 m). Rolled socks into a laundry basket, moving back a step each round. *"Active play in a small room."*
- W67. **Animal-walk deck** (18–96 m). Draw a card, walk the animal. *"Burns energy without a run outside."*
- W68. **Tape-line course** (30–96 m). Balance, hop and tiptoe along floor tape. *"A course that peels off afterwards."*
- W69. **Air detective** (72–144 m). Draw how the filter works and why windows are shut. *"Worries shrink when they understand."*

*Power cuts (torches only, no real flames)*

## 2. Everyday routines as play

### Takeaway
Programmes from the NHS, Vroom, The Basics and CDC converge on one model: talk, follow the child's attention, and take turns during things that already happen. Evidence is real but uneven: strong for coaching and text nudges, promising for prompts in everyday places, and absent for routine-play tips evaluated on their own.

### Cited Findings

#### Programmes
- **NHS Best Start in Life:** talk and sing while feeding; at nappy changes "Talk to them about what you're doing", repeat the same words, and stay face to face; at bath time talk about the water and its sounds; on walks "Watch their face to see what interests them" and name it. The page does not cover shopping — [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/learning-to-talk/).
- **Vroom Brain Building Basics** (Bezos Family Foundation; 1,000+ tips; ages 5 and under; English and Spanish link): Look, Follow, Chat, Take Turns, Stretch — [Vroom](https://www.vroom.org/). Its approach is "turning routine, shared moments into brain-building moments… mealtime, bath time or anytime" — [Vroom briefing](https://earlysuccess.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Vroom-Briefing.pdf).
- **The Basics** (grew out of Harvard's Achievement Gap Initiative; Ron Ferguson is founder): Maximize Love, Manage Stress; Talk, Sing, and Point; Count, Group, and Compare; Explore through Movement and Play; Read and Discuss Stories. "Basics Insights" sends two or more messages weekly in English, Spanish, Brazilian Portuguese and Arabic. Evaluation reports are linked but I did not read them — [The Basics](https://thebasics.org/).
- **CDC:** "Talk to your baby", answer sounds by repeating them and adding words, feed slowly, read, and sing — [CDC infants](https://www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/infants.html). For toddlers: read daily, build on their words, let them help with dressing and feeding, ask them to find objects, and take outings such as bus rides — [CDC toddlers](https://www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/toddlers-1-2-years.html).

#### Evidence
- **Text nudges (READY4K!):** an eight-month randomised trial raised parental involvement by 0.15–0.29 SD and children's early literacy by about 0.11 SD — [York, Loeb & Doss, NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20659).
- **Coached play (Jamaica, the basis of Reach Up):** one-hour weekly home visits for two years, coaching mothers in play and interaction with stunted toddlers, raised average earnings by 42% twenty years later — [Gertler et al., NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19185). This is intensive coaching, not an app, so don't borrow its effect size.
- **Supermarket Speak:** signs in supermarkets significantly increased the amount and quality of adult-child talk in low-SES stores and had little effect in mid-SES stores — [Ridge et al., 2015](https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/items/59c5454a-743e-407e-8fa0-7ef05d9705bb). Prompts included "What is your favourite vegetable?" and "Where does the milk come from?" — [NTU summary](https://blogs.ntu.edu.sg/reading/?p=516).
- **Signs at a food pantry** (212 families, children about 2–10): families with 16+ turns rose from 6% to 19%, and 63% near academic signs discussed number, colour or shape, against 14% near non-academic signs — [Shivaram et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.645788/full).
- **Urban Thinkscape (Philadelphia bus stop):** more caregiver-child interaction and language. Reported effect sizes conflict: 28% with N=280 — [Hirsh-Pasek slides](https://www.gu.org/app/uploads/2024/03/2024-Play-for-All-Ages-Slides.pdf); 25% — [Newsweek](https://mag.newsweek.com/scientists-sprinkle-playful-learning-stations-cities-around-world-1813801). Interaction was similar to a nearby playground — [WEF](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/how-can-we-create-fun-and-effective-learning-opportunities-for-children-in-public-spaces/).

### Inferences
- PlayNest's in-app prompts act like the supermarket signs: a cue at the moment, with no extra time. Evidence that a phone prompt works as well as a physical sign doesn't exist in what I read, so copy should say "gets you talking", not "boosts language".
- The Ridge finding (effect in low-SES stores, little in mid-SES) suggests such prompts may help most where they add something new. Don't promise universal gains.
- Maths and colour prompts raise maths and colour talk (Shivaram), so a routine card can deliberately tag `count`, `shape` or `colour`.
- Thunderstorm mode must hide bath ideas (see section 1).
- Video calls: PlayNest's existing `aap-screen-time` source is commonly read as exempting video chat from screen limits. I did not re-check the wording in this run.

## 3. Festivals and celebrations

### Takeaway
Festival content must separate three things: **dates** (many need a shipped table, and `Intl` is wrong in places), **facts** (who celebrates and who doesn't), and **practice** (what outsiders may enjoy and what belongs to the community). Every flame, fire, firecracker or choking risk needs a built-in safe swap.

### Cited Findings

#### Dates: what `Intl` can and can't do (tested November 2026)
PlayNest is a Capacitor/React app ([package.json](D:/dev/tests/baby/package.json)), so `Intl` runs in the device's system WebView, not a JavaScript engine shipped with the app.

- **Supported calendars per MDN:** `buddhist`, `chinese`, `coptic`, `dangi`, `ethioaa`, `ethiopic`, `gregory`, `hebrew`, `indian`, `islamic-civil`, `islamic-tbla`, `islamic-umalqura`, `iso8601`, `japanese`, `persian`, `roc`. `islamic` has an unspecified algorithm. - **`buddhist` is the Gregorian calendar with a different year** (20 March 2026 returns month 3, day 20, year 2569) [Computed], so it can't give Vesak or Loy Krathong.

- **Islamic variants disagree:** `islamic-umalqura` and `islamic-civil` differ on 863 of 1,826 days in 2026–2030 [Computed]. #### Persian and wider Iranian-cultural celebrations

**Nowruz**
- The current UNESCO listing (2024) names 13 states: Afghanistan, Azerbaijan, India, Iran, Iraq, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Pakistan, Tajikistan, Türkiye, Turkmenistan and Mongolia — [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/nawrouz-novruz-nowrouz-nowrouz-nawrouz-nauryz-nooruz-nowruz-navruz-nevruz-nowruz-navruz-02097).
- It is a holy day for Zoroastrians, Baháʼís and Ismaili Shia Muslims and largely secular for others. Kurds mark it as Newroz. Most Armenians do not celebrate it. The haft-sin has seven items beginning with "s" (sabzeh, samanu, senjed, serkeh, sib, sir, somaq); mirror, candles, painted eggs and goldfish are optional. Its origins are unclear, and it became popular in about the past 100 years — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowruz).
- **Respect:** Haji Firuz is performed with a sooted face. The Collective for Black Iranians, among others, has called for an end to it, and scholars link it to the slave trade and blackface performance — [Wikipedia, Hajji Firuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hajji_Firuz). Leave him out and use Amu Nowruz if a herald is wanted.
- [Try] **Grow sabzeh** (36–144 m): lentils or wheat on a damp cloth on a plate, with a daily look. An adult handles seeds near under-3s.
- [Try] **Seven-things hunt** (48–120 m): seven objects starting with the same letter, in your home language.
- [Try] **Painted-egg family** (24–144 m): a hard-boiled egg for each family member (from Azerbaijan's [khoncha custom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowruz)).
- [Try] **Mirror on the spring table** (3–18 m): a safe mirror among flowers. *"Babies love faces, and it's a real part of the table."*

**Chaharshanbe Suri** (eve of the last Wednesday of the year)
- People jump over bonfires saying "your redness for my paleness"; disguised people bang spoons on bowls door to door for snacks; ajil (nuts and dried fruit) is eaten. Iranian authorities have discouraged it since 1979 and issue warnings — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaharshanbe_Suri).
- **Safety evidence:** in 35 firecracker patients in Tehran in 2011 (ages 3–72), hands were injured in 37% and eyes in 20%. The paper's discussion gives handling explosives as about 54% of injuries by activity and jumping over fire as 5.9%, and I could not tell whether those figures come from this sample or earlier work — [Hatamabadi et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3876519/). In a 2014 Tabriz series, 72.9% were injured by firecrackers and 27.1% burned by fires — [Tabriz study](https://sid.ir/fileserver/je/5064320160303.pdf). Officials in 2019 reported 3 deaths and almost 2,000 injuries over four weeks, including 453 eye injuries — [Rudaw](https://rudaw.net/english/middleeast/iran/20032019).
- **The app must never suggest fire-jumping for children.**
- [Try] **Ribbon-fire jump** (36–120 m): orange ribbon on the floor, jump and shout the rhyme. No flame. *"All of the jump with none of the burn."*
- [Try] **Spoon-bang round** (24–96 m): bang spoons on bowls at each room's door for a treat. Whole nuts stay away from under-5s (existing `aap-choking` source).
- [Try] **Older-child talk** (96–144 m): firecrackers and why people get hurt, before anyone offers one.

**Sizdah Bedar** (13 Farvardin)
- Iranians spend the day outdoors, knot the sabzeh stems and throw them into moving water — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sizdah_Be-dar).
- [Try] **Family picnic with a wish knot** (48–144 m): tie a knot in a blade of sabzeh and say a wish. Hold hands near moving water.
- [Try] **Return it to the soil** (24–96 m): dig the sabzeh into a garden if there's no safe water.

**Yalda / Chella**
- UNESCO lists Afghanistan and Iran (2022). It falls on "the last night of autumn". Families gather at elders' homes with a lamp, water, red fruits (pomegranates, watermelons, jujube), nuts and sweets, with poetry, stories, games and music, and gifts for children — [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/01877).
- Reading from Hafez as a divination is popular, and people may sit round a korsi (a low table with a heated base) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yalda_Night). Never use charcoal heaters indoors — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/psa-toolkit/avoiding-carbon-monoxide-poisoning.html).
- [Try] **Longest-night stories** (36–144 m): stay up a little later for one extra story each.
- [Try] **Pomegranate count** (24–96 m): seeds into a bowl, for older toddlers who eat small foods safely.
- [Try] **Poem pick** (72–144 m): open a poetry book anywhere and talk about one line. (Some families won't want the fortune-telling framing, so offer it as a poem.)
- [Try] **Red-food table** (18–72 m): find everything red to eat.

**Mehregan**
- A Zoroastrian lesson plan gives 16 Mehr, a purple cloth, pomegranates, persimmons and apples, gratitude, and Mehr's link to love and keeping promises — [FEZANA](https://fezana.org/wp-content/uploads/FEZANA-Mehregan-PreK-K.pdf). Some Iranian sources place it on 10 Mehr after calendar reform — [Tehran Times](https://www.tehrantimes.com/news/304445/Mehregan-the-time-honored-festival-of-autumn). UNESCO listing 2024 for Iran and Tajikistan — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehregan).
- [Try] **Promise chain** (48–144 m): write one promise on each paper link, and check back in a week.
- [Try] **Purple-table hunt** (18–60 m): find everything purple for a shared table.
- [Try] **Thank-you fruit bowl** (24–84 m): name one thing you're thankful for with each fruit.

#### Islamic celebrations (each family decides; never bundled into one "Muslim" switch)

**Ramadan**
- Fasting from dawn to sunset is obligatory for Muslims who have reached puberty, and pre-pubertal children are exempt. Traditions include iftar starting with dates, more charity, and Laylat al-Qadr; lanterns hang in streets in some countries — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan).
- The fanous is a lantern traced to medieval Cairo; its origin stories are hedged ("it is said"), so don't state them as fact — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanous).
- [Try] **Paper fanous with an LED** (48–144 m). *"Lanterns are the fun part, with no flame."*
- [Try] **Kindness calendar** (36–144 m): one small kind act a day.
- [Try] **Moon diary** (48–144 m): draw the moon every evening of the month.
- [Try] **Set the iftar table** (24–96 m): dates in a dish and a count of cups. Pitted dates, cut small for young children. Nothing in the app should encourage children to fast.

**Hag al-Laila and Gerga'oon**
- **Hag al-Laila** is an Emirati children's tradition on the eve of 15 Sha'ban. Children in traditional clothes go door to door with woven bags singing "Atoona Hag Al Laila" for sweets and nuts — [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/living-in-uae/ask-us/what-is-hag-al-laila-the-emirati-tradition-that-marks-the-countdown-to-ramadan-1.500421158).
- **Gerga'oon** is mid-*Ramadan*, not mid-Sha'ban, in Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain and some Saudi cities, and Oman has Qaranqasho — [The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/lifestyle/things-to-do/2026/01/30/hag-al-leila-ramadan-dubai-abu-dhabi-uae/). Sources conflict on the night: 15th (The National), 14th ([Daily Tribune, Bahrain](https://www.newsofbahrain.com/bahrain/89043.html)), 13th–15th ([mirror of Wikipedia](https://wiki.riteme.site/wiki/Gargee%27an)). Offer "mid-Ramadan" and let families set the night.
- [Try] **Sing at every door** (24–96 m): relatives or rooms give a treat. Swap whole nuts for sweets or dates for under-5s.
- [Try] **Make a treat bag** (36–120 m): decorate a cloth bag.

**Eid al-Fitr**
- Lasts one to three days, depending on the country. Zakat al-Fitr is paid before the prayer. Children receive Eidi money; families visit; food is central (ma'amoul, kahk, baklava, shemai) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eid_al-Fitr).
- [Try] **Eidi envelopes** (36–120 m): decorate them with a kind note inside.
- [Try] **Biscuit press** (36–144 m): shape ma'amoul or a family recipe.
- [Try] **Share jar** (48–144 m): choose who your family's gift is for.
- [Try] **Greet in three languages** (48–144 m): "Eid Mubarak" and any family language.

**Eid al-Adha**
- Falls on 10 Dhu al-Hijja and honours Ibrahim's obedience; accounts differ on naming Ishmael or Isaac. Those who can afford it sacrifice livestock, and meat is generally split three ways (family, friends and relatives, the poor) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eid_al-Adha).
- [Try] **Thirds** (48–120 m): split a treat into three and deliver two. *"Sharing you can see."*
- [Try] **Gift for a neighbour** (24–96 m): make a card together.
- Leave the sacrifice story and any detail to the family, and don't illustrate it in the app.

**Mawlid (care needed)**
- 12 Rabi I; many Twelver Shia use 17 Rabi I. Most Sunni and Shia Muslims observe it, Salafi and Wahhabi scholars oppose it and prohibit observance, and Deobandi jurists treat it as a matter of scholarly disagreement. It is a holiday in Egypt, Pakistan, Indonesia, Iran and others, but not in Saudi Arabia or Qatar. Children often join recitations — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mawlid).
- [Try] **Story and poem evening** (48–144 m), and **share food with neighbours** (24–144 m). Show only if a family turns this on specifically.

#### South Asian and Hindu festivals

**Diwali / Deepavali**
- Falls on the new moon around Ashvin–Kartika (roughly mid-October to mid-November), over five days. Hindus, Jains and Sikhs mark it, as do Newar Buddhists; other Buddhists generally do not. Meanings vary by region: Kali worship in eastern India, Krishna and Narakasura in the south, a new year in Gujarat, Tihar in Nepal — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diwali). A Hindu community group frames it as light over darkness and offers a free activity packet with rangoli — [Hindu American Foundation](https://www.hinduamerican.org/diwali). UNESCO inscribed it in December 2025 — [Newsonair](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/unesco-adds-deepavali-to-intangible-cultural-heritage-list).
- **Flame safety:** sparklers burn at about 2,000°F; "Never allow young children to play with or ignite fireworks, including sparklers"; about 1,300 ER-treated US sparkler injuries in 2025 — [CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Fireworks). Keep flames in sturdy holders and never unattended — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/psa-toolkit/proper-use-of-candles-during-a-power-outage.html).
- [Try] **Rice-flour rangoli** (24–120 m): dots and lines on a tray or doorstep.
- [Try] **Paint a clay diya, light it with LED** (36–120 m). Real diyas stay on a high, adult-lit shelf.
- [Try] **Welcome-home lights story** (36–96 m): Rama's return, with a torch trail. Choose the family's own story.
- [Try] **Sweet round** (24–120 m): carry a plate to a neighbour.

**Holi**
- Falls on the last full moon of Phalguna, usually March. Holika Dahan is the bonfire the night before, recalling Prahlada's survival; the colours recall Radha and Krishna. Hindus and others celebrate, as do Sikhs, Jains and Newars, and it is described as open to non-Hindus — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holi).
- **Colour safety:** malachite green was linked to severe eye irritation (Velpandian et al., 2007), and metal-based dyes to more skin problems, often sold unlabelled (Ghosh et al., 2009). Natural sources include turmeric for yellow and beetroot for magenta — [Wikipedia, citing studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holi).
- [Try] **Kitchen-colour splat** (18–96 m): turmeric or beetroot with cornflour on paper or an old T-shirt. Keep it from eyes and mouth, and no shop powder for small children. *"All colour, and you know what's in it."*
- [Try] **Puppet story** (36–96 m): Prahlada and Holika with paper puppets and no fire.
- Don't include the bonfire as a family activity.

**Raksha Bandhan**
- Falls on the Shravana full moon (28 Aug 2026). Sisters tie a rakhi on brothers, who give a gift and share responsibility. It can also create "voluntary kin" across caste, class and religion — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raksha_Bandhan).
- [Try] **Thread bracelet** (48–144 m): for a sibling, cousin or close friend, which also suits families without a brother-sister pair. Not for under-3s to wear unsupervised.
- [Try] **Promise to look after** (36–120 m): each child names one way they'll help the other.

**Navratri**
- Sharada Navaratri falls in Ashvin (11–19 Oct 2026) and Vasanta in spring (19–27 Mar 2026). It is Durga Puja in Bengal, Assam and Odisha; garba in Gujarat; Dashain in Nepal; it ends with Vijayadashami or Dussehra — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navaratri).
- [Try] **Circle clap steps** (48–144 m): a simple clap-and-step circle, inspired by garba.
- [Try] **Toy steps display** (36–120 m): arrange favourite toys on stairs or books, as an adaptation of the display custom named Golu/Kolu. I could not confirm details of this custom.

**Pongal**
- A Tamil harvest festival to the sun god Surya. Thai Pongal is usually 14 or 15 January, with four days (Bhogi, Thai Pongal, Mattu Pongal, Kaanum Pongal). Rice is boiled with milk and jaggery until it boils over, and kolam are drawn in rice flour; cattle are honoured — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pongal_(festival)).
- [Try] **Dot kolam** (48–144 m): rice flour or chalk on the step.
- [Try] **Watch it boil over** (24–96 m): the adult stirs, and the child cheers from a safe distance.
- [Try] **Thank an animal** (18–84 m): draw an animal that helps people.

**Onam**
- Falls in Chingam on the Thiruvonam star, about ten days from Atham, usually August–September. It is Kerala's main festival, and Christians and Muslims also take part. Customs include pookkalam flower carpets, sadya on banana leaves, Mahabali's homecoming, snake-boat races and swings — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onam).
- [Try] **Flower carpet that grows daily** (18–120 m): fallen petals and leaves, one ring a day.
- [Try] **Leaf-plate meal** (36–144 m): eat together from a big leaf or a paper equivalent.
- [Try] **Welcome-the-king story** (48–120 m): Mahabali's visit.

**Ganesh Chaturthi**
- Falls in Bhadrapada (22 Aug–20 Sep), with clay murti, modak and immersion. Plaster-of-Paris idols pollute water; some families dissolve clay idols in a barrel at home and put the clay on the garden — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganesh_Chaturthi).
- [Try] **Make modak** (36–144 m): rice dough and a jaggery-coconut filling, cut small for young children.
- [Try] **Clay back to garden** (60–144 m): for families who already keep a murti. Shape a small clay form, let it dissolve in a bucket and water the plants. Don't offer idol-making as a generic craft (my inference).

#### Buddhist

**Vesak**
- Marks the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and death; observed by Theravada, Tibetan and Navayana Buddhists. Dates vary widely: in 2026 Myanmar observed 30 April; Cambodia, India and Laos 1 May; Sri Lanka 30 May; Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand 31 May. East Asian Buddha's Birthday is 8 April in Japan and the 8th of the fourth lunar month in China, Korea and Vietnam. Customs include lanterns, dana and bathing a Buddha image. Life release is common, but Singapore bans it because animals may die or harm ecosystems — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesak).
- [Try] **Paper lotus lantern with an LED** (36–144 m).
- [Try] **A day of giving** (24–144 m): the child chooses a gift of time or food.
- **Don't suggest releasing animals.** Leave bathing the image to practising families.

#### East Asian

**Lunar New Year (China and wider)**
- UNESCO listed it in 2024. Customs include a reunion dinner, cleaning beforehand, red paper-cuts, red envelopes, lion and dragon dances. Firecrackers are banned in parts of some cities — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_New_Year).
- [Try] **Red paper-cuts** (60–144 m); **box lion dance** (24–84 m); **zodiac animal walk** (18–72 m); **kind-words red envelope** (36–120 m). Pairs with the existing Catch the Dragon's Tail.

**Tết (Vietnam)**
- Children wear new clothes and greet elders, who give money in red envelopes (*mừng tuổi* in the North, *lì xì* in the South). Families cook bánh chưng (square, for the Earth) and bánh tét (cylindrical, for the moon), and the origin story is told while they cook — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%E1%BA%BFt).
- [Try] **Square and round cakes in playdough** (36–96 m), with the story.
- [Try] **Long-life wishes to elders** (24–120 m): *Sống lâu trăm tuổi*.
- [Try] **Blossom branch of wishes** (36–120 m): paper blossoms on a branch.

**Seollal (Korea)**
- Children in traditional clothing bow deeply (*sebae*) and say "saehae bok mani badeuseyo"; elders give money and words of wisdom. Yut nori is played and tteokguk eaten — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_New_Year).
- [Try] **Bow and wish** (24–96 m): practise the bow, and elders give a word of advice rather than money. Pairs with the existing Yut Nori. Cut tteok small for young children.

**Mid-Autumn: Tết Trung Thu and Chuseok**
- Falls on the 15th of the 8th lunar month. In Vietnam it is also called the "Children's Festival": children parade with lanterns, often star-shaped, wear masks and sing; rice figures (tò he) and pomelo sculptures appear. Vietnam lists it as its Children's Day — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Autumn_Festival); [Wikipedia, Children's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children%27s_Day).
- **Chuseok:** three days in South Korea, with family rites (charye), grave visits and tidying (seongmyo, beolcho), songpyeon rice cakes, the ganggangsullae circle dance under the moon, and ssireum wrestling — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuseok).
- [Try] **Star lantern with an LED** (48–144 m) and an evening parade.
- [Try] **Pomelo face** (24–84 m).
- [Try] **Half-moon dough shapes** (36–120 m), then steam or just play.
- [Try] **Moon circle dance** (36–120 m).
- [Try] **Who's in our family?** (48–144 m). Leave ancestor rites to families who practise them.

#### Japan

**Hinamatsuri**
- 3 March, not a national holiday, for girls' health and happiness. Tiered dolls, peach blossoms, hishi mochi, chirashi; nagashi-bina floats paper dolls away — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinamatsuri).
- [Try] **Toy tiers** (24–84 m) on stairs; **paper-doll float** (48–120 m) in a basin, not a river.

**Tanabata**
- 7 July, or around 7 August in some regions. Orihime and Hikoboshi (Vega and Altair) meet once a year across the Milky Way. Wishes go on tanzaku strips hung on bamboo — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanabata).
- [Try] **Wish branch** (36–144 m); **find Vega and Altair** (72–144 m), easiest in the northern summer sky.

**Kodomo no Hi**
- 5 May. Boys' Day until it was renamed in 1948 to include girls. Carp streamers per family member, helmets, kashiwa mochi — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children%27s_Day_(Japan)).
- [Try] **One carp per person** (36–120 m); **newspaper helmet** (48–120 m). *"Everyone in the family gets a fish."*

**Setsubun**
- Usually 3 February (2 February in 2021 and 2025). Roasted beans are thrown at a family member in an oni mask with the shout "Oni wa soto! Fuku wa uchi!", and people eat one bean per year of age plus one — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setsubun).
- [Try] **Paper-ball oni toss** (24–96 m): pom-poms or paper balls, with no real beans around under-5s. *"Loud, and nothing to choke on."*
- [Try] **Worry oni** (48–144 m): draw what you want to chase out, and toss at it.

#### Thailand

**Songkran**
- Mid-April; UNESCO 2023. Pouring water symbolises "cleansing, reverence and good fortune", alongside respect for elders and family reunion — [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/songkran-in-thailand-traditional-thai-new-year-festival-01719). Scented water is poured over elders' hands (*rod nam dam hua*), and sand stupas are built. Road deaths roughly double, about 52 a day against 27 otherwise (2009–2013), with motorcycles in an estimated 70–80% of holiday crashes — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songkran).
- [Try] **Water on grandparents' hands** (18–144 m), and they give a blessing.
- [Try] **Cup splash** (36–96 m): a small cup each, in the garden, which also fits drought limits.
- [Try] **Sand stupa** (36–96 m).

**Loy Krathong**
- Falls on the full moon of the 12th Thai lunar month. Floats of banana trunk or bread carry a candle and incense. Foam is increasingly banned; Bangkok cleared 661,935 floats and six tonnes of waste in 2016. Sky lanterns led Chiang Mai to cancel 78 flights in 2017, and Bangkok prohibits them — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loy_Krathong).
- [Try] **Basin float** (36–120 m): banana stalk or leaf, an LED tealight, a basin. An adult holds on near any real water.
- [Try] **Let-it-go note** (72–144 m): write down what to leave behind and compost it.
- **No sky lanterns and no foam.**

#### Christian

**Christmas and Advent**
- Advent starts on the fourth Sunday before Christmas in most Western churches, with a wreath and calendars; Orthodox churches keep a 40-day Nativity Fast — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advent). Many non-Christians celebrate Christmas culturally — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas). PBS Kids frames holiday stress and acknowledges grief — [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/parents/holidays).
- [Try] **Kindness paper chain** (36–144 m), one link a day; **LED wreath count** (18–72 m), adding one light each Sunday; **Christmas in summer** (24–144 m) for Southern Hemisphere families, with a beach-themed or shade-based version.

**St Nicholas**
- 6 December (19 December on the Old Calendar). Children set out shoes for small gifts, and in the Netherlands and Belgium hay and a carrot for the horse. Zwarte Piet is criticised as racist — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Nicholas_Day).
- [Try] **Shoe surprise** (24–120 m). Leave Zwarte Piet out.

**Three Kings Day and Epiphany**
- 6 January (19 January on the Julian calendar). In the West it commemorates the Magi, in the East Christ's baptism. In Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay children leave shoes with grass and water for the camels on 5 January, and eat Rosca de Reyes — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epiphany_(holiday)).
- [Try] **Grass and water for the camels** (24–96 m); **ring-cake with a hidden bean** for over-5s, or a paper note for younger children.

**St Lucia**
- 13 December. The girl chosen as Lucia wears a crown of lit candles; households share saffron buns (lussekatter); star boys carry lanterns — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Lucy%27s_Day).
- [Try] **Paper crown with LEDs** (36–120 m); **morning tray for a parent** (48–144 m). **Never real candles on a child's head.**

**Easter**
- Falls on the Sunday after the ecclesiastical full moon; Orthodox churches use the Julian reckoning. Eggs are dyed, rolled, tapped and hunted. Some groups reject it, including Quakers and Jehovah's Witnesses — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter).
- [Try] **Onion-skin egg dye** (24–144 m); **egg roll race** (18–84 m); **clue hunt** (48–144 m).

#### Jewish (note the sundown start)

**Hanukkah**
- Begins at sundown on 25 Kislev for eight nights. Lights on a hanukkiah, dreidel, fried foods, gelt — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanukkah). A "quite minor festival" though beloved, with gift-giving influenced by Christmas — [My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/hanukkah-101/).
- [Try] **Count the lights** (18–60 m): one more each night, watched from a safe distance. Adult lights; place out of reach and never unattended.
- [Try] **Dreidel game** (48–144 m), with counters rather than raisins or coins for young children.
- [Try] **Grate-and-fry latkes** (60–144 m): the child grates, the adult fries.

**Purim**
- Falls on 14 Adar (Adar II in leap years); Jerusalem observes on the 15th. The Book of Esther is read; graggers drown out Haman's name; costumes, food gifts (mishloach manot), gifts to the poor, hamantaschen — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purim).
- [Try] **Shaker gragger** (24–96 m), sealed well; **food-gift basket** (36–144 m); **dress-up day** (18–144 m).

**Sukkot**
- Begins on 15 Tishrei for seven days. A sukkah has at least three walls and a plant-material roof, with lulav and etrog; it is also the harvest "Festival of Ingathering" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukkot).
- [Try] **Shoebox sukkah** (48–120 m); **dinner under branches** (18–144 m).

**Passover**
- Begins at 15 Nisan for seven or eight days. The youngest child traditionally asks the Four Questions, and children hunt the afikoman for a prize — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passover_Seder).
- **Respect:** for many Jews, so-called "Christian Seders" are described as "100% cultural appropriation" — [Salon, via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_observances_of_Jewish_holidays). Don't offer a model seder to non-Jewish families.
- [Try, Jewish families] **Afikoman hunt** (24–144 m); **child leads the Four Questions** (36–144 m). [Try, others] **Why is tonight different?** (48–144 m), a general question game, without the ritual.

#### Other

**Kwanzaa**
- 26 December–1 January; created 1966 by Maulana Karenga; seven principles, kinara, mkeka, zawadi, karamu — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwanzaa). "Kwanzaa is not a religious holiday, but a cultural one". "Kwanzaa is clearly an African holiday created for African peoples. But other people can and do celebrate it." "Audience attendance is one thing; conducting a ritual is another." — [Official Kwanzaa site](https://www.officialkwanzaawebsite.org/faq.html).
- [Try, families who keep it] **Principle of the day** (36–144 m); **paper-weave mkeka** (36–120 m); **LED kinara** (18–96 m).
- [Try, others] **Learn the seven principles** (72–144 m), without running the ritual.

**Yam festivals**
- **Igbo New Yam (Iri ji), Nigeria:** early August at the end of rains; new yams aren't eaten until the rite; the oldest man or a titleholder eats first; dances and masquerades follow — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iri_ji).
- **Asogli Te Za, Ghana (Ewe, Ho):** September; gods and ancestors are served mashed yam first, and the rest is shared by the community — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asogli_Te_Za).
- **Homowo (Ga, Accra):** "hooting at hunger"; kpokpoi is shared; a noise ban runs during sowing. The page is flagged for possible machine-written text, so verify it — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homowo).
- [Try] **Elder eats first** (24–120 m): serve the oldest person the first piece. **Yam-every-way meal** (36–144 m). **Quiet-then-loud game** (24–96 m): a noise ban, then a joyful cheer. Community rites stay with the community.

**Meskel and Timkat (Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox)**
- **Meskel:** commemorates the True Cross; a Demera bonfire with daisies on the firewood; people mark foreheads with its charcoal. UNESCO 2013 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meskel).
- **Timkat:** Epiphany, with the tabot carried in procession and water blessed and sprinkled. UNESCO 2019 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timkat).
- [Try] **Paper-daisy garland** (18–96 m), with no bonfire at home. **Home music parade** (24–96 m). Leave blessing, tabot and procession to the church.

**Día de los Muertos**
- UNESCO: practised by Indigenous communities in Mexico, late October to early November, blending pre-Hispanic rites and Catholic feasts. It marks the "transitory return to Earth" of the dead, with petals, candles, favourite dishes and paper cut-outs — [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/indigenous-festivity-dedicated-to-the-dead-00054). Scholars disagree on origins — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_the_Dead). Marigolds, photographs, pan de muerto, sugar skulls; children's *calaverita* custom is not trick-or-treating, as it carries no threat — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_the_Dead).
- [Try] **Someone we remember** (36–144 m): one photo, one favourite food, one story. *"It's OK to talk about people who've died."*
- [Try] **Paper marigolds** (24–120 m); **papel picado** (60–144 m), with child-safe scissors; **LED on the shelf**, with any real candles adult-managed.
- Don't present it as a Halloween alternative or costume (my inference; no source retrieved).

**Carnival**
- Ends on Shrove Tuesday before Lent, with regional variety: Rio, Trinidad, Venice, and Fastelavn in Lutheran countries. Masks let people step out of everyday roles. Some carnivals aren't tied to Lent (Barbados' Crop Over, Antigua) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnival).
- [Try] **Mask making** (36–120 m); **home parade with shakers** (24–84 m).

**Inti Raymi**
- Inca sun festival at the winter solstice. Banned under Spanish rule; a 1944 reconstruction led to the staged performance at Sacsayhuamán on 24 June. Andean communities still observe it with music, costume and food — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inti_Raymi).
- [Try] **Shortest-shadow day** (72–144 m): measure a stick's shadow, in the Southern Hemisphere in June. **Sun drawings** (24–72 m). Treat it as learning about, not re-enacting.

**Matariki (Aotearoa New Zealand)**
- Matariki is the Māori name for a star cluster that rises in midwinter and, for many Māori, heralds the new year — [matariki.com](https://www.matariki.com/). The cluster is the Pleiades — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matariki). It is a time to "reflect on the past, celebrate the present, and plan for the future" — [Te Papa](https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/discover-collections/read-watch-play/matariki-maori-new-year).
- The official method is Tangaroa of Pipiri; the Friday holiday is the closest to that lunar period. Prepared by Prof Rangi Mātāmua. Iwi differ: some use Puanga, Atutahi or Rehua; the celebration "is not restricted to a single day" — [MBIE report](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/assets/matariki-dates-2022-to-2052-matariki-advisory-group.pdf).
- **What Māori sources ask:** Matariki "isn't universally observed", partly because it can't be seen from everywhere, and some iwi use Puanga — [Te Papa](https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/matariki-maori-new-year/matariki-regional-variations). Māori cultural advisers warned against commercialising it, and fireworks were raised under that heading — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matariki).
- Te Papa's suggestions: reconnect with whānau, a feast, sharing stories, whakataukī, waiata — [Te Papa](https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/matariki-maori-new-year/how-celebrate-matariki). The Pōhutukawa star is linked to those who have died — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matariki).
- [Try] **Pre-dawn look** (72–144 m): on a clear morning, without pressure. **Remember, celebrate, plan** (36–144 m): one person you miss, one good thing, one hope. **Learn a waiata** (24–144 m) from Te Papa. **Feast with whānau** (all ages).
- Don't script hautapu in the app; link to Te Papa's guidance. No fireworks.

**NAIDOC Week (Australia)**
- Runs from the first to second Sunday of July. It began with 1920s advocacy and the 1938 Day of Mourning; the name was extended to Torres Strait Islander peoples in 1991 — [NAIDOC history](https://www.naidoc.org.au/about/history). 2026 theme: "50 Years of Deadly". The site warns that images and voices of deceased people may appear — [NAIDOC](https://www.naidoc.org.au/).

### Inferences
- **Ship a table, not an algorithm.** Generate 2026–2036 at build time into JSON, reviewed by hand, with a `source` and `confidence` for each entry. Don't compute in the WebView at runtime: engines vary, and the tested ICU build is wrong for at least two Lunar New Years. Add a unit test pinning LNY 2027 = 6 Feb and 2030 = 3 Feb, which would fail if someone swaps in `Intl`.
- **Show ranges in Islamic dates and let the family correct them:** "Eid is expected around 20 March. Your community will confirm." A one-tap "It's today for us" would respect sighting differences and needs no network.
- **Handle more than one occurrence per Gregorian year:** Ramadan starts twice in 2030, and mid-Sha'ban twice in 2028.
- **Handle sundown starts:** Jewish festivals and Eid begin the evening before. Show "starts this evening".
- **Keep Tết, Seollal and Chinese New Year as separate entries,** because their dates differ. Korea differs in 2027 and 2028, and Vietnam in 2030.
- **Use a universal flame rule:** every activity with a flame in the source custom uses LED, adult-only or skip. Add `fireRisk`, `chokingRisk`, `waterRisk` and `smokeRisk` flags, so condition filters (smoky, power cut, stormy) work automatically.
- **Hemisphere mismatch:** Yalda's "longest night" and Tanabata's summer stars don't fit a southern family. Inti Raymi and Matariki are midwinter in June. Say which sky a festival belongs to rather than implying it's local.
- **Mid-festival timing:** with Diwali on 8 Nov and Loy Krathong on 24 Nov 2026, first release timing affects what users see first. If the app ships before December, Hanukkah (from sundown 4 Dec), Yalda (21 Dec) and Kwanzaa will be the first "Celebrate" collections many see, so they need review first.

### Gaps
- **UNESCO claims in the brief:** I could **not** confirm UNESCO inscriptions for **Holi** or **Mid-Autumn**. The Mid-Autumn article mentions none for the festival, and I found no Holi listing. Don't claim them. UNESCO listings for Deepavali (news and government only), Spring Festival and Mehregan were not checked on UNESCO's own pages. The Iftar listing wasn't retrieved.
- **Diwali 2029–2030:** no authoritative panchang was reachable (Drik Panchang 403; timeanddate 403). Local tithi can shift a date by a day; I found no source on specific diaspora differences.
- **Holi, Navratri, Raksha Bandhan, Onam, Ganesh Chaturthi dates for 2027–2030** weren't retrieved. Each needs a panchang-sourced table. Vesak 2027 is listed as 20 May, without per-country detail, and 2028–2030 is absent.
- **Setsubun future dates** need a solar-term table; only 3 Feb 2027 was seen.
- **Whole-bean choking at Setsubun:** I recall Japan's Consumer Affairs Agency advising against beans and nuts for under-5s, but the page I reached doesn't include it. Confirm before citing; the swap is still sensible under the existing `aap-choking` source.
- **Sabzeh germination time** isn't in my sources. Test it before giving a start date.
- **Live goldfish on haft-sin:** I found no source on environmental or welfare concerns, though I'd avoid suggesting live animals. **Hindu American Foundation on colour runs** (403): the claim that Hindu groups object to commercial colour runs is unverified.
- **Dot painting and Aboriginal art protocols:** Narragunnawali and Arts Law could not be reached, so the common guidance not to copy Aboriginal dot painting is unverified. The safe default is to link NAIDOC resources and not write such crafts.
- **Day of the Dead appropriation:** the Smithsonian page returned 403, and the face-paint caution is my own.
- **Rangi Mātāmua on fireworks:** Wikipedia attributes the fireworks concern to a Newshub and Retail NZ report. I could not verify any direct quote from him.

## 4. How an app should handle this inclusively

### Takeaway
Keep everything festival-related **off by default**, opt-in per celebration, and never inferred. The comparators I could check don't offer a model to copy: Bebbo and Vroom don't mention festivals on the pages I read, and PBS Kids shows a mostly Western, Northern selection without a stated principle.

### Cited Findings
- **Bebbo (UNICEF):** free, works offline, localised in 17 countries; each day suggests an article and a game. The home page mentions no holidays, festivals or religion, referring only to adapting to local context — [Bebbo](https://www.bebbo.app/).
- **Vroom:** the home page doesn't mention holidays or festivals — [Vroom](https://www.vroom.org/). Its daily tip is "personalized to the child" — [Vroom briefing](https://earlysuccess.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Vroom-Briefing.pdf).
- **PBS Kids for Parents:** covers Christmas, Hanukkah, Kwanzaa, Thanksgiving, New Year and Nochebuena, plus snow-day ideas. It focuses on relationships over gifts and acknowledges grief, with no explicit statement on diverse families — [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/parents/holidays).
- **Non-observance is real inside faiths:** Salafi and Wahhabi scholars prohibit Mawlid observance — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mawlid). Quakers and Jehovah's Witnesses reject Easter — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter). Other Buddhists generally don't observe Diwali — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diwali). Most Armenians don't celebrate Nowruz — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowruz). Some iwi use Puanga — [Te Papa](https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/matariki-maori-new-year/matariki-regional-variations).
- **Community limits on outsiders:** Kwanzaa's founder distinguishes attending from "conducting a ritual" and says "particular people should always be in control of and conduct their own celebrations" — [Official Kwanzaa site](https://www.officialkwanzaawebsite.org/faq.html). Many Jews object to Christian seders — [Wikipedia, citing Salon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_observances_of_Jewish_holidays).
- **Privacy:** a family's chosen celebrations may reveal "religious or philosophical beliefs", a special category under GDPR Article 9 — [GDPR Art. 9](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/).

### Inferences
- **Three states per celebration:** *Ours* (appears in "right now" with the full family version), *Curious* (browsable, with a "learn about it" version that excludes rituals), and *Hidden* (default).
- **Separate defaults by type.** Weather, season and routine are on by default. Civic, shared days (Earth Day, Children's Day, Grandparents' Day) are optional and off, or a single gentle on/off. Religious and cultural celebrations are always individually off.
- **Offer a picker, not a religion question.** Ask "Which celebrations does your family enjoy?" and show the list, alphabetically. Never ask for a faith, and don't bundle ("All Muslim holidays" would wrongly switch on Mawlid; "All Christian" would wrongly apply Western dates to Orthodox families). Offer calendar variants: Orthodox or Western Easter, 16 or 10 Mehr, Matariki or Puanga.
- **Never infer** from device language, locale, name or time zone, even though the app could. A guess that is wrong about faith is worse than offering nothing.
- **Keep choices on the device only,** in line with the no-account philosophy, and exclude them from analytics. This avoids creating sensitive data in the first place. Whether GDPR applies to a purely local setting is a question for counsel.
- **Add per-entry metadata:** `whoCelebrates`, `notEveryoneDoes`, `insiderOnly` (rituals), `curiousVersion`, a reviewer name and a date.
- **Write the copy accordingly.** Prefer "Some families mark Mawlid; others don't" over "Muslims celebrate". Don't equate one festival with another ("Jewish Christmas" or "Persian Halloween"). My Jewish Learning calls Hanukkah a "quite minor festival" whose gift-giving grew under Christmas's influence, which shows how misleading the comparison is.
- **Don't guilt.** No streaks, and no notification on a day the family didn't choose. If they turn on notifications for a celebration, one per celebration is plenty.
- **Death-related content needs a gentle door.** Día de los Muertos, Matariki's remembrance and Chuseok's rites should say so up front, since some families are grieving (PBS's grief article shows this is a known need).
- **Offer a family-added celebration.** A blank "add our own" date covers name days, local festivals and family traditions this list will never reach.

### Gaps
- **Sesame Workshop:** its handling couldn't be retrieved in this run, so nothing is claimed.
- **Bebbo and Vroom in-app content:** I read only their public pages, so "no mention" doesn't prove the app has none. **PBS Kids** coverage may be broader than this one page, since it reflects the winter-holiday page I reached.
- **Anti-bias "tourist curriculum" guidance (Derman-Sparks, NAEYC):** the page reached doesn't discuss holidays, so it isn't cited. It would be a useful addition.
- **User research:** I found no study of how families feel about festival content in parenting apps, or of opt-in versus opt-out effects. The defaults above are my judgement.

## 5. Child interests

### Takeaway
Building play around a child's interests is well-supported as a *practice* and costs little. However, the strongest number I could verify is from autism intervention, and general-population outcome evidence wasn't retrievable here. Offer interests as a light, changeable flavour, and don't claim learning gains.

### Cited Findings
- **Interest-based intervention:** a meta-analysis of 24 studies with 78 children aged 2–6 with autism found that using children's own interests to encourage social behaviour and reduce problem behaviour was effective, both against no intervention and for high-interest versus low-interest activities. The abstract reports no effect-size values, and this applies to autistic children, not all children — [Dunst, Trivette & Hamby, 2012](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/article/MED/22934173?resultType=core&format=json).
- **Extremely intense interests:** from surveys and interviews with 177 parents, nearly one third of young children have them, and they are much more common for boys than girls — [DeLoache, Simcock & Macari, 2007](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/article/MED/18020834?resultType=core&format=json). The title itself names planes, trains, automobiles and tea sets.
- **Existence and bibliographic details confirmed; findings not read:** Hidi & Renninger's "The Four-Phase Model of Interest Development" (2006, *Educational Psychologist* 41(2), 111–127) — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4); Alexander, Johnson, Leibham & Kelley, "The development of conceptual interests in young children" (2008, *Cognitive Development* 23(2), 324–334) — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.cogdev.2007.11.004).
- **Following the child's lead in programmes:** Vroom's "Follow" asks parents to respond to a child's words, sounds, ideas and movements — [Vroom](https://www.vroom.org/). NHS says to watch what interests a baby and name it — [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/learning-to-talk/).
- **Montessori "sensitive periods":** Montessori described periods of special sensitivity (order about 1–3, small objects about 18 months–3, language birth–6, and others). The article cites no study testing the framework; research there concerns whole Montessori programmes, with mixed results — [Wikipedia, Montessori education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montessori_education).

### Inferences

#### What this means for PlayNest
- **Copy should stay honest:** *"Easier to get them started when it's something they already love."* That is a claim about engagement, and it doesn't promise learning gains.
- **Don't present "sensitive periods" as fact.** They are Montessori's observations, untested in what I read.
- **Don't gender the list or defaults,** despite DeLoache's sex difference. Show all interests in a neutral order. The study's own title pairs trains with tea sets.
- **Treat intense interests as ordinary:** nearly a third of young children have them. Don't suggest a screening meaning. Don't infer autism from an interest, and let the existing milestones guide handle concerns.
- **Don't use the Dunst result for general claims.** It is, however, useful for a line to parents of autistic children: starting from a child's interests is an established approach, and their clinician can advise.

#### How to offer it in one sentence
- **Prompt:** *"What's [Name] into right now? Pick up to three, and we'll work it into your ideas."*
- **Result line:** *"Because Mira's into dinosaurs: sort the laundry into herbivore and carnivore piles."*
- **Refresh:** after about three months, one quiet line: *"Still into dinosaurs?"* Interests change, so never lock them in.
- **Skip is fine:** the app works just as well without any choice.
- **Implementation:** no need to rewrite 528 activities. Add an optional `interestHook` per interest to a subset of flexible activities, such as sorting, counting, pretend, movement and stories. An idea with no hook simply appears unchanged.

### Gaps
- **General-population evidence:** I could not retrieve abstracts or findings for **Hidi & Renninger (2006)**, **Alexander et al. (2008)** or **Neitzel** (Crossref returned 429 for the likely 2008 *Journal of Educational Psychology* paper, so even its citation is unconfirmed). From memory only, and **unverified**: Hidi & Renninger describe four phases, from triggered situational interest to well-developed individual interest. Have this checked before anyone cites it.
