# World games for PlayNest: East and Southeast Asia, Pacific, Americas, Europe

Retrieved 4 November 2026. Read the "How this was researched" note under Question 1 Gaps before relying on confidence grades.

## Question 1: Which new games can a writer author as play activities, across 0-12 years?

### Takeaway
This file records 93 entries (some are named families of local versions) that do not duplicate the existing 56 world games, and every band from birth to 12 years is covered, including 21 for babies and toddlers. Rules and chants come from the cited pages, but about two-thirds of the entries rest on Wikipedia (roughly 26 grade A, 36 grade B and 29 grade C, with some mixed), so grade C entries need a second source before publication, and the Indigenous entries need the permissions in Question 5.

### Cited Findings

#### How to read an entry
- **Confidence.** A = UNESCO, a national institution, a national-scale collection, or a primary historical text that I read. B = a publisher's encyclopedia or dictionary, or a Wikipedia article that I read and that names identifiable scholarly or archive sources. C = Wikipedia stub or tagged-as-unsourced page, or one non-institutional source. Treat C as low confidence.
- **Editorial lines.** "Fit", "Builds" and "Safety/adapt" are my judgement, not sourced claims, unless a source is attached. Chant translations and romanisations are mine unless stated.
- **Ages** are in months, matching the app's format. Year levels given by Yulunga are noted where they exist.
- **Choking.** Anything with stones, seeds, beads, marbles or discs is unsuitable for under-3s and should be checked against the app's existing sources `cpsc-small-parts` and `aap-choking`. Entries say so only where the fix is not obvious.
- **Sensitivity.** Where no sensitivity line appears, I found nothing specific in the sources I read.
- **Overlap.** "Overlap" flags where the mechanics match something already in the app.

#### Birth to 12 months (lap and touch)

**1. Потягунюшки, порастунюшки (Potyagunyushki, porastunyushki)**, "little stretches, little growings"
- Origin: Russian pestushka, a rhyme for the moment a baby wakes. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 0-9 m; adult and baby; nothing needed; on a changing mat or bed.
- Play: when baby wakes, stroke from head to feet and gently lengthen arms and legs as you say the lines.
- Words (traditional): Потягунюшки, порастунюшки, / Поперёк толстунюшки, / А в ручки хватунюшки, / А в роток говорок, / А в головку разумок. ("Stretchies, growsies, / plump across the middle, / grabbing in the little hands, / talk in the little mouth, / wits in the little head.")
- Builds: senses, feelings, language.
- Safety/adapt: stroke, never pull joints.
- Sources: [Малые жанры фольклора, ru.wikipedia](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Малые_жанры_фольклора).

**2. Сорока-ворона (Soroka-vorona)**, "magpie-crow"
- Origin: Russian potéshka, described with Ladushki as a classic example of the genre. Confidence: B. Note that Ladushki is already in the app, Soroka is not.
- Fit: 3-18 m; adult and baby; nothing needed; anywhere.
- Play: circle a finger on baby's palm; on each "fed this one" fold one finger down; finish on the little finger.
- Words (traditional): Сорока-ворона / Кашу варила, / Деток кормила: / Этому дала, / Этому дала, / Этому дала, / Этому дала, / А этому не дала. ("Magpie-crow cooked porridge, fed her chicks: gave to this one... but not this one.") A longer variant scolds the last finger: "Why didn't you saw wood? Why didn't you carry water?"
- Builds: senses, language, feelings.
- Safety/adapt: for a warmer ending, let the little finger get the last spoon.
- Sources: [Малые жанры фольклора](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Малые_жанры_фольклора); [Ладушки](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ладушки).

**3. ちょちちょちあわわ、かいぐりかいぐり、とっとのめ (Chochi chochi awawa, kaiguri kaiguri, totto no me)**, baby gesture chain; "totto no me" means "bird's eye"
- Origin: Japan. Confidence: B, because each gesture is defined in the Digital Daijisen dictionary.
- Fit: 6-18 m; adult and baby face to face; nothing needed.
- Play, as the dictionary defines each part: chochi chochi = clap hands gently; awawa = pat your open mouth with your palm while voicing "awawa"; kaiguri kaiguri = roll both hands around each other at chest height as if winding thread; totto no me = poke your right forefinger into your left palm. A sister gesture, otsumu tenten, is patting your own head.
- Words: the phrase itself; traditional.
- Builds: hands, language, thinking (imitation), feelings.
- Overlap: "totto no me" uses the same palm-poke as Korean gonji-gonji, already in the app, so frame this as a cousin.
- Sources: [かいぐり, Daijisen](https://www.weblio.jp/content/かいぐり); [とっとの目](https://www.weblio.jp/content/とっとのめ); [あわわ](https://www.weblio.jp/content/あわわ); [ちょうちちょうち](https://www.weblio.jp/content/ちょちちょち); [おつむてんてん](https://www.weblio.jp/content/おつむてんてん).

**4. 도리도리 and 잼잼 (Doridori and jaemjaem, also 지암지암)**, "shake-shake" and "grip-grip"
- Origin: Korea, two of the ten baby moves called 단동십훈 (Dandong sibhun). Confidence: C. The only page I could read is a poorly sourced Korean Wikipedia article.
- Fit: 6-15 m; adult and baby; nothing needed.
- Play: doridori = shake your head side to side and invite baby to copy; jaemjaem = open and close both hands.
- Builds: movement, hands, thinking (imitation).
- Sensitivity: do not repeat the claim that these date from Dangun (2333 BCE). The same article rejects that as impossible and ties its spread to the Jeungsando movement and Hwandan Gogi followers.
- Overlap: gonji-gonji, jjak-jjak-kung and seomma-seomma from the same set are already in the app.
- Sources: [단동십훈, ko.wikipedia](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/단동십훈).

**5. 불아불아 and 시상시상 (Bura-bura and sisang-sisang)**, sway and rock moves
- Origin: Korea, same set as entry 4. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 5-12 m, once baby sits with support; adult and baby.
- Play: bura-bura = hold baby's waist and sway left and right; sisang-sisang = baby sits and rocks forward and back. Two others in the set are 아함아함 (palm over mouth) and 질라아비 휠휠 (holding baby's arms and dancing together).
- Builds: movement, senses, feelings.
- Safety/adapt: support trunk and head; slow and small.
- Sources: [단동십훈](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/단동십훈).

**6. Das ist der Daumen**, "this is the thumb"
- Origin: German-speaking lands; the same finger-story form exists in many countries, with French and Venetian variants on the same page, so make no single-origin claim. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 3-20 m; adult and baby; nothing needed.
- Play: wiggle or touch one finger per line.
- Words (traditional): Das ist der Daumen, / der schüttelt die Pflaumen, / der hebt sie auf, / der trägt sie nach Haus, / und der Kleinste, der isst sie alle auf! ("This is the thumb, this one shakes the plums, this one picks them up, this one carries them home, and the littlest eats them all up!")
- Builds: senses, language, hands.
- Overlap: Dedo mindinho and This Little Piggy share the form.
- Sources: [Fingerspiel, de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerspiel).

**7. Hoppe hoppe Reiter**, "hop, hop, rider"
- Origin: the most widespread German-language knee ride. Confidence: B. Regional cousins on the same page: Low German "Zuck, zuck, zuck na Möhln" (Holstein), Palatine "Hoppe, reire, geire", Swiss "Ryte, ryte, Rössli" and "Joggeli chasch au ryte?".
- Fit: 8-36 m, once baby holds head steady; adult and child; nothing needed.
- Play: child sits on your knees facing you, hands held as reins. Bounce to the rhyme. On "plumps!" open your knees so the child slides almost to the floor, and pull back up by the hands.
- Words (traditional): Hoppe hoppe Reiter, wenn er fällt, dann schreit er. / Fällt er in den Graben, fressen ihn die Raben. / Fällt er in den Sumpf, macht der Reiter plumps! ("Hop, hop, rider, if he falls he yells. If he falls in the ditch, the ravens eat him. If he falls in the swamp, the rider goes plop!")
- Builds: movement, feelings, self-control (waiting for the drop), language.
- Safety/adapt: hold under the arms or trunk, not by the hands alone, for babies under about 2 years, because of pull injury to elbows. Small drop only. Replace ravens with a sillier animal if the words bother you.
- Sources: [Kniereitvers, de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kniereitvers).

**8. Rida, rida ranka / Ride, ride ranke**, "ride, ride a cock-horse"
- Origin: folk rhyme known across the Nordic countries; Svenskt visarkiv holds about a hundred recordings and notations. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 6-30 m; adult and child; nothing needed.
- Play: child rides your knee; bounce to the words; make the two dog barks at the end big.
- Words (traditional, Norwegian text from Elling Holst, 1888): Ride, ride ranke! / Hesten heter Planke! ... Hvor skal junkern ri'e? / Til kongsgården og frie... / Den ene sier: Vovvovvov! / Den annen sier: Voff, voff, voff! ("Ride, ride a horse! The horse is called Planke... Where shall the young squire ride? To the royal farm to woo... One dog says woof woof woof, the other says wuff wuff wuff.")
- Builds: movement, language, feelings.
- Safety/adapt: the Swedish folk text ends by calling Margareta "the fat one", so drop that line. Avoid Hans Henric Hallbäck's literary rewrite if you want the folk version; it is often wrongly credited as the source.
- Sources: [Rida, rida ranka, sv.wikipedia](https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rida,_rida_ranka); [Ride ride ranke, no.wikipedia](https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ride_ride_ranke).

**9. Cadê o toucinho que estava aqui?**, "where's the bacon that was here?"
- Origin: Brazil, in the Mapa do Brincar collection of children's own games. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 6-36 m, or child to child from 4 years; two people; nothing needed.
- Play: name each finger, tap the palm and ask. Run through the chain of questions and answers, then walk two fingers up the arm to tickle under it: "Foi por aqui, aqui, aqui... ACHOU!" ("It went this way, this way... FOUND it!").
- Words (traditional): "Cadê o toucinho que estava aqui? Gato comeu. Cadê o gato? Foi pro mato. Cadê o mato? Fogo queimou. Cadê o fogo? Água apagou. Cadê a água? Boi bebeu..." ("Where's the bacon? The cat ate it. Where's the cat? Off to the bush. Where's the bush? Fire burned it. Where's the fire? Water put it out. Where's the water? The ox drank it...")
- Builds: language, thinking (chains), feelings, senses.
- Overlap: finger names repeat Dedo mindinho; the question chain and tickle are new. The tail of the chain mentions a friar and a Mass, so stop at the ox for a secular version.
- Sources: [Mapa do Brincar](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/cantadas/118-cade-o-toucinho-que-estava-aqui).

**10. Kéo cưa lừa xẻ**, "pull the saw, push the saw"
- Origin: Vietnam. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 8-36 m with an adult, or two children from 3 years; two people; nothing needed; sit facing on the floor.
- Play: hold hands, feet touching if you like, and rock back and forth as if sawing a log, one push or pull per syllable. No winner, though some play that whoever is pushed down on the last word has lost.
- Words (traditional): Kéo cưa lừa xẻ / Ông thợ nào khỏe / Về ăn cơm vua / Ông thợ nào thua / Về bú tí mẹ. ("Pull the saw, push the saw; the strong workman goes to eat the king's rice; the workman who loses goes home to nurse at mum's breast.")
- Builds: movement, language, feelings (turn-taking).
- Safety/adapt: for babies, sit them on your thighs and support the back; move from the trunk, never tug the arms.
- Sources: [Kéo cưa lừa xẻ, vi.wikipedia](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kéo_cưa_lừa_xẻ).

#### 1 to 3 years

**11. Backe, backe Kuchen**, "bake, bake a cake"
- Origin: German; thought to come from Saxony and Thuringia before 1840. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 9-30 m; adult and child; nothing needed, or use real dough on baking day.
- Play: clap a steady beat, then mime each ingredient and "shove into the oven" with both hands at the end.
- Words (traditional): Backe, backe Kuchen, / der Bäcker hat gerufen. / Wer will guten Kuchen backen, / der muss haben sieben Sachen: / Eier und Schmalz, / Zucker und Salz, / Milch und Mehl, / Safran macht den Kuchen gehl! / Schieb, schieb in'n Ofen 'nein. ("Bake, bake a cake, the baker has called. Who wants good cake must have seven things: eggs and lard, sugar and salt, milk and flour, saffron makes the cake yellow! Shove, shove into the oven.")
- Builds: hands, language, thinking (sequence).
- Overlap: same family as Pat-a-cake and Tortillitas, but with a list to mime.
- Sources: [Backe, backe Kuchen, de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backe,_backe_Kuchen).

**12. Himpelchen und Pimpelchen**, two little men with no meaning beyond their names
- Origin: German-language oral fingerplay with many text variants. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 12-36 m; adult and child; nothing needed; thumbs can wear paper hats or drawn faces.
- Play: the two thumbs are the two men. Climb them high, wiggle them for the pointed caps, tuck them away into the fists as they crawl into the mountain, and make gentle sleeping sounds. Pop them out on "Heißa, heißa, hopsassa". The source notes this is an activity-and-rest rhythm in miniature.
- Words (one traditional variant): Himpelchen und Pimpelchen / stiegen auf einen hohen Berg. / Himpelchen war ein Heinzelmann / und Pimpelchen war ein Zwerg. / Sie blieben dort lange sitzen / und wackelten mit ihren Zipfelmützen. / Doch nach vielen, vielen Wochen / sind sie in den Berg gekrochen. / Dort schlafen sie in guter Ruh. / Seid mal still und hört schön zu! / Pütsche pü, pütsche pü... / Doch nach sieben Wochen / sind sie aus dem Berg gekrochen. / Heißa, heißa, hopsassa, / Himpelchen und Pimpelchen sind wieder da! ("...climbed a high mountain. One was a brownie, one a dwarf. They sat a long time and wagged their pointed caps. After many weeks they crawled into the mountain. There they sleep in peace. Be still and listen! Snore, snore... After seven weeks they crawled out. Hooray, they are back!")
- Builds: imagination, self-control (the quiet part), hands, language.
- Sources: [Himpelchen und Pimpelchen, de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himpelchen_und_Pimpelchen).

**13. Большие ноги, маленькие ножки (Bol'shie nogi, malen'kie nozhki)**, "big feet, little feet"
- Origin: Russian pestushka said when a child starts to walk. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 10-24 m; adult and child; nothing needed.
- Play: stomp slowly together on the "big feet" lines; patter quickly on the "little feet" lines.
- Words (traditional): Большие ноги / Шли по дороге: / Топ, топ, топ, / Топ, топ, топ. / Маленькие ножки / Бежали по дорожке: / Топ, топ, топ, топ, / Топ, топ, топ, топ! ("Big feet walked down the road, stomp, stomp, stomp. Little feet ran down the path, tip-tip-tip-tip.")
- Builds: movement, self-control (slow versus fast), language.
- Sources: [Малые жанры фольклора](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Малые_жанры_фольклора).

**14. ひらいたひらいた (Hiraita hiraita)**, "it opened, it opened"
- Origin: Japan; warabe uta recorded around 1820 by Gyōchi in Edo's Asakusa area under the title "Renge", and now sung nationwide. Confidence: B, because it cites Gyōchi's Dōyōshū and a National Diet Library scan.
- Fit: 18-48 m; 3 or more; nothing needed; indoors or outdoors.
- Play: hold hands in a ring. Step out to make the ring big during "opened"; step in to make it small during "closed". The flower is a lotus, not milk vetch.
- Words (traditional): ひらいた ひらいた / なんのはなが ひらいた / れんげのはなが ひらいた / ひらいたと おもったら / いつのまにか つぼんだ; second verse swaps ひらいた and つぼんだ. ("It opened, it opened. What flower opened? The lotus opened. Just when we thought it had opened, before we knew it, it closed.")
- Builds: movement, language, feelings, thinking (opposites).
- Sources: [ひらいたひらいた, ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ひらいたひらいた).

**15. Häschen in der Grube**, "little hare in the hollow"
- Origin: German; words by Friedrich Fröbel, 1840, for his first kindergarten, to a tune adapted from a folk song. Authored but long out of copyright, so do not call it anonymous folk. Also known in Finland as "Jänis istui maassa". Confidence: B.
- Fit: 20-48 m; 3 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: children circle, holding hands, around one crouching "hare". On "hüpf!" the hare hops with hands as ears. Older children can add Fröbel's second verse and chase.
- Words: Häschen in der Grube saß und schlief, saß und schlief. / Armes Häschen, bist du krank, / dass du nicht mehr hüpfen kannst? / Häschen, hüpf! Häschen, hüpf! Häschen, hüpf! ("Little hare in the hollow sat and slept. Poor hare, are you ill, that you can't hop any more? Hare, hop!")
- Builds: movement, imagination, feelings.
- Sources: [Häschen in der Grube, de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Häschen_in_der_Grube).

**16. Kolo, kolo mlýnský**, "wheel, wheel, mill wheel"
- Origin: Czech pre-school ring game; the page itself compares it to Ring a Ring o' Roses and Ringelpiez, so make no unique-origin claim. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 24-54 m; 3 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: turn in a ring both ways while saying the rhyme; on "bác!" everyone sits down as the broken wheel. Older children add a verse to mend it and spin again.
- Words (traditional): Kolo, kolo mlýnský, / za čtyři rýnský, / kolo se nám polámalo, / mnoho škody nadělalo, / udělalo bác! ("Wheel, wheel, mill wheel, worth four Rhenish coins, our wheel has broken, did much damage, and went bang!") Second verse: Přineste nám hoblík, pilku, / zahrajem si ještě chvilku, / jak to kolo spravíme, / ještě se zatočíme. ("Bring a plane and saw, we'll play a bit more; when we mend the wheel we'll spin again.")
- Builds: movement, language, self-control.
- Overlap: Ring Around the Rosie and Ringel, Ringel, Reihe. The mending verse is what is new.
- Sources: [Kolo, kolo mlýnský, cs.wikipedia](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolo,_kolo_mlýnský).

**17. Katie Bairdie**, Scots action song with animal verses
- Origin: Scotland, in the national Bookbug programme run by Scottish Book Trust. Confidence: A for the actions; the page I read gave the actions but I did not capture the words, and it does not state public-domain status.
- Fit: 6-36 m; adult and child; nothing needed.
- Play: use your face and hands for each animal verse: gently stroke around baby's mouth on "around the moo", wrinkle your nose for "pig", open and shut your hand like a duck's bill on "had a duck". Dance on the chorus, or link arms and turn with an older child. Animal toys can join in.
- Builds: language (Scots), senses, movement, imagination.
- Sources: [Scottish Book Trust: Katie Bairdie](https://www.scottishbooktrust.com/songs-and-rhymes/katie-bairdie).

**18. Дай, бабо, огънче (Day, babo, oganche)**, "Granny, give me a little fire"
- Origin: Bulgaria; a text titled "Дете за огнец" was printed in Petko Slaveykov's 1883 first-grade reader. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 24-60 m; adult and child, or two children; nothing needed.
- Play: the "granny" spreads both hands and touches matching fingertips to make a ladder. The child walks a finger up rung by rung, saying "Бабо, дай огънче" ("Granny, give me fire"), and granny answers "Качи се по-нагоре" ("Climb higher"). At the top the child peeks into the house made by the hands and asks about the dark thing inside: "Is it the dog?" Granny answers "It's your grandfather's hat" (other answers are fine). On the final line the hands spring apart and the dog nips.
- Builds: language, imagination, hands, feelings, self-control.
- Safety/adapt: nip means a gentle squeeze.
- Sources: [Дай, бабо, огънче, bg.wikipedia](https://bg.wikipedia.org/wiki/Дай,_бабо,_огънче).

**19. จ้ำจี้มะเขือเปาะ (Jam ji makhuea po)**, counting-out rhyme named for a round eggplant
- Origin: Thailand; for small children, 5-7 players in the source. Confidence: C, because the cited university culture page is a dead link.
- Fit: 30-72 m; works with 2 or more; nothing needed; sit in a ring.
- Play: everyone puts one hand on the floor. One singer's forefinger taps round the fingers in time. Whose finger is touched on the last word curls that finger under. Go on until one player has most fingers tucked.
- Words (traditional): จ้ำจี้มะเขือเปาะ กระเทาะหน้าแว่น พายเรืออกแอ่น กระแท่นต้นกุ่ม สาว ๆ หนุ่ม ๆ อาบน้ำท่าไหน อาบน้ำท่าวัด เอาแป้งที่ไหนผัด เอากระจกที่ไหนส่อง เยี่ยม ๆ มอง ๆ นกขุนทองร้องกรู๊. Rough sense only, as much of it is sound-play: "Jam ji, round eggplant... paddling a boat... girls and boys, where do you bathe? At the temple pier. Where's your powder? Where's your mirror? Peep, peep, the myna bird calls groo."
- Builds: language, thinking (counting), self-control.
- Sources: [การละเล่นเด็กไทย, th.wikipedia](https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/การละเล่นเด็กไทย).

**20. Заинька (Zain'ka)**, "little bunny"
- Origin: Russian ring game recorded in E. A. Pokrovsky's study of children's games. Confidence: A, because this is a primary 19th-century text.
- Fit: 24-60 m; 4 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: one child is the bunny in the middle while the ring walks and sings that there is nowhere to jump out. On the commands the bunny dances, jumps and claps.
- Words (traditional): Заинька, серенький, / Некуда заиньке / Выскочити... Семеро ворот / Крепко заперты стоят... Заинька, серенький, / Попляши! ... Поскачи! ... В ладоши! ("Little grey bunny, nowhere to leap out... seven gates stand locked tight... little grey bunny, dance! jump! clap!")
- Builds: movement, imagination, language, feelings.
- Sources: [Покровский, Детские игры, Wikisource](https://ru.wikisource.org/wiki/Детские_игры,_преимущественно_русские_%28Покровский%29).

**21. Turi turi**, swinging-rope jump
- Origin: Maidhargari children (north-west central Queensland) made the rope from Bauhinia or white-gum roots; a vine rope was used by Wogadj children (Daly River). Confidence: A. See Question 5 for permissions.
- Fit: 36-84 m (Yulunga: Kindergarten to Year 3); about 6 players; a long rope or dressing-gown cord; outdoors or a clear room.
- Play: two players swing the rope side to side, never overhead. One child at a time jumps it once or a few times, moves away, and the next comes in.
- Builds: movement, self-control, feelings (turns).
- Safety/adapt: keep the rope low. With one adult, tie one end to a chair and wiggle it like a snake along the ground.
- Sources: [Yulunga, Australian Sports Commission](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga).

#### 3 to 5 years

**22. Stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi**, "the old bear is fast asleep"
- Origin: Poland; described by Maria Germanówna in a 1923 teachers' manual, to the tune of the folk song "Od Krakowa ciemny las". Confidence: B.
- Fit: 30-72 m; 4 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: one bear crouches and pretends to sleep. Others ring round on tiptoe, squatting at each "śpi". On "łapie!" everyone scatters and the bear chases. The first one touched becomes the bear.
- Words (traditional): Stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi (2x), / My się go boimy, na palcach chodzimy, / Jak się zbudzi to nas zje (2x). / Pierwsza godzina niedźwiedź śpi, / druga godzina niedźwiedź chrapie, / trzecia godzina niedźwiedź łapie! ("The old bear is fast asleep. We're afraid, we tiptoe; if he wakes he'll eat us. First hour the bear sleeps, second hour he snores, third hour he catches!")
- Builds: self-control, movement, feelings, language.
- Safety/adapt: indoors, the bear crawls and everyone crawls.
- Sources: [Stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi, pl.wikipedia](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stary_niedźwiedź_mocno_śpi).

**23. У медведя на бору (U medvedya na boru)**, "at the bear's in the pine wood"
- Origin: Russian chasing game recorded by Pokrovsky. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 36-72 m; 3 or more; a clear space.
- Play: one child dozes as the bear. Others gather pretend mushrooms and berries near him, singing. The bear stirs, stretches, shuffles out and at last chases. If nobody is caught for a while, he goes back and it restarts.
- Words (traditional): У медведя на бору / Грибы, ягоды беру! / Медведь постыл, / На печи застыл! ("At the bear's in the pine wood I pick mushrooms and berries! The tiresome bear lies stiff on the stove!")
- Builds: self-control, imagination, movement.
- Sources: [Покровский](https://ru.wikisource.org/wiki/Детские_игры,_преимущественно_русские_%28Покровский%29).

**24. Zakdoekje leggen**, "laying the little handkerchief"
- Origin: Netherlands and Flanders; oldest print in songbooks from the 1880s, 61 hits in the Boekenoogen collection, 99 records in the Meertens Institute's song bank. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 36-72 m; 5 or more, sitting in a ring; a cloth.
- Play: one child walks round the outside singing, drops the cloth behind someone and runs. The sitter must notice, grab the cloth and chase.
- Words (traditional): Zakdoekje leggen, / niemand zeggen, / 'k heb de hele nacht gewaakt, / twee paar schoenen heb ik afgemaakt, / één van stof en één van leer, / hier leg ik mijn zakdoekje neer. ("Lay the hanky, tell no one. I've been up all night and finished two pairs of shoes, one of cloth, one of leather; here I lay my hanky down.")
- Builds: self-control, senses, movement, language.
- Overlap: same family as entries 25-28; none is in the app under this mechanic. Choose one or two rather than all.
- Sources: [Zakdoekje leggen, nl.wikipedia](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakdoekje_leggen); [Nederlandse Liederenbank](https://www.liederenbank.nl/resultaatlijst.php?zoek=56962&actie=incipitnorm&lan=nl).

**25. มอญซ่อนผ้า (Mon son pha)**, "the Mon hides the cloth"
- Origin: Thailand. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 42-84 m; 5 or more; a cloth.
- Play: as entry 24. If the sitter notices, they chase the walker with the cloth; if not, the walker comes back round, taps them, and they swap.
- Words (traditional): มอญซ่อนผ้า ตุ๊กตาอยู่ข้างหลัง ไว้โน่นไว้นี่ ฉันจะตีก้นเธอ ("The Mon hides the cloth, the doll's behind you, put here, put there, I'll smack your bottom.")
- Safety/adapt: replace hitting with a tag; soften the last line.
- Sensitivity: "Mon" names an ethnic people, so check with a Thai reader that the framing is acceptable.
- Sources: [การละเล่นเด็กไทย](https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/การละเล่นเด็กไทย).

**26. លាក់កន្សែង (Leak Kanseng)**, "hiding the towel"
- Origin: Cambodia, a Khmer New Year game. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 42-84 m; 5 or more; a towel twisted round.
- Play: as entry 24, with a walker singing and a krama-style towel. In the source the chosen child beats the neighbour with the towel, so replace this with a chase.
- Sources: [Cambodian New Year, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodian_New_Year).

**27. Corre cutia**, "run, agouti"
- Origin: Brazil; many regional versions in Mapa do Brincar. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 42-96 m; 5 or more, seated in a circle; a ball.
- Play: one child runs round with a ball during the song, puts it behind someone when it ends, and that child chases. If caught, the runner pays a forfeit and stays out until they swap.
- Words (traditional, Araguari, MG): Corre cutia de noite e de dia / Debaixo da cama da sua tia / Comendo um pedaço de melancia / Pintinho piou: piu, piu / O galo cantou: corococó / Lencinho branco caiu no chão / Quem roubou foi o ladrão... **28. 丢手绢 (Diū shǒujuàn)**, "drop the handkerchief"
- Origin: China in this form, but the page records the same game in Germany, Britain (Gomme 1894), the USA, India, Ghana and Chile, and in medieval Europe, so make no Chinese-origin claim. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 42-84 m; 5 or more; a cloth.
- Play: as entry 24. The dropper aims to run one lap back to the gap.

**29. Brown Girl in the Ring**
- Origin: West Indies; said to originate in Jamaica; anthologised in "There's a Brown Girl in the Ring" (Lomax, Elder and Hawes). Confidence: B for the game, and the lyric is not verified here.
- Fit: 36-84 m; 5 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: children hold hands in a ring; one girl goes in and dances as the song directs; the others copy.
- Sensitivity: the 1978 Boney M. recording credited Frank Farian and led to a long arrangement dispute in Germany, so do not use that version. Take words only from the Lomax anthology or a Caribbean cultural body.

**30. 福笑い (Fukuwarai)**, "lucky laugh"
- Origin: Japan; New Year game; Japan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs children's site calls it centuries old, while Japanese Wikipedia says it settled as a New Year game around the Meiji era and origin is unclear. Say "traditional New Year game" and avoid a date. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 36-96 m; 2 or more; paper, crayons, scissors, a scarf; indoors.
- Play: draw a face outline. Cut out eyes, brows, nose and mouth. Blindfold a player, who puts the pieces on one at a time. Everyone laughs at the funny face; you can award the funniest or the most correct.
- Builds: hands, senses, feelings, language (left, up, down hints), imagination.
- Safety/adapt: under-4s can shut their eyes instead of a blindfold. The page's face is Okame/Otafuku, but any face is fine. Use your own family's faces for fun.
- Sources: [Kids Web Japan: Fukuwarai](https://web-japan.org/kidsweb/virtual/fukuwarai/index.html); [福笑い](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/福笑い).

**31. Boca de forno**, "oven's mouth"
- Origin: Brazil; this version from Sapucaia do Sul, RS, with several versions in the collection. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 42-96 m; 3 or more; household objects; indoors or out.
- Play: a chosen "king" calls, the group answers, and then the king orders a fetch, such as three pebbles. Tasks get harder.
- Words (traditional): "Boca de forno." "Forno." "Tirando bolo?" "Bolo." "Quando eu mandar..." "Vou." "E se não for?" "Não ganha." "Senhor rei mandou trazer três pedrinhas." ("Oven's mouth! Oven. Taking out cake? Cake. When I command... I go. And if you don't? You don't win. The lord king orders three pebbles.")
- Builds: language, thinking, movement, self-control. Great for sibling groups.
- Sources: [Mapa do Brincar: Boca de forno 1](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/diversas/665-boca-de-forno-1).

**32. Koabangan**, hide the object
- Origin: Kokominni boys, north Queensland, seen in the early 1900s; the object was commonly a goanna claw. Yulunga suggests it for younger children. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 42-96 m (K-6); 4 or more; a ball or toy; outdoors or a room.
- Play: players sit in a circle with heads low and hands over their faces. One hides the object and signals. Everyone jumps up to search. The finder hides next. Variation: "hot" and "cold" hints.

**33. Waayin**, animal tracks
- Origin: from the Datiwuy word for land animals and reptiles (northern NT); adults drew tracks in smoothed sand to teach children, and children competed to copy them. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 48-120 m (K-6); 2 or more; sand, soil, or a tray of flour or salt; a stick; outdoors or kitchen table.
- Play: one person draws tracks of several animals; others guess and comment. Extend by photographing real tracks.
- Builds: thinking, senses, hands, imagination. Use your own local animals.
- Sources: [Yulunga](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga).

**34. Regina reginella**, "queen, little queen"
- Origin: Italy; the page is flagged as nearly unsourced, though it links RAI. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 48-96 m; 3 or more; a clear stretch of floor.
- Play: one queen at the far end. Each player asks in turn; the queen answers with a count and animal ("5 lion steps", "4 kangaroo steps", ant steps, or backward crab steps). First to reach her becomes queen.
- Words (traditional): Regina reginella, quanti passi devo fare / per arrivare al tuo castello così grande così bello? ("Queen, little queen, how many steps must I take to reach your castle so big, so beautiful?")
- Builds: language, movement, imagination, thinking (counting), feelings (fairness: the queen holds real power).
- Sources: [Regina reginella, it.wikipedia](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regina_reginella).

**35. Topfschlagen**, "hitting the pot"
- Origin: Germany; children's party game. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 36-84 m; 3 or more; a saucepan, wooden spoon, small treat, scarf; indoors.
- Play: blindfold a child, give them a spoon and turn them round. Hide a treat under an upturned pot. They crawl, tapping the floor; others call "warm" and "cold". When the spoon hits the pot, they take the prize.
- Builds: senses, language, self-control.
- Safety/adapt: crawling keeps it safe; clear the floor. Leave out the game's history, which involved a live cockerel at fairs.
- Sources: [Topfschlagen, de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topfschlagen).

**36. おしくらまんじゅう (Oshikura manjū)**, "push-push steamed bun"
- Origin: Japan; a cold-weather warm-up game, and the page is tagged as needing sources. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 36-96 m; 4 or more (the source says fewer does not warm you up); nothing needed.
- Play: form a ring facing out, arms linked. On the signal, sing and push with backs and bottoms, into the centre then out.
- Words (traditional): おしくらまんじゅう、押されて泣くな ("Push-push bun, don't cry when you get pushed.")
- Builds: movement, senses, feelings, self-control.
- Safety/adapt: match sizes; a surface with room to fall; stop on "stop". With a toddler it can be a gentle back-to-back wiggle.
- Sources: [おしくらまんじゅう, ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/おしくらまんじゅう).

**37. Chi chi chành chành**
- Origin: Vietnam; popular folk finger game. Confidence: C. The page holds two conflicting scholarly-sounding origin theories; report neither as fact.
- Fit: 36-96 m; 3 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: one player holds out an open palm and the others place a forefinger in it. As the rhyme ends, they pull out fast and the palm snaps shut. Whoever is caught holds out the palm next.
- Words (traditional): Chi chi chành chành / Cái đanh thổi lửa / Con ngựa chết trương / Ba vương ngũ đế / Bắt dế đi tìm / Ù à ù ập. (Partly nonsense: "...the nail blows the fire, the horse lies dead, three kings five emperors, catch a cricket and go look, oo-ah-oo-CATCH.")

- Sources: [Chi chi chành chành, vi.wikipedia](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi_chi_chành_chành).

**38. Sawsaw suka**, "dip in the vinegar"
- Origin: Philippines; used to choose who is "it". Confidence: C.

**39. ずいずいずっころばし (Zuizui zukkorobashi)**
- Origin: Japan; warabe uta, selected in 2006 for the Agency for Cultural Affairs and Japan PTA "Nihon no Uta Hyakusen". Confidence: B.
- Fit: 36-84 m; 2 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: each player makes loose fists with a hole. One player dips a finger into each hole in turn, on the beat. The fist reached at the end is chosen. Often used to pick "it".
- Words (traditional): ずいずいずっころばし / ごまみそずい / 茶壺に追われて / とっぴんしゃん / 抜けたら、どんどこしょ / 俵のねずみが / 米食ってちゅう、/ ちゅうちゅうちゅう / おっとさんがよんでも、/ おっかさんがよんでも、/ 行きっこなしよ / 井戸のまわりで、/ お茶碗欠いたのだぁれ. (Largely nonsense. "...sesame miso... chased by the tea jar, slam the door; once past, phew! The mouse in the rice bale eats rice, squeak, squeak; though father calls, though mother calls, no going out. Who chipped the tea bowl by the well?")
- Sensitivity: the usual reading links it to the Edo tea-jar procession, but the page notes the song is absent from Edo documents, and one theory reads it sexually. Do not explain the meaning; say "meaning is debated" if at all.
- Sources: [ずいずいずっころばし, ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ずいずいずっころばし).

**40. Nu na nu nống**
- Origin: Vietnam; the page claims pre-Han origin with no source, so do not repeat it. Confidence: C.

- Words: the rhyme text was not on the page I could read; get it from a Vietnamese đồng dao collection.

**41. Galinha e pintinhos**, "hen and chicks"
- Origin: Brazil; from Guará, SP. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 48-84 m; 5 or more; a yard.

**42. Гуси-лебеди (Gusi-lebedi)**, "geese-swans"
- Origin: Russia; recorded by Pokrovsky. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 48-84 m; 4 or more; a yard.

**43. Blind-chase family: Bịt mắt bắt dê (Vietnam), Жмурки/Опанас (Russia, Ukraine), Gallina ciega (Spain, Latin America), Τυφλόμυγα (Greece), โพงพาง (Thailand)**
- Origin: found across many cultures; the Vietnamese page puts it in ancient Greece and Tudor England, and Goya painted the Spanish form. Do not assign one origin. Confidence: B for the family, C for each local page.
- Fit: 60-108 m; 4 or more; a scarf; a cleared space.
- Play: blindfold one player and turn them. Others move and call. The seeker catches, sometimes names, and swaps.
- Local words (traditional). Vietnam: Một bầy trẻ nhỏ / Bịt mắt bắt dê / Dê vấp bờ hè / Ngã kềnh bốn vó... ("A crowd of little ones, blindfolded, catch the goat; the goat trips at the porch and falls on all fours..."). Southern Russia and eastern Ukraine: «Опанас, Опанас, лови мух, а не нас!» ("Opanas, catch flies, not us!"). Thailand: โพงพางเอ๋ย สำเภาเข้าลอด ปูปลาตาบอด เข้าลอดโพงพาง, then "Live fish or dead fish?"; if "dead", nobody may move, and the seeker must name who they touch.
- Safety/adapt: clear furniture, no stairs; a spotter; let children close their eyes instead if they dislike blindfolds.
- Sources: [Bịt mắt bắt dê](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bịt_mắt_bắt_dê); [Жмурки](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Жмурки); [Gallina ciega](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallina_ciega); [Τυφλόμυγα](https://el.wikipedia.org/wiki/Τυφλόμυγα); [โพงพาง in การละเล่นเด็กไทย](https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/การละเล่นเด็กไทย).

**44. 通りゃんせ (Tōryanse)**, "please pass through"
- Origin: Japan; thought to be Edo-period, with an arrangement credited to Motoori Nagayo and a disputed attribution to Noguchi Ujō on a 1920-era recording. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 48-84 m; 4 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: two children make an arch; the rest file under while singing; on the last line the arch drops and catches someone. (The arch action comes from the family, and the page describes it only as a play song.)
- Words (traditional): 通りゃんせ 通りゃんせ / ここはどこの 細道じゃ / 天神さまの 細道じゃ / ちっと通して 下しゃんせ / 御用のないもの 通しゃせぬ / この子の七つの お祝いに / お札を納めに まいります / 行きはよいよい 帰りはこわい / こわいながらも / 通りゃんせ 通りゃんせ. ("Pass through. Whose narrow path is this? Tenjin's path. Please let us through. No one without business may pass. For this child's seventh birthday we bring a talisman. Going is easy; coming back is scary. Though it's scary, pass through.")
- Sensitivity: online theories link it to spirit abductions or discrimination; the page calls these unfounded, so do not repeat them.
- Sources: [通りゃんせ, ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/通りゃんせ).

**45. Bro bro breja**, "bridge, bridge, broad"
- Origin: Sweden, with versions worldwide; known by the 17th century; related to London Bridge. Page cites the Dialect and Folklore Archive in Uppsala. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 48-84 m; 6 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: two arch-keepers secretly pick "gold" and "silver". Others file under; the arch drops on the last word. The captive is rocked, whispers a choice and lines up behind that keeper. When all are caught, the sides have a tug of war.
- Words (traditional): Bro bro breja / stockar och stenar / alla goda renar / ingen kommer här fram, här fram / förrän han säger sin kärestas namn, / vad heter han? Then: Har du tagit prästens sko, ... ja eller nej? ("Bridge, broad bridge; logs and stones; all good boundaries; no one comes through until he names his sweetheart. What's his name? ... Did you take the priest's shoe, yes or no?")
- Safety/adapt: swap "sweetheart" for "favourite animal". Soft tug only.
- Sources: [Bro bro breja, sv.wikipedia](https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bro_bro_breja); [London Bridge, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Bridge_Is_Falling_Down).

**46. รีรีข้าวสาร (Ri ri khao san)**, "rice, milled rice"
- Origin: Thailand. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 48-84 m; 5 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: two make an arch; others hold waists in a line and pass under, singing. On the last word the arch drops. The caught child is out or does a little dance.
- Words (traditional): รีรีข้าวสาร สองทะนานข้าวเปลือก เลือกท้องใบลาน คดข้าวใส่จาน เก็บเบี้ยใต้ถุนร้าน พานเอาคนข้างหลังไว้ให้ดี ("Milled rice, two measures of paddy, choose the palm leaf, scoop rice on a plate, gather cowries under the shop; catch the one behind and hold tight.")
- Sources: [การละเล่นเด็กไทย](https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/การละเล่นเด็กไทย).

**47. Bom barqueiro**, "good boatman"
- Origin: Brazil; Cuiabá, MT, with several versions. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 48-84 m; 5 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: as the arch family; ends when no one is left to catch.
- Words (traditional): Passa, passa, bom barqueiro / Deixa nós passar / Eu tenho muito filho para criar / Não posso mais esperar / Passa, passa pela última ficará / Se não for a da frente, há de ser a de trás. **48. Adoleta**
- Origin: Brazil, where the page attributes it to French immigrants ("un, deux et trois" or "andouillette", with "lepeti" from "le petit"); this etymology rests on non-scholarly sources. Treat it as Brazilian and unsettled. Confidence: A for the Mapa version's rules.
- Fit: 60-120 m; 3 or more, or 2 for the pair version; nothing needed.
- Play: in a ring, right hand on the left hand of the neighbour. Each syllable passes the clap round. The player about to be hit on the final "u" tries to pull away: if they do, the hitter is out; if not, they are out. Last player wins.
- Words (traditional, São Paulo): Adoleta / Lepeti, peti, cola / Puxa o rabo do tatu / Quem saiu foi tu / Com a letra "u". ("...Pull the armadillo's tail; who's out is you; with the letter U.") Another common variant: "Le café com chocolá".
- Builds: self-control, language, movement, feelings.
- Sources: [Mapa do Brincar: Adoleta 1](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/palmas/372-adoleta-1); [Adoleta, pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoleta).

**49. Escravos de Jó**, "Job's slaves"
- Origin: Brazil. Origin and meaning are unsettled: Massarani (1952) linked the tune to an Italian original, similar games exist in Catalonia and Aragon ("Els esclops d'en Pau"), and Nair Spinelli Lauria credits French sailors. Villa-Lobos harmonised it in the 1930s (Guia Prático no. 55). Confidence: A; the pt.wikipedia page is unusually well referenced.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 4 or more; a cup or block each for the passing version; table or floor.
- Play, hand version from Mapa: hands in a ring, each above one neighbour's and below the other's; slap on the beat; "tira" pulls the hand away, "põe" returns it, and "zig-zig" reverses direction; whoever is hit when the song ends goes to the middle, and the last one left wins.
- Play, passing version: the pt.wikipedia page says only that players sit in a circle, each with an object, pass it on the beat, and hold or move it differently at certain points. The usual moves as I know them, not confirmed by a source I read: lift on "tira", put down on "põe", leave it on "deixa ficar", and on "zigue, zigue, zá" move it across and back without letting go. Verify before publishing.
- Words (traditional, Tatuí, SP): Escravos de Jó / Jogavam Caxangá / Tira, põe, deixa ficar / Guerreiros com guerreiros / fazem zig-zig-zá. ("Job's slaves played caxangá. Take off, put on, leave it there. Warriors with warriors go zig-zig-zag.")
- Builds: self-control, movement, thinking, feelings. This is the best cup-rhythm game in this set; see Question 4.
- Sensitivity: the word "slaves" matters in Brazil's history. Name the song, say its origin is debated, and do not make light of it. Consider leading with the object-passing rhythm and a one-line note. A sensitivity review by a Brazilian reader is worthwhile.
- Sources: [Mapa do Brincar](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/roda/562-escravos-de-jo); [Escravos de Jó, pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escravos_de_Jó).

**50. Nanay, Tatay**, "Mum, Dad"
- Origin: Philippines; clapping song for two or four players. Confidence: C, because the lyric is cited to a personal blog.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 2 or 4; nothing needed.
- Play: clap a partner pattern through the verse, then do a set number of claps (5, then more each round). Whoever slips is out.
- Words (traditional): Nanay, Tatay, gusto ko tinapay / Ate, Kuya, gusto ko kape, / Lahat ng gusto ko ay susundin niyo. / Ang magkamali ay pipingutin ko... ("Mum, Dad, I want bread. Big sister, big brother, I want coffee. Everything I want you'll do. Whoever slips, I'll pinch their ear...")

- Sources: [Traditional games in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_in_the_Philippines).

**51. Sāsā**, "to strike"
- Origin: Samoa; a seated group dance of precise claps and hand moves showing daily life (cooking, paddling canoes, cleaning, fish, birds). Performed by all ages and genders. History is poorly documented. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 48-144 m; 2 or more; nothing needed.
- Play: this is not a game with rules, so frame it as "inspired by". Sit together; a leader calls; all clap thighs and hands in time and show an everyday action together; speed up.
- Sensitivity: real sāsā is performance culture with a fa'aluma leader, and it mimes the 'ava ceremony; leave that out and do not present a home version as authentic. Credit Samoa and invite families to watch a real group.

**52. Statue-sneak family: だるまさんがころんだ (Japan), 무궁화 꽃이 피었습니다 (Korea), 一二三木头人 (China), Ochs am Berg (Austria and German lands), Un, due, tre, stella! (Italy), Batatinha frita 1, 2, 3 (Brazil)**

**53. Rồng rắn lên mây**, "the dragon-snake climbs the clouds", with งูกินหาง (Ngu kin hang), "the snake eats its tail" (Thailand)
- Origin: Vietnam, and a Yunnan version appears on the same page; Thai version recorded at Ayutthaya Songkran in 1932. China's 老鷹捉小雞 (eagle catches chicks) and Japan's 比比丘女 are relatives. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 60-96 m; 5 or more; a yard.
- Play: one is the doctor; the rest form a line holding waists and wind round singing. The doctor says "not home" twice, then "home", and they talk: "Where are you going?" "To buy medicine." "For whom?" "For my child." "How old?" "One", "Two"... Then: "I'll take the head." "All bones." "The middle." "All blood." "The tail." "Chase all you like!" - Overlap: Catch the Dragon's Tail is already in the app; the doctor dialogue is what is new. Add as a variant.
- Safety/adapt: walking pace; firm no-pull rule on younger children. Thai source notes the mixed-gender holding was once seen as improper.
- Sources: [Rồng rắn lên mây](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rồng_rắn_lên_mây); [งูกินหาง](https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/งูกินหาง); [老鷹捉小雞](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/老鹰捉小鸡).

**55. Tabud nuri**, "snake coiling"

**57. Inkanyi**, "play", finish-together race

**58. Segur etug**, "play"
- Origin: Mer Island, Torres Strait; from the Meryam Mir word. Confidence: A.

**59. Tok**, "to speak", secret language
- Origin: reported on Waiben (Thursday) Island in the early 1970s among girls speaking Torres Strait Creole, and later on Dauan. Confidence: A.

**60. Cublak-cublak suweng**

**61. Hompimpa and suit (gajah-orang-semut)**

**62. あっち向いてホイ (Acchi muite hoi)**, "look that way, hup"
- Origin: Japan; derived from rock-paper-scissors; said to have begun as a Gion party game and spread on 1970s television. Do not call it ancient. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 48-132 m; 2; nothing needed; car and queue friendly.
- Play: play janken. The winner says "acchi muite hoi" and points up, down, left or right as the loser turns their face. If they match, the pointer wins; if not, play janken again.

**63. あんたがたどこさ (Antagata dokosa)**, "where are you from?"
- Origin: Japan; warabe uta temari song, set in Kumamoto per one account and Kawagoe per another. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 60-108 m; 1 or more; a bouncy ball.
- Play: bounce the ball to the beat. On every "sa", swing a leg over the ball. On the last line, crouch and cover the ball with your skirt or hakama; the page says it fails if the ball rolls out and that it does not work in trousers, so holding it between the knees is my adaptation.
- Words (traditional): あんたがたどこさ 肥後さ 肥後どこさ 熊本さ 熊本どこさ 船場さ / 船場山には狸がおってさ それを猟師が鉄砲で撃ってさ 煮てさ 焼いてさ 食ってさ それを木の葉でちょいと隠せ. ("Where are you from? Higo. Where in Higo? Kumamoto. Where? Senba. On Senba hill lives a raccoon dog; the hunter shot it; stew it, roast it, eat it; cover it with a leaf.") A shrimp version also exists.
- Builds: movement, language, thinking (rhythm).
- Safety/adapt: the shrimp version is gentler. Non-bouncing version: pass a beanbag under a leg on "sa".
- Sources: [あんたがたどこさ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/あんたがたどこさ).

**64. เดินกะลา (Doen kala)**, "walking on coconut shells"
- Origin: Thailand, with regional names (เดินหมากกุบกับ, กุบกับ) and an English name set by the Royal Institute. Confidence: B/C. Coconut-shell or can walkers are found widely; make no sole-origin claim.

- Safety/adapt: flat ground, shoes on, no stairs. Keep cords short enough not to trip. Adults punch holes and smooth tin edges.

**65. Koekhappen**, "biting the cake"

**66. ចោលឈូង (Chol Chhoung)**, "throw the scarf ball"
- Origin: Cambodia; played especially on the first night of Khmer New Year by two groups of 10 to 20. Confidence: C.

**67. Chơi chuyền / đánh chuyền**, stick-and-ball jacks
- Origin: Vietnam; especially popular with girls. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 1 or more; 10 chopsticks or sticks about 20 cm, plus a small ball (tennis ball, lemon or citrus fruit); a floor.
- Play: toss the ball, scatter the sticks with the other hand, catch. Round 1: toss, pick one stick, catch. Round 2: pick two. Go on to nine. Dropped stick passes the turn. Later rounds tap sticks on the palm ("chấm chấm một") and knock them ("gõ gõ một").

**69. Line-guard family: Patintero (Philippines), Gobak sodor/Galah asin (Indonesia), Htote si toe (Myanmar)**
- Origin: separate local traditions with shared mechanics; Wikipedia also links Atya patya (India). The Indonesian page contradicts itself (Yogyakarta versus West Java), so say "Java". Confidence: B for Patintero (Wikipedia plus Playworks), C for the others.
- Fit: 84-144 m; 4-12; chalk, water or tape; a yard 5-6 m long by about 4 m wide.
- Play: draw a rectangle divided into squares. Taggers must keep both feet on their line. Runners cross and return. Tagged runners are out or swap. In Patintero, the team leader (patotot) can also use the centre line.
- Builds: movement, thinking (strategy), feelings (teamwork), self-control.
- Safety/adapt: Playworks' school version keeps runners moving in a recycle line rather than out, and uses tagging by touch.
- Sources: [Patintero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patintero); [Playworks](https://www.playworks.org/game-library/patintero/); [Gobak sodor](https://id.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gobak_sodor); [Traditional games of Myanmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_of_Myanmar).

**70. Tumbang preso**, "knock down the prisoner"
- Origin: Philippines. Confidence: B/C.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 3-9; an empty plastic bottle or tin in a small circle; a slipper or beanbag each; a yard.
- Play: "it" (taya) guards the can. Others throw from a toe-line to knock it down, then run for their throwers. It must stand the can back up before tagging. A player tagged while fetching becomes it.
- Safety/adapt: beanbags and a plastic bottle; no throwing near faces.
- Sources: [Tumbang preso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumbang_preso); [Traditional games in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_in_the_Philippines).

**71. Luksong tinik**, "jump over the thorns"
- Origin: Philippines. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 84-144 m, with mats; 3 or more; soft ground.
- Play: two players sit soles and hands stacked to make a barrier that grows. Others run and jump without touching.
- Safety/adapt: high-risk as played. Use a pool noodle or elastic held at rising heights, and never jump over a person's body. Luksong baka (leap the crouching player) is riskier still; omit it for home use.
- Sources: [Traditional games in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_in_the_Philippines).

**72. Langit-lupa**, "heaven and earth"
- Origin: Philippines. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 60-120 m; 3 or more; a yard with safe low things to stand on.
- Play: "it" can only tag players on the ground; those on raised things are safe. A tagged player becomes it.

**73. 고누 (Gonu)**, from a word meaning "to contest"
- Origin: Korea; a Goryeo-period kiln relic bears a board, and a Kim Hong-do painting (18th c., National Museum of Korea) may show boys playing the well version, though one reading says it is a different game. Similar games exist in China, Japan, Mongolia and India. Confidence: A.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 2; a board drawn on paper or soil; four pieces or more.
- Play, well gonu (우물고누), the simplest: a small crossing board with a "well" on one side, two pieces each, moved along lines. Whoever gets stuck loses. The first move has one restriction (the page's diagram is needed to state it exactly). Harder forms: four-line gonu (capture by lining up) and gonjil gonu, which resembles nine men's morris.
- Builds: thinking, self-control.
- Sources: [고누, Encyclopedia of Korean Culture](https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0003367); [고누, ko.wikipedia](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/고누).

**74. Mū tōrere**
- Origin: Māori, mainly North Island. Confidence: B. See Question 5.
- Fit: 96-144 m; 2; an eight-pointed star on paper or sand; four counters each.
- Play: counters start on the points, side by side by colour; the centre (pūtahi) is empty. Move one counter to an adjacent empty point, or to the centre only if the moving counter is next to an opponent's. Whoever blocks the other wins.
- Builds: thinking. Mū tōrere has 1,180 reachable positions, and the start position is a draw with best play.
- Sensitivity: the page says it is tightly interwoven with stories and histories. Credit Māori and seek a Māori reviewer before publishing.
- Sources: [Mū tōrere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C5%AB_t%C5%8Drere).

**75. Kōnane**
- Origin: Hawaiʻi; recorded in the Kumulipo and noted by Cook's expedition; the Bishop Museum hosted the Ka Makou Kōnane Invitational in February 2026. Confidence: A/B.
- Fit: 84-144 m; 2; a grid (6 × 6 is enough) and two-colour counters, such as beans or buttons.
- Play: fill the board in alternating colours. Black removes one of its pieces from the middle or a corner. White removes an adjacent one. Then players jump orthogonally over an opponent's piece into an empty space, capturing it, and may make several jumps in one line without changing direction. Whoever cannot capture loses.
- Builds: thinking, self-control.
- Sensitivity: do not call it "Hawaiian checkers". Advocate and tournament co-organiser Kapena Baptista explains the label came from Cook's crew and misrepresents the game's values.
- Sources: [Kōnane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dnane); [Hawaiʻi Public Radio, Feb 2026](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/the-conversation/2026-02-23/konane-players-perpetuate-hawaiian-strategy-game-at-inaugural-tournament).

**76. Ô ăn quan**, "mandarin squares"
- Origin: Vietnam. Confidence: B/C.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 2 (sometimes more); a rectangle of 10 squares with a half-round at each end, drawn on paper or pavement; about 50 small pieces plus 2 big "mandarins".
- Play: 5 pieces in each square; one mandarin in each half-round. Pick up a whole square on your side and sow one by one in your chosen direction. If the next square has pieces, pick them up and go on. If the next square is empty and the one after has pieces, capture them. A mandarin is usually worth 10 or 5 pieces. Most at the end wins.
- Overlap: Oware is in the app. Add only as a Vietnamese variant, with its own board.
- Sources: [Ô ăn quan](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ô_ăn_quan).

**77. Sungka / congkak / congklak / dakon**
- Origin: maritime Southeast Asia: Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, Brunei, Thailand (หมากขุม), with Tamil and Maldivian relatives; differs from other mancalas in that your store takes seeds. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 84-144 m; 2; an egg carton with 2 bowls; shells, beans or buttons.
- Play: 7 pits each and a store each. Sow clockwise, skipping the opponent's store. Land in a non-empty pit: scoop and continue. Land in your store: extra move. Land in your own empty pit: capture the seeds opposite. Land in an opponent's empty pit: turn ends. Most stones wins.
- Overlap: Oware. Add as a variant.
- Sources: [Southeast Asian mancala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asian_mancala); [Traditional games in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_games_in_the_Philippines).

**78. Jogo da onça / Adugo**, "jaguar game"
- Origin: Indigenous Brazil: Boe (Bororo) of Mato Grosso, with Manchineri and Guarani also recorded; it resembles an Inca puma game and Nepal's Bagh-Chal. Confidence: B. Ciência Hoje das Crianças covers the Jogos Indígenas do Brasil project.
- Fit: 84-144 m; 2; a board drawn in sand or on paper; 1 jaguar and 14 dogs.
- Play: the jaguar starts at the centre and moves first. Pieces move one point at a time along lines. The jaguar captures by short jumps as in draughts; dogs cannot capture. The jaguar wins by taking 5 dogs; the dogs win by trapping it.
- Overlap: Tigers and Goats is in the app; the board and people differ.
- Sensitivity: credit the Boe (Bororo) people by name. The board diagram must come from a good source.
- Sources: [Jogo da onça](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jogo_da_onça); [Adugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adugo); [CHC: Brincadeira na aldeia](https://chc.org.br/brincadeira-na-aldeia/).

**79. Patolli**
- Origin: pre-Columbian Mesoamerica (Teotihuacan, Toltec, Aztec, Maya); a race game with heavy gambling. Strictly, the name means the Aztec version known from codices; rulesets for boards found elsewhere are unknown. Confidence: B for the history, C for any playable rules.
- Fit: 108-144 m as a history-and-maths activity; 2-4; a drawn X-shaped board of 52 squares; black beans marked on one side, used as dice.
- Play: toss the beans. One hole showing brings a marker onto the board; later tosses move it clockwise round the X by the number of holes showing. First all the way round wins. This is a reconstruction, so label it as such.
- Sensitivity: strong ties to gambling and possibly religious meaning. Play without stakes and say "modern reconstruction". Consider leaving out of the app.
- Sources: [Patolli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patolli); [Mexicolore: Classic Maya patolli](https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/maya/home/classic-maya-patolli).

**80. Шагай (Shagai) horse race**, ankle-bone game
- Origin: Mongolia, Central Asia (shagai, chükö, asyk). UNESCO inscribed the related adult team sport, Mongolian knuckle-bone shooting, in 2014. Confidence: A for the heritage, C for the home race.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 2 or more; sheep or goat ankle bones (four sides read as horse, camel, sheep, goat).
- Play: lay a line of bones as a track. Each player flicks their "horse" bone along the line in turn, racing to the end.
- Safety/adapt: not for under-3s. If bones are not available, four-sided clay or wooden pieces with animal drawings are an adaptation, not the tradition.
- Sensitivity: UNESCO notes Mongolians revere these bones and use them in rites; handle them respectfully, and say so.
- Sources: [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/mongolian-knuckle-bone-shooting-00959); [Shagai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shagai).

**81. 비석치기 (Biseok-chigi)**, "stone hitting"
- Origin: Korea; origin unclear, with folk accounts on the page and a link to the National Folk Museum's encyclopedia. Confidence: B/C.
- Fit: 84-144 m; 2 or more; a flat stone or thick book or beanbag each; two lines about 2 m apart.
- Play: opponents stand their stones on the far line. Stage 1: throw yours to knock one over. Later stages carry your stone on a foot, between knees, at the waist, on the belly, on a shoulder, on a cheek, on the head with eyes shut, then drop it to knock theirs down.
- Builds: movement, senses, self-control, feelings.
- Safety/adapt: beanbags and foam blocks indoors, with heads and eyes kept safe.
- Sources: [비석치기](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/비석치기).

**82. 딱지치기 (Ddakji-chigi)**, "paper tile hitting"
- Origin: Korea (North and South), at least Joseon-era per the pages; Japan's menko is a relative with an Edo-period history. Confidence: B/C.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 2 or more; two sheets of stiff paper each.
- Play: fold a square tile. Loser of rock-paper-scissors sets theirs down; the other slams theirs to flip it. If flipped, you win it, or swap.
- Sensitivity: Squid Game again; do not reference it, especially the slap. Make clear there is no betting.
- Sources: [딱지 (장난감)](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/딱지_%28장난감%29); [Ddakji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ddakji); [めんこ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/めんこ).

**83. おはじき (Ohajiki)**, "flicks"
- Origin: Japan; said to have come from China in the Nara period, with shells and pebbles in earlier forms, and glass discs from the late Meiji period. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 60-108 m; 2 or more; flat glass marbles are about 12 mm, so use buttons or bottle caps at home with younger siblings around.
- Play: flick one piece at another with your finger; a hit keeps it. Variations: flick between two pieces, or into a drawn area.
- Builds: hands, thinking, self-control.
- Safety/adapt: not with under-3s around.
- Sources: [おはじき](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/おはじき).

**84. Sjoelen**, Dutch shove-board
- Origin: Netherlands; an old Dutch game, and now an organised sport. The 17th-century date on the page is flagged as uncited. Confidence: B.
- Fit: 72-144 m; 1 or more; a long table or plank, books for walls, four card gates; 30 discs or jar lids.
- Play: slide discs through four gates worth 2, 3, 4, 1 from left to right. Three rounds, reusing discs that failed to enter. Score the gate values, except that one disc in every gate counts as 20 rather than 10, two in every gate as 40, and so on.
- Builds: hands, thinking (adding), self-control.
- Sources: [Sjoelen](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sjoelen).

**85. Juego del sapo / de la rana**, "toad" or "frog" game
- Origin: Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Argentina, Uruguay as sapo; Spain and Chile as rana; Colombia as rana or bolirana. An Inca-legend origin is claimed by some, so do not state it as fact. Often played in bars, per photographs on the page. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 60-144 m (the page's infobox gives 9+); 2 or more; a cardboard box with holes, a frog face over the best hole; beanbags or coins for older children.
- Play: throw from a line, ten tosses. Frog's mouth scores most.
- Sources: [Sapo, es.wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapo_%28juego%29).

**86. Ручеёк (Ruchyok)**, "little stream"
- Origin: Russia; among the most popular Russian town games by the mid-19th century, with a Belarusian relative ("razluchki"). Confidence: C.
- Fit: 72-144 m (the page says 7+); 8 or more; a hall or yard; music if you like.
- Play: pairs hold hands high to form a tunnel. A lone player enters, picks a partner, and they run to the far end. The left-out one enters next. Faster is funnier.
- Sensitivity: historically a courting game; for children, let them pick any partner.
- Sources: [Ручеёк](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ручеёк_%28игра%29).

**87. はないちもんめ (Hana ichimonme)**, "flowers worth one monme"
- Origin: Japan; spread in the early Shōwa era, with roots in older games. A theory links it to the sale of people, though this is contested. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 60-108 m; 6 or more; a yard.
- Play: two lines hold hands and walk toward each other in turn singing. Each team privately picks someone it "wants". The two named play janken; the winner's team gets the player. Ends when one side is gone.
- Words (traditional): かってうれしいはないちもんめ / まけてくやしいはないちもんめ / あの子がほしい / あの子じゃわからん / 相談しましょ / そうしましょ. ("We won, glad, a flower for one monme. We lost, sad... We want that child. Can't tell which child. Let's talk it over.")
- Sensitivity: being chosen last can hurt; use with care, or skip for the app.
- Sources: [はないちもんめ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/はないちもんめ).

**88. 강강술래 (Ganggangsullae)**
- Origin: south-western Korea (Jeolla coast); a women's moonlit ring dance around Chuseok, inscribed by UNESCO. The Admiral Yi origin is a folk etymology. Confidence: A for existence, B for details.
- Fit: 72-144 m as a family ring; 5 or more; outdoors; nothing needed.
- Play: join hands; a leader sings a line; all answer "ganggangsullae" as the ring starts slowly and speeds up. Mid-dance games include gosari-kkeokgi ("bracken picking"), cheong-eo-yeokgi ("stringing herring"), giwa-balgi ("treading tiles"), kkori-ttagi ("tail catching").
- Sensitivity: it is a living, women-led heritage. Describe a "family ring inspired by" it, with the refrain, and link to UNESCO's video. No lyrics are in my notes beyond the refrain.
- Sources: [UNESCO: Ganggangsullae](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/ganggangsullae-00188); [강강술래](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/강강술래).

**89. あやとり / 翻花绳 / 실뜨기 (Ayatori, fān huā shéng, sil-tteugi)**: see Question 4.

**90. Kamai and wame**, Aboriginal and Torres Strait string figures: see Question 4.

**91. Poi**
- Origin: Māori; usually practised by women; recent academic work found no evidence for the legend that men used it for weapon wrist training. Confidence: B. The New Zealand curriculum activity collection exists but blocked my access.
- Fit: 60-144 m; 1 or more; a ball of scrunched paper in a plastic bag or fabric, on a cord fingertip-to-wrist length for short poi; open space.
- Play: swing in circles forward, then to the side, then to a beat; two poi later; add a counting song.
- Sensitivity: traditional poi used harakeke and raupō and go with waiata and storytelling. Make clear this is a home practice toy. Use the correct term; credit Māori. Do not teach any waiata or haka from a non-Māori source.
- Safety/adapt: soft ball; stand well apart; never around necks.
- Sources: [Poi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poi_%28performance_art%29).

**92. ခြင်းလုံး (Chinlone)**, "cane ball"
- Origin: Myanmar; a non-competitive traditional game about 1,500 years old according to the page, played by about six in a circle, without hands. Confidence: C.
- Fit: 96-144 m; 2-6; a light cane or soft ball, or balloon; outdoors.
- Play: keep it up with feet, knees and head, in a walking circle; aim for smooth style, not scores.
- Overlap: Sipa and Jianzi are in the app; the non-competitive walking circle is the new idea.
- Sources: [Chinlone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinlone).

**93. Kī-o-rahi**
- Origin: New Zealand. Its age is contested: Māori scholar Patu Hohepa said "we cannot track it in the traditional Maori world", while teacher Harko Brown and sport coordinator Henry Anderson describe it as old and handed down. Confidence: B for the dispute, and do not state "ancient". I did not reach any Sport New Zealand or Māori sport body material.
- Fit: 96-144 m; school or club game of 16; a circular field with markers (pou) and a central target (tupu).
- Play: teams score by touching pou and hitting the target; rules vary by area and are agreed beforehand (tatu).
- Use: more a PE or club game than a home activity; a short "game to look out for" is the better format.
- Sources: [Kī-o-rahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%AB-o-rahi).

## Question 2: How do warabe uta, Korean baby moves, Kniereiter, pestushki and similar lap games work as a genre, and what does research say?

### Takeaway
They share one recipe: an adult-led, repeatable script of rhythm, touch and a predictable build-up to a climax, passed on orally, starting before the baby can speak, and moving from being done to the baby towards the baby doing it alone. I found one peekaboo research summary and no study specific to these named traditions, so any developmental claim in the app should rest on existing sources such as `music-synchrony` and `harvard-serve-return`.

### Cited Findings
- **Japan, warabe uta.** Songs children sing while playing, passed down orally; categories include picture-drawing songs, counting songs, solo and pair hand games, group games, ball songs and lullabies ([わらべうた](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/わらべうた)). Hand-play (手遊び) is defined as rhythmic finger play that promotes attention and development, and its origin is said to be warabe uta ([手遊び](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/手遊び)). The page says hand-play works as shared rhythm between carer and child, and that resuming after an interruption can support a child's sense of agency, though this is stated as a claim in the Japanese childcare literature it cites ([手遊び](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/手遊び)). Inai inai baa is said to delight babies from about 6 months, and around age 1 a baby may start hiding behind furniture and producing the effect themselves, on a page tagged as needing sources ([いないいないばあ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/いないいないばあ)).
- **Japan, baby gesture chain.** The dictionary defines the chain chochi-chochi, awawa, kaiguri, totto no me, with otsumu tenten as a sibling ([かいぐり](https://www.weblio.jp/content/かいぐり); [とっとの目](https://www.weblio.jp/content/とっとのめ); [あわわ](https://www.weblio.jp/content/あわわ); [おつむてんてん](https://www.weblio.jp/content/おつむてんてん)).
- **Korea, Dandong sibhun.** Ten named moves: bura-bura (sway at the waist), sisang-sisang (rock), doridori (head shake), gonji-gonji (finger to palm), jaemjaem (open-close hands), seomma-seomma (stand on the palm), eobi-eobi ("no, no"), aham-aham (palm on mouth), jjakjjakkung (clap), jilla-abi hwil-hwil (arm dance). The page reports and rejects the claim of Dangun-era origin, is tagged as poorly sourced, and its one footnote is a children's book ([단동십훈](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/단동십훈)).
- **German lands, Kniereiter.** The child rides knees facing the adult, hands as reins, bounced to a recited or sung verse; the end mimes a fall and a rescue, which the page says children enjoy for the anticipation. Regional versions appear from Holstein to Switzerland, with one Swiss form speeding up each verse. References include Enzensberger's *Allerleirauh* (1961) ([Kniereitvers](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kniereitvers)). Fingerspiele run from simple hand gestures to finger-as-puppet stories, in many countries ([Fingerspiel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerspiel)). Himpelchen is described as a tiny day-night rhythm that regulates activity and rest ([Himpelchen und Pimpelchen](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himpelchen_und_Pimpelchen)).
- **Slavic, pestushki and potéshki.** Pestushka, from "to nurse, cherish", is a short rhyme by mothers and nurses accompanying what a baby does in early life, such as waking and first steps. Potéshka is a song-rhyme accompanying play with fingers, hands and feet, and is said to work as massage and exercise and to stimulate motor reflexes ([Малые жанры фольклора](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Малые_жанры_фольклора)). Up to about 3-3.5 years a child's development depends fully on adults, and folklorist G. S. Vinogradov called this "the poetry of nurture"; the largest potéshka group is the pestushki ([Детский фольклор](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Детский_фольклор)). In Ukraine, chukykalky are rhymes for bouncing a baby on the knee or foot, rich in sound-play; N. Sivachuk says they suit children too young to understand the words ([Детский фольклор](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Детский_фольклор)). Pokrovsky's 19th-century survey is the classic source for older children's games ([Покровский](https://ru.wikisource.org/wiki/Детские_игры,_преимущественно_русские_%28Покровский%29)).
- **Nordic.** Ride ride ranke is sung with a child "riding" an adult's knee ([Ride ride ranke](https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ride_ride_ranke)).
- **Scotland.** Bookbug offers well-known baby songs and rhymes and runs free family sessions ([Scottish Book Trust](https://www.scottishbooktrust.com/songs-and-rhymes)).
- **Peekaboo research.** Reporting Parrott and Gleitman's study with 6- to 8-month-olds, a BBC article says trick versions, in which a different adult reappears, produced less smiling and laughing, even though they were more surprising, and that this gap grew with age, which suggests babies enjoy a predicted outcome. It also says peekaboo changes with age, starting with simple looming ([BBC Future](https://www.bbc.com/future/story/20140417-why-all-babies-love-peekaboo)). Linguist Iris Nomikou compares the game to dialogue and others call it "protoconversation"; Bruner and Sherwood (1976) is the classic reference ([Peekaboo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peekaboo)).

### Inferences
My proposed structure for under-2s, drawn from the sources above but not stated by any one of them:

| Stage | What baby does | What adults do | Examples in this file |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 m | Receives touch and voice | Stroke, name, sing on waking and at changes | 1 |
| 3-8 m | Anticipates | Same words, same touch, a small surprise at the end | 2, 6, 9 |
| 6-12 m | Rocks and bounces with support | Hold trunk, keep tempo, pause before the climax | 5, 8, 10 |
| 8-15 m | Copies a single gesture | Do it, wait, cheer, repeat | 3, 4, 11 |
| 12-24 m | Asks for it, begins to lead | Let them start it and choose the ending | 7, 12, 13 |
| 18-36 m | Moves with others | Small rings with a clear stop | 14, 15, 16, 20 |

- Authoring rule for the app: same words every time; one touch per beat; a pause before the climax; let baby's reaction set the pace; and a note on how to hand the lead to the child as they grow. This fits the app's Serve & return guide.
- Safety rules common to the genre: head control first; hold the trunk rather than arms; small drops; stop when baby looks away.

I tried to open Phillips-Silver and Trainor on bouncing and infant rhythm (PubMed cookie wall) and Hilton et al. 2022 on infant-directed song across cultures (Nature login redirect), so I do not report their content. Both are worth checking in a follow-up.
- Dates for Korean, Japanese baby gestures and most rhymes are unknown. The sources give none for the baby gestures.

## Question 3: Which traditional homemade toys can families make safely at home?

### Takeaway
Most can be made from paper, card, cups and cord at home, but several traditional methods (carving, pins, tin edges, knives, stones) need an adult-only step or a substitute. Build steps below are from the cited source where a source is named; "Home version" lines are my adaptations.

### Cited Findings

**Baby toys**
- **Den-den daiko, bolang gu (でんでん太鼓, 拨浪鼓), pellet drum.** A small two-headed drum on a stick with two cords and beads; twirling makes the beads hit the skin. Used to soothe small children in Japan, as a children's toy and vendor's noisemaker in China, with Korean relatives ([でんでん太鼓](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/でんでん太鼓); [Pellet drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pellet_drum); [鞀](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/拨浪鼓)). Home version: two paper bowls glued round a wooden spoon handle, with knotted cord in place of beads, for an adult to twirl in front of a baby. Cord length and knots must be checked for safety; the baby should not hold it unsupervised.

**Spin and balance**
- **Trompo, pião, peonza.** Tops have no single origin; clay examples by the Euphrates date to about 4000 BCE per the page ([Trompo](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trompo)). Brazilian Indigenous children's tops made from fruit and bamboo sticks are recorded ([CHC](https://chc.org.br/brincadeira-na-aldeia/)). Malaysia's gasing and Korea's whipped paengi are relatives ([Gasing](https://ms.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasing); [팽이치기](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/팽이치기)). Home version: push a short dowel or pencil stub through a jar lid or stiff card disc and spin by hand. No string-launched wooden tops with nails around small children.
- **Perinola/pirinola.** A six-sided hand top with instructions on each face; a gambling game for tokens, related to the dreidel ([Perinola](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perinola)). Home version: make a card hexagon with actions ("hop", "sing"), no stakes.
- **Ngor-go, button spinner.** Mallanpara (north Queensland) spin toy made from a gourd; two holes, a loop of twine, and it spins as the hands pull apart and come together. Yulunga suggests a large button or small wheel, sticks at the ends to protect fingers, twine up to 2 m ([Yulunga](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga)). Button whirligigs are found in many cultures, including Greek and Native American ones ([Whirligig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whirligig)). Safety (mine): card disc, not metal; keep away from hair and faces.
- **Koara, leaf propeller.** Leaves folded into spinners were recorded in many places, and Aunty Ruth Hegarty of Cherbourg recalled making propellers from cardboard and a straw ([Yulunga](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga)).
- **Catavento, pinwheel.** Square of paper, cut to the middle along the folds, bring the points to the centre, pin, stick on a straw or skewer ([Mapa do Brincar](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/construir/184-catavento)). Adaptation: brass fastener and straw in place of pin and skewer for under-6s.

**Catch toys**
- **Balero, capirucho, emboque, bilboquê.** A stem and a ball joined by cord, known by different names across Latin America and Spain ([Balero](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balero)). In North America it was played with a ring, and in Labrador Inuit communities with a pierced rabbit skull ([Cup-and-ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cup-and-ball)). Ticuna children make it in Brazil ([CHC](https://chc.org.br/brincadeira-na-aldeia/)). Overlap: the app has homemade kendama; add a ring-and-stick variant with a card ring and short cord. No sources I could read describe the Native ring-and-pin in enough detail, so do not credit a specific nation without one.
- **Peteca de papel.** Stuff a plastic bag with crumpled paper, tie and tape the top, and stick long leaves in the hole, or leave the bag's ends as feathers ([Mapa do Brincar](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/construir/204-peteca-de-papel-e-plastico)). Safety: keep plastic bags away from under-3s.

**Folding and dolls**
- **Origami.** Traditional Japanese paper folding ([Kids Web Japan: Origami](https://web-japan.org/kidsweb/virtual/origami/index.html)).
- **Kokeshi.** Limbless wooden dolls from Tōhoku, made for more than 150 years as children's toys ([Kokeshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kokeshi)). Home version: a wooden spoon or clothes peg with a drawn face. Say "inspired by"; real kokeshi are a turner's craft.
- **Boneca de espiga.** Corn cob doll: four sticks pushed in as arms and legs, and a face drawn on ([Mapa do Brincar](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/construir/177-boneca-de-espiga-de-milho)). Adaptation: blunt sticks; adult pushes them in.
- **Tò he.** Vietnamese dough figures. Xuân La village uses rice flour with a little glutinous rice (10 to 1), soaked, ground, boiled, kneaded and coloured. Colours were once plant-based: yellow from turmeric or sophora, red from gấc, black from burnt straw, green from indigo or galangal leaf. Originally edible, modern ones with industrial colours mostly are not ([Tò he](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tò_he)). Similar: Chinese miànsù, and Brazil's salt dough, 1 kg flour, 0.5 kg salt, half a PET bottle of water, plus colouring, shaped into toucans, snakes and jaguars ([Mapa do Brincar](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/construir/203-bichos-de-massinha-caseira)). Safety (mine): salt dough is not food, and a lot of salt can be dangerous if eaten, so check against a poison-control source.
- **Nesting from paper cups.** The first Russian matryoshka was made in the 1890s by Vasily Zvyozdochkin, designed by Sergey Malyutin ([Matryoshka doll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matryoshka_doll)). Paper cups of different sizes with drawn faces are my adaptation, not a tradition, and should say so.

**Ride-on and walking**
- **ขี่ม้าก้านกล้วย, banana-stalk hobby horse.** Thailand; the Royal Institute's English name is "banana rib hobbyhorse riding"; adults trim a stalk into a neck shape, and the child rides and neighs, with races for two or more ([ขี่ม้าก้านกล้วย](https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/ขี่ม้าก้านกล้วย)). Home version: a cardboard tube or pool noodle with a sock head; 24-72 m.
- **Doen kala** (entry 64) and **perna de pau**, stilts, listed in [Mapa do Brincar's Construir index](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/construir/indice/).

**Water**
- **Kaarnalaiva / barkbåt, bark boats.** Finnish and Swedish; thick pine bark is whittled to a hull, with a twig mast and a leaf, paper or cloth sail. Tove Jansson's books feature them ([Kaarnalaiva](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaarnalaiva); [Barkbåt](https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barkbåt)). Adult carves; a child under about 8 decorates. Home version: cork and a toothpick mast. Water supervision always; see the app's `aap-drowning` source.
- **Barco de sucata and barquinho de aninga** (boats from scrap and from plant material) appear in the [Construir index](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/construir/indice/); I did not read the steps.

**Board and paper**
- **Fukuwarai** (entry 30), **Ddakji** (entry 82), **Sungka board** (entry 77, egg carton), **Ô ăn quan** (entry 76), **Gonu** (entry 73), **Mū tōrere** (entry 74), and **Sjoelen** (entry 84).

### Inferences
- Rule of thumb for the writer: replace the traditional hard part with paper, card or cloth for under-6s; state that the parent does any cutting, punching or carving; and label substitutes "inspired by". Mapa do Brincar is the richest source for further toys, with 30 items in its first Construir index page alone.

### Gaps
- Andean string games: nothing verified. Traditional paengi construction: the page I read was too short. Mexican balero and trompo: no INAH or museum page reached, so the make-at-home steps are mine.
- I could not read a detailed ddakji folding guide or Japanese menko instructions; the ddakji fold should be checked against a video or an institutional source.

## Question 4: What are the best string-figure, hand-clap and cup-rhythm games?

### Takeaway
Two-player cat's cradle is the best string game, because it is globally shared, nothing but a loop is needed, and every step is documented. Adoleta and Escravos de Jó are the best clap and rhythm finds for the app, while the popular "cup game" is a modern American-style pattern rather than a traditional world game.

### Cited Findings

**String**
- **No single origin.** Japan's ayatori page says it is now thought to have arisen separately in many places, and figures have been collected from East Asia, Australia, the Pacific, the Americas, Africa, Europe, the West Indies and the far north since the late 19th century. The International String Figure Association (ISFA) exists to preserve them; the Japanese page dates ISFA to 1993 and a Japanese association to 1978, while the English page dates ISFA to 1978 ([あやとり](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/あやとり); [String figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_figure)). China: 翻花绳, also called 挑绷绷, with a loop of yarn or rubber bands ([翻花绳](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/翻花绳)). Korea: 실뜨기 ([실뜨기](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/실뜨기)).
- **Two-player sequence** (age 72 m and up; a loop about 1-2 m). Person A makes the Cradle; B lifts it off into Soldier's Bed, by pinching the two crosses and taking under the straight strings; A into Candles; B into Manger; A into Diamonds; B into Cat's Eye; A into Fish in a Dish; B ends with Clock. The same figures have other names: Soldier's Bed is "Chess Board" in Korea and "A Mountain Cat" in Japan; Candles is "Chopsticks" in Korea; Cat's Eye is "Cow's Eyeball" in Korea and "Horse Eye" in Japan; Fish in a Dish is "Rice-mill" in Korea and "Musical Instrument" in Japan ([String figures collection: Cat's Cradle](http://www.alysion.org/figures/catcradle.htm)). A site with a Kid's Guide to Easy String Figures exists, and I did not read that subpage ([alysion.org](http://www.alysion.org/figures/)). Rules of competition (Japan): a figure that collapses when you take it loses ([あやとり](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/あやとり)).
- **Ayatori names**: river, boat, rice field, diamond, drum, frog, among others, varying by area ([あやとり](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/あやとり)).
- **Kamai, wame, meeroo-meeroo.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait string figures were played almost everywhere in Australia by women and young children of both genders, with Yulunga noting that local names include kamai, morkuru and man-jing in north Queensland, meeroo-meeroo in one part of Western Australia, and wame in the Torres Strait. A loop up to 2 m long, ends spliced ([Yulunga](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga)).
- **Navajo.** String games are a winter storytelling activity, per a Navajo culture site's note on the book by Don Mose Jr. with Theresa Breznau ([navajopeople.org](https://navajopeople.org/blog/navajo-string-games/)). See Question 5.
- **Others.** Inuit figures were described by Boas in 1888; Noble's *String Figures of Papua New Guinea* (1979) and Jayne's *String Figures* (1906) are standard references ([String figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_figure)).

**Clap**
- Simple clapping games suit children from about 24 months, are usually cooperative, and can involve own hands, partner's hands and cross-claps; Sara Bernstein describes 79 basic claps ([Clapping game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clapping_game)). Pat-a-cake is recorded from 1698 ([Hand game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_game)).
- Ring clapping: Adoleta (entry 48), plus the Mapa do Brincar index of 30+ clapping games, including Babalu, Batom, Ana banana and Choco-pito ([Mapa do Brincar: Palmas index](https://mapadobrincar.folha.com.br/brincadeiras/palmas/indice/)).
- Partner clapping: Nanay, Tatay (entry 50). In Japan, おちゃらかほい, お寺の和尚さん and なべなべそこぬけ are listed as two-person hand songs, and I could not get their rules ([わらべうた](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/わらべうた)).
- Catch: Chi chi chành chành (37), Sawsaw suka (38), Zuizui zukkorobashi (39).
- Group rhythm: Sāsā (51).

**Cup rhythm**
- The "cup game" is described as a children's clapping game with coordinated tapping, clapping and cup-passing. It reached mass attention through the 2012 film *Pitch Perfect*, with Lulu and the Lampshades the first to use it as percussion in performance. The song derives from A. P. Carter's 1931 "When I'm Gone" ([Cups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cups_%28song%29)). So the pattern is free to teach, but the song is not a traditional world item and may carry rights.
- Escravos de Jó (entry 49) is commonly played with each player passing an object rhythmically, which makes it a true traditional cup game; Catalan and Aragonese relatives exist ([Escravos de Jó](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escravos_de_Jó)).

### Inferences
- Recommended set: Cat's Cradle (6+ years), Adoleta (5+), Escravos de Jó with cups (6+), Nanay Tatay with adjusted forfeits (6+), Zuizui zukkorobashi (3+). Add the Mapa do Brincar clapping index as the next batch to explore.
- Keep string loops off necks and out of unsupervised reach of under-3s, in line with the app's safe play guide.

### Gaps
- Māori whai and Hawaiian hei: no source reached with how-to steps. Dickey's *String Figures from Hawaii* (Bishop Museum Bulletin 54) and Andersen's *Maori String Figures* are leads; the guessed Bishop URL returned 404.
- No research on cup-rhythm play found.

## Question 5: For Indigenous games, what is publicly shared, who should be credited, and what should not be adapted?

### Takeaway
Yulunga offers the most usable public set, but adapting it needs written approval from the Australian Sports Commission, and it recommends involving local Elders. Several North American games are explicitly tied to ceremony, gender or season, and should be described, not turned into activities, without the community's involvement.

### Cited Findings

**Australia: Yulunga**
- The ASC "encourages the non-commercial use" of Yulunga by the Australian public, but "any reproduction or adaptation of this resource requires the approval of the Australian Sports Commission"; requests go to reconciliation@ausport.gov.au ([Yulunga](https://www.ausport.gov.au/yulunga)). The 2008 PDF also says no part may be reproduced without written permission and gives copyright@ausport.gov.au ([Yulunga card set](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga)).
- It recommends that local Elders be consulted and invited to be involved before use in programs, and gives sample acknowledgement statements ([Yulunga card set](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga)).
- Credit: Dr Ken Edwards as author, Troy Meston assisting; the ASC recognises the traditional owners, without naming each ([Yulunga](https://www.ausport.gov.au/yulunga)).
- Limits: most accounts were recorded in the 19th century by outsiders; some games have been modified, some reconstructed "from vague or incomplete accounts"; approval was sought from traditional owners or organisations "where possible"; and games used in initiation or religious events were avoided ([Yulunga card set](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga)).
- Do not use without Elders' advice: the bullroarer (kandomarngutta), which Yulunga says has special significance or restricted use in some regions, and corroboree games such as wittchim and yongar ngardongin, which the card says need acknowledgement of traditional origin each time ([Yulunga card set](https://www.ausport.gov.au/schools/resource-hub/yulunga)).
- Marn Grook has a long-running origins debate ([Marn Grook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marn_Grook)); avoid repeating claims about it as settled.

**Haudenosaunee**
- Snow snake is "often referred to as a medicine game", hand-made, and "for men only": women were not allowed to touch a man's snow snake, and in some places could not watch. Today it is mostly sport and camaraderie ([Oneida Indian Nation](https://www.oneidaindiannation.org/snow-snake-traditional-winter-game-of-the-haudenosaunee)). The same page compares this to a lacrosse stick. Do not adapt either; a respectful "games to learn about" line is the most the app should do.

**Other North American nations**
- Moccasin game (Ojibwe, Dakota, Lakota and others) is a gambling game with drum and songs, now seeing a resurgence at powwows ([Moccasin game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moccasin_game)). Hand-game traditions such as slahal may have ritual importance ([Hand game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_game)). Do not adapt.
- Navajo string games are described as winter storytelling ([navajopeople.org](https://navajopeople.org/blog/navajo-string-games/)). If included at all, note the season and link to Navajo authors. My general knowledge says the winter-only rule is strict, but I did not find a stronger source in this session.

**Inuit**
- Inuit games are about survival and celebration: strength, endurance, agility, accuracy, balance. Many Inuit prefer to learn through oral means and demonstration, passing it on through family, coaches and Elders ([Indigenous Peoples Atlas of Canada](https://indigenouspeoplesatlasofcanada.ca/article/games-and-sports/)).
- WEIO presents the games as "an important connection to our traditional cultures; rich with history, stories, and spirituality" ([WEIO](https://www.weio.org/about)).
- Katajjaniq (throat singing) is a traditional game of Nunavik women, designated as Québec's first element of intangible heritage ([Avataq](https://www.avataq.qc.ca/en/content/view/full/2949)). The register says it is a symbol of identity, handed down among women, with a transmission break after the 1950s followed by a community-led revival ([Répertoire du patrimoine culturel du Québec](https://www.patrimoine-culturel.gouv.qc.ca/rpcq/detail.do?id=3&methode=consulter&type=imma)). Share recordings by Inuit singers rather than teaching imitation. Many Arctic sports (knuckle hop, high kick) are athletic and not suited to home adaptation ([Arctic sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_sports)).

**Māori**
- Credit Māori for poi, mū tōrere and tī rākau (the last already in the app). Kī-o-rahi's age is disputed among Māori commentators ([Kī-o-rahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%AB-o-rahi)). Mū tōrere is tied to stories and histories ([Mū tōrere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C5%AB_t%C5%8Drere)).

**Hawaiʻi**
- Kōnane is uniquely Hawaiian, according to a Native Hawaiian advocate, who rejects "checkers" as a colonial label; he says the game embodies regenerative values rather than wiping out your opponent ([Hawaiʻi Public Radio](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/the-conversation/2026-02-23/konane-players-perpetuate-hawaiian-strategy-game-at-inaugural-tournament)). ʻUlu maika sources disagree on whether it was men-only ([Ulu maika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulu_maika)).

**Brazil**
- The Jogos Indígenas do Brasil project visited eight villages and catalogued games such as the Bororo jaguar game, Canela puzzles, Pareci dice and Ticuna's banana game Poï Aru Nhagü. Public schools could request kits via the Ministry of Education ([CHC](https://chc.org.br/brincadeira-na-aldeia/)). The project site, www.jogosindigenasdobrasil.art.br, was not read.

### Inferences
- Before publishing any Yulunga-based activity, PlayNest should email reconciliation@ausport.gov.au, because an app is a reproduction or adaptation, and possibly commercial. If approval is not given, either drop those entries or cut them to a short credit and a link.
- Write in your own words, name the people and place, and avoid "Aboriginal game" as a flat label. Good low-risk Yulunga candidates once approved: Inkanyi, Waayin, Tabud nuri, Kai wed, Segur etug, Tok, Turi turi, Koabangan, Nanyima, Kamai. Note that "Walbiri" memory game is based on sand-drawn maps of Country; keep it out unless a Warlpiri reviewer advises otherwise.
- Existing app item "Nalukataq-inspired blanket swing" uses a term that, to my understanding, names an Iñupiat whaling celebration; I did not read a source on Nalukataq in this session. WEIO's emphasis on spirituality, and the general pattern here, suggest renaming it as a plain blanket swing with a separate, accurate cultural note, and having an Iñupiat reviewer look at it. The same applies to Tī rākau and a Māori reviewer, if not already done.
- Budget for cultural readers rather than relying on web pages: Māori, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander, Native Hawaiian, Boe (Bororo), Inuit.

### Gaps
- No NMAI, First Nations or Inuit organisation page with a children's activity list could be reached. Te Papa, Te Ara and Māori sport organisations were not reachable, so Māori entries rest on Wikipedia, which is not good enough to publish from.
- Nothing verified for Mapuche, Aymara, Quechua, Maya or Nahua children's games beyond names and the disputed "kapichuá" and "pallay" etymologies.
- Who holds authority over each game varies, and these notes cannot substitute for asking the communities.
