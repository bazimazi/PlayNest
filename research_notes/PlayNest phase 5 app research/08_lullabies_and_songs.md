# Songbook research: public-domain songs and the science of singing to children

Status note for the report writer: current as of 2026-11-04. The shared WebSearch budget ran out early, so most of this comes from direct fetches of known pages, DOI metadata lookups and downloaded files. Lyrics are deliberately not reproduced here: each song has a romanised title, my own short gist and a URL for the full words. Wikipedia is abbreviated to W and Mama Lisa's World to ML. ML is a discovery source and most of its songs rest on contributor notes, so its "traditional" labels are weak evidence.

## Q1. What is the evidence for parents singing to children?

### Takeaway
Live parent singing has good short-term evidence for calming infants and holding their attention, and one recent randomised trial links more singing to better infant mood. Claims about language, reading and general cognitive gains are much weaker: mostly small or correlational, and one large meta-analysis finds no transfer from music training once study quality is controlled.

### Cited Findings

Soothing and mood
- Randomised trial: 110 families of young infants (mean 3.67 months), 10 weeks, offset design, described by the authors as exploratory. More infant-directed singing improved infant mood after the intervention but not caregiver mood. — [Cho, Yurdum et al. 2025, Child Development](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.14246)
- 144 US infants (mean 7.2 months, range 2.1–14.3) relaxed to eight unfamiliar foreign lullabies compared with eight non-lullabies: lower heart rate, smaller pupils (30 infants tested) and a damped electrodermal rise. Stimuli were animated recordings, not live singing. The authors say they studied a single WEIRD society. — [Bainbridge et al. 2021, Nature Human Behaviour](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220405/)
- Press coverage of that study reports lullabies tended to be slower, with smaller pitch ranges and a less steady beat. — [Victoria University of Wellington](https://wgtn.ac.nz/news/2020/12/anatomy-of-a-lullaby); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/babies-relax-when-listening-unfamiliar-lullabies-other-cultures)
- 8- and 10-month-olds (n = 68 per age group) after a still-face stress: talking increased negative affect, while singing familiar or unfamiliar songs reduced it and raised attention. Familiar songs helped most and produced more smiling. — [Cirelli & Trehub 2020, Developmental Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000917)
- 30 mother-infant pairs: soothing renditions of one song lowered both mother and infant arousal, while playful renditions kept arousal stable and drew more infant attention. — [Cirelli, Jurewicz & Trehub, J Cognitive Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_01402)
- Infants 7–10 months listened to singing roughly twice as long as speech before distress, including singing in an unfamiliar language (Turkish). — [Corbeil, Trehub & Peretz, Infancy](https://doi.org/10.1111/infa.12114)
- Review: mothers' multimodal singing beat maternal speech for recovery from distress at 10 months, and play songs beat lullabies at reducing arousal in Western infants. — [Trehub, Ghazban & Corbeil 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.12622)
- Salivary cortisol converged after 10 minutes of maternal singing at 6 months: low-baseline infants rose modestly and high-baseline infants fell modestly. — [Shenfield, Trehub & Nakata 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356030314002)

Social meaning: live versus toy
- 5-month-olds who heard a song at home from a parent later looked longer at a stranger singing that melody, and more home exposure predicted a stronger preference. Infants who heard it from a toy, or from a socially unrelated adult, showed no such effect, although they remembered the melody more than 8 months later. — [Mehr, Song & Spelke 2016, Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615626691)
- Infant-directed singing entrained infants' eye-looking to the caregiver's eyes from 2 months, doubling in strength by 6 months. — [Lense et al. 2022, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2116967119)
- 7-month-olds showed neural tracking of live maternal singing, stronger for lullabies, and more rhythmic movement to playsongs. Tracking and movement to playsongs related to later vocabulary (assessed at 20 months). — [Nguyen et al. 2023, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2023.101313)

How common singing is
- Most of 945 surveyed parents sing to their infant daily, unrelated to income or ethnicity. Fathers sing less, parents sing less as infants age, and frequency has changed little over 30 years. — [Yan et al. 2021, Phil Trans B](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0089)
- Music appears in every society sampled, with more variation within societies than between them. — [Mehr et al. 2019, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax0868)

Parents' wellbeing
- 134 mothers with postnatal depression symptoms were randomised to singing workshops, play workshops or usual care. The overall effect was non-significant; a faster improvement appeared only in the moderate-severe subgroup. — [Fancourt & Perkins 2018](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.2017.29)
- Survey during Covid: music use was studied alongside parent-child attachment, a correlational design. — [Steinberg, Liu & Lense 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.641733)

Language and rhyme
- Reported parental singing predicted language outcomes in the second year, in a small sample (n = 36 at 6 months, n = 26 at follow-up). — [Franco et al. 2021, J Child Language](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305000921000167)
- Knowledge of nursery rhymes at about age 3 predicted reading and spelling over three years in 64 children, after controlling for social background, IQ and early phonological skill. — [Bryant et al. 1989](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305000900010485)
- Rhythm skills were the best predictors of phonological awareness in 3–4-year-olds (n = 40). — [Politimou et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00948)
- Infants' cortical responses to nursery rhymes at 4, 7 and 11 months were used to track the emergence of phonetic-feature encoding. — [Di Liberto et al. 2023, Nature Communications](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43490-x); related work on sung speech: [Attaheri et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118698)

Music classes
- Random assignment to 6 months of active music classes from 6 months of age gave better prelinguistic gestures and social behaviour than passive exposure. — [Gerry, Unrau & Trainor 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2012.01142.x)
- A 12-session randomised music intervention at 9 months targeted neural processing of temporal structure in music and speech. — [Zhao & Kuhl 2016, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1603984113)
- Finnish kindergarten music playschool (N = 66, 5–6 years, two school years) improved phoneme processing and vocabulary but not non-verbal reasoning or inhibition. — [Linnavalli et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-27126-5)
- Counter-evidence: a meta-analysis (N = 6,984) found the overall effect of music training on cognitive and academic skills to be about zero once study quality is controlled. — [Sala & Gobet 2020](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-020-01060-2)

### Inferences
- Lead with calming, connection and enjoyment. Avoid "makes your baby smarter" claims.
- Familiar songs do better than new ones, so the feature should encourage repeating a few favourites over browsing many.
- Infants respond to unfamiliar-language singing in the Corbeil and Bainbridge studies, which supports a world songbook and suggests a parent need not be a native speaker of the song's language. No study here tested a parent singing in a language they do not speak.
- The evidence base is mostly Western, so "works across cultures" is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- Title-level only, because the abstract was not returned and I make no claims from them: [Mehr et al. 2018, Form and Function in Human Song](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2017.12.042), [Hilton et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-022-01410-x), [Nakata & Trehub 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infbeh.2004.03.002), [Persico et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wombi.2017.01.007), [Papadimitriou et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infbeh.2021.101651), [Williams et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2015.01.004), [Goswami temporal sampling framework](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2010.10.001), [Moritz et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11145-012-9389-0).
- Bilingual and heritage-language singing (Frontiers 2021–2025): I found no verified paper, because the search budget ran out before I could look. Needs a follow-up search.
- Rhythm and self-regulation: not verified. The two DOIs I recalled failed (one resolved to an unrelated paper, one returned 404), so no study is cited for this claim.
- 2024–26 sweep is incomplete. Beyond Cho 2025, I only saw a reference to a 2026 Science paper on [The Music Lab's page](https://www.themusiclab.org/about), and its DOI listing looked wrong, so it is unverified.
- Not reached: Carnegie Hall Lullaby Project (HTTP 403). UNICEF Bebbo, Lomax Global Jukebox and Smithsonian Folkways were not checked.

## Q2. What should parents who think they can't sing be told, is a one-time music-box guide helpful, and what levels are safe?

### Takeaway
Tell parents their own imperfect voice is the point. A music-box guide played once is a reasonable way to teach the tune to the parent, but no study tests it directly. The risk to design against is a recording replacing the parent, so the app should not loop, autoplay or run at bedtime, and should stay quiet and short.

### Cited Findings
- Mothers and fathers both raise pitch and slow down when singing to an infant, and listeners can tell which version was sung to a baby. Infant-directed singing is something ordinary parents do. — [Trehub et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.33.3.500)
- Mothers repeat songs at nearly the same pitch and tempo a week apart, far more stably than speech. — [Bergeson & Trehub 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00413)
- Repetition and stereotypy make caregivers' singing memorable and meaningful to the pair. — [Trehub 2019](https://doi.org/10.1386/ijmec.14.1.9_1)
- Familiar songs reduce distress most. — [Cirelli & Trehub 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000917)
- A soothing style of the same song lowers arousal and a playful one sustains attention, so style can matter as much as song choice. — [Cirelli, Jurewicz & Trehub](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_01402)
- A melody from a toy was remembered but carried no social meaning, unlike the same melody from a parent. — [Mehr, Song & Spelke 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615626691)
- WHO weekly safe-listening table: 60 dB and below unlimited, 80 dB 40 hours, 85 dB 12.5 hours, 90 dB 4 hours, 100 dB 20 minutes. For children, WHO advises speakers over headphones where possible, keeping volume below 60% of device maximum, and quiet breaks. It gives no infant-specific guidance. — [WHO Q&A on safe listening](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/deafness-and-hearing-loss-safe-listening)
- The WHO standard of 2 March 2022 covers entertainment venues and events. Its landing page states no dB limit and says nothing about infants. — [WHO global standard for safe listening venues and events](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240043114)
- All 14 infant sleep machines tested exceeded 50 dB(A) at 30 cm at maximum volume, 50 dB(A) being the recommended limit in hospital nurseries. Three exceeded 85 dB(A). — [Hugh et al. 2014, Pediatrics](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2013-3617)

### Inferences
These are my design suggestions, not tested findings.
- Reassuring copy: babies are not judging pitch; your voice, face and closeness are what they get from you. Humming, "la la", a lower key, a slower pace, or speaking the rhyme in rhythm all count. Pick two or three songs and repeat them.
- Guide design: tap to hear the tune once, then the app stops and prompts "now you try". No auto-repeat, no background play, no sleep timer, no playlist. Offer the replay as an explicit, parent-only action for learning, and suggest learning it before the baby is present or in the daytime.
- Mehr 2016 suggests the guide teaches the parent and should not be presented as the baby's experience. Bainbridge's recorded-lullaby result should not be used to justify playing recordings to babies.
- Volume: start low, cap in-app gain where the platform allows, keep the phone an arm's length or more away, and never use headphones on a baby. Hugh 2014 suggests aiming near or below 50 dB(A) at the baby. A single guide lasting under a minute is far below any weekly limit, so the larger risk is habit rather than dose.
- Fixed-pitch music-box tones cannot represent microtonal scales or ornament, so maqam, raga and pelog songs should be labelled "approximate tune" or left without a guide.

### Gaps
- I found no study on parent pitch accuracy and infant outcomes, or on a once-played instrumental guide as a teaching aid.
- WHO's venue dB limit is not on the landing page, and I did not read the full PDF. Do not cite a figure from it without checking.
- The 50 dB(A) figure is a hospital nursery recommendation, not a home standard.

## Q3. Which famous songs are wrongly assumed traditional, or otherwise should not be used?

### Takeaway
Do not use the songs below as "traditional". Two names from the brief turned out to be authored works: "Nani Teri Morni", which the brief suggested as traditional, is a film song, and "San-toki" is an authored 1928 song. I also found that the Polish lullaby "Na Wojtusia z popielnika" has a named poet, though ML lists it with no author. No lyrics are quoted here.

### Cited Findings
Rule of thumb
- US: works published before 1 January 1931 are public domain as of 2026; foreign works have caveats, and sound recordings are separate. — [Cornell copyright chart](https://guides.library.cornell.edu/copyright/publicdomain)

Do not use (authored and in, or likely in, copyright)
- "Kookaburra": Marion Sinclair (d. 1988), under copyright until 2038 in Australia; Larrikin Music holds the publishing rights and won the "Down Under" case. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kookaburra_(song))
- "Baby Shark": the underlying chant is older, but specific versions are contested. Johnny Only (Jonathan Wright) sued SmartStudy in Korea, and the Seoul court ruled against him on 23 July 2021. Avoid both commercial versions. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baby_Shark)
- "Lakdi Ki Kaathi": from the 1983 film Masoom. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masoom_(1983_film))
- "Nani Teri Morni": from the 1960 film Masoom, not traditional. Credits conflict between sources. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masoom_(1960_film)); [Atul's Song A Day](https://atulsongaday.me/2009/07/07/naani-teri-morni-ko-mor-le-gaye/)
- "Chanda Mama Door Ke": the brief calls it a film song. I could not retrieve a page confirming the credits, so treat it as in copyright.
- "Sa Ugoy ng Duyan": Lucio San Pedro (d. 2002) and Levi Celerio (d. 2002). — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa_Ugoy_ng_Duyan); [San Pedro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucio_San_Pedro); [Celerio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_Celerio)
- "San-toki" (산토끼): written and composed by Lee Il-rae in 1928, printed 1938. His death date is unverified here, so treat it as in copyright. — [Korean W](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/산토끼_(동요))
- "Na Wojtusia z popielnika" / "Bajka iskierki": text by Janina Porazińska, published 1925, set to a traditional melody. Her death date is unverified here (I recall 1971), so do not use the text. — [Polish W](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bajka_iskierki)
- "Ampar-Ampar Pisang": often called regional, but credited to A. Zaini. — [Indonesian W](https://id.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampar-Ampar_Pisang)
- "Tūtira Mai Ngā Iwi": Wiremu Te Tau Huata, 1950s. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%ABtira_Mai_Ng%C4%81_Iwi)
- "Malaika": attributed to Adam Salim, 1945. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaika)
- "Jambo Bwana": Them Mushrooms, 1982. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jambo_Bwana)
- "Lala mpendwa" (the Swahili "lala mtoto" the brief asked about): the ML contributor says she proposed it herself and that few people use it, so it is not documented as traditional. — [ML](https://www.mamalisa.com/?t=es&p=5060)

Safe, but commonly misdescribed
- "Happy Birthday": not traditional. The tune comes from the Hill sisters' "Good Morning to All". The US copyright claim was declared invalid in 2015 and Warner Chappell settled in 2016; EU copyright expired on 1 January 2017. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_Birthday_to_You)
- "Numi Numi": authored, not traditional, but both authors died over 70 years ago (see the catalogue). — [ML](https://www.mamalisa.com/?t=es&p=7365)
- "Hine e Hine": authored by Fanny Howie (d. 1916). — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanny_Howie)

Hold until checked (I could not verify the status)
- "Thula Sizwe": no reliable page reached.
- "Yalla Tnam Rima": no page reached. The common recording is by Fairuz (from memory), so do not use any recorded arrangement or its words until checked.
- 东北摇篮曲 "Northeastern Lullaby": ML lists it without an author. I recall a modern composer credit but could not confirm it. — [ML](https://www.mamalisa.com/?t=es&p=4962)
- "Akatombo": authored by named 20th-century writers. Check the term by market, as Japan's rules differ from life+70 markets. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akatombo)

### Inferences
- As of 2026, life+70 means authors who died before 1 January 1956 (my arithmetic, not a cited rule). Terms differ by country, so get legal review before launch for each market.
- Traditional words do not make a modern translation, arrangement, engraving or recording free. Write our own translations and sequence our own audio. Use ML MIDI and images as reference only, and do not redistribute them.
- Existing activity "Mbube Mbube" shares its name with Solomon Linda's song. The existing activity should be checked for whether it uses that song's words or tune. — [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lion_Sleeps_Tonight)

### Gaps
- No lawyer has reviewed this. Ownership of orphan works, unknown modern lyricists on school songs, and Indigenous cultural rights are not settled by a death date.
- From memory only and unverified: several popular Indonesian and Chinese school songs listed on ML as children's songs have named 20th-century authors. Do not assume they are traditional.

## Q4. Which songs can ship, with what melody evidence?

### Takeaway
The objective of 60 songs cleared to ship is not met. The catalogue has 93 entries: 76 with a notated melody retrieved from a source (confidence 11 high, 62 medium, 3 low), 2 that reuse the Frère Jacques tune by reference, and 15 with no verified melody. Only about 37 of the 76 have a firm public-domain basis (a dead named author or a source documenting age); the rest rely mainly on ML's "traditional" label. Only 24 are melody-verified with a firmer basis and no sensitivity flag, and that shortlist is thin on Africa, the Middle East and South Asia. Full lyrics, word-level romanisation and full translations are not in these notes (see Gaps).

### Cited Findings

### How to read the catalogue

How melodies were obtained
- Each ABC block was generated by script from a retrieved source, not typed from memory: W `<score>` LilyPond, an ML MIDI file, or the [Digital Tradition ABC set](http://sniff.numachi.com/~rickheit/dtrad/). L'abe igi orombo is the exception, transcribed by me from ML's engraved score image.
- Format: K:C with explicit accidentals, L:1/8, rhythm snapped to a sixteenth-note grid. The key centre is given in the text, and Q is the tempo in the source, which can be slowed. Meter tags from MIDI files can be wrong, so check bar lines against the source score before shipping.
- The music-box audio should be our own sequencing of these notes. Do not redistribute the source files.

Confidence
- High: two sources agree. I compared note-interval sequences by script (match score in the entry) or by eye.
- Medium: one source, or a score and MIDI from the same publisher.
- Low: a single source for a tune that varies widely or does not fit fixed pitches.
- Comparison scores below 0.9 usually reflect extra verses in the MIDI, but a parent or native speaker should still check them.

Routines (my suggestions, not from sources; ages are also my suggestions)
- R1 lullaby: 1) hold the baby close, 2) sway on the strong beat, 3) sing softly and slow down at the end, 4) hum the tune once more and stop.
- R2 lap song: 1) sit the baby on your knees facing you, 2) bounce gently on the beat, 3) pause before the last word, 4) finish with a small lift or a cuddle.
- R3 circle: 1) hold hands in a ring, 2) walk on the beat, 3) do the song's action at the end, 4) repeat, letting a child choose the next action.

Public-domain caution: "traditional" below usually means a source calls it that and I found no named author. It is not a legal clearance.

**79. لالا لالا گل پونه — Lala lala gol-e pooneh — "sleep, sleep, pennyroyal flower"**
- Persian; Iran | lullaby | suggested age 0–24 m
- Gist (my wording): A dog comes to the door and likes the bread it is given. Its owner comes, likes the bread, and dislikes the water. Full words: https://www.mamalisa.com/?t=es&p=1182
- Actions: R1
- Melody: left blank: I could not verify one. ML links only a video or audio recording, or nothing, and I did not transcribe by ear.
- Public-domain basis: Traditional per ML; no author found. ML shows the Persian as an image, so I typed the title from its transliteration; have a Persian speaker check it. ML lists several sibling "lala lala gol-e..." verses.
- Sensitivity: A related ML version says the father has gone away.

**80. دس دسی — Das dasi — "clap, clap"**
- Persian; Iran | lap clapping rhyme | suggested age 6–24 m
- Gist (my wording): Clap hands, father is coming and you can hear his shoes; other relatives follow, each with a funny detail. Full words: https://www.mamalisa.com/?t=es&p=2595
- Actions: From ML: 1) sit the baby on your knees leaning against you, 2) clap the baby's hands together while chanting.
- Melody: Not needed as a guide if spoken. ML gives no tune I could verify.
- Public-domain basis: Traditional per ML; no author found.
- Sensitivity: One verse mentions the mother's breasts and another teases an aunt's big mouth. Pick verses with a Persian reviewer.

**81. لی لی لی لی حوضک — Li li li li hozak — "li li little pool"**
