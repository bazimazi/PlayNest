## A1. What does the evidence say matters most in middle childhood (ages 8–12)?

### Takeaway
What I could verify points to four priorities: more unsupervised outdoor time and independence, keeping sport fun and varied through the age-11 drop-off, family meals and talk, and helping others. Claims that specific games or activities (chess, music, cooking) raise general cognition should be avoided. Most of what I could verify is correlational or small-scale, and the key "independence" literature could not be opened this session.

### Cited Findings

**Independence and outdoor play**
- British Children's Play Survey (nationally representative, 1,974 parents and caregivers of 5–11s): children are allowed to play outdoors alone at an average age of 10.8, against 9.7 for their parents' generation. 56% of parents let their children play independently later than they themselves were allowed to. — [Play England, 22 Aug 2026](https://www.playengland.org.uk/newsblog/british-childrens-play-survey-exposes-the-generational-gap-in-childrens-freedom-to-play) [R]; preprint linked from that page: [OSF](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/36ptm_v2) [not opened]
- The same survey: children average under 25 minutes a week playing in streets near home. Outdoor and adventurous play declines with age, and boys have more total, outdoor and adventurous play than girls. Play England attributes the gap to traffic, limited public space, safety concerns and screens, not to children wanting less play (that attribution is the charity's interpretation). — [Play England](https://www.playengland.org.uk/newsblog/british-childrens-play-survey-exposes-the-generational-gap-in-childrens-freedom-to-play) [R]
- Safe Kids Worldwide says children under 10 need an adult to cross the street, because most cannot judge the speed and distance of cars until about age 10, though children differ. — [Safe Kids](https://www.safekids.org/tip/pedestrian-safety-tips) [R]
- Let Grow's own research page lists only a small pilot of independence therapy, one study of its Play Club, a dissertation and a study in progress. It offers no controlled trials or large peer-reviewed results. — [Let Grow research page](https://letgrow.org/resource/research-studies-on-let-grow/) [R]
- Let Grow's safety myth-busting cites NCMEC data that non-family abductions were 0.3% of 2019 missing-child cases, and more than 90% of reports involve runaways. These are US figures from an advocacy group, from an article first published in 2019. — [Let Grow](https://letgrow.org/child-safety-myth-busting/) [R]
- Finnish Schools on the Move now covers more than 90% of Finnish comprehensive schools, and the programme reports that 83% of pupils take part in physical activity during the school day and that active breaks are linked to better grades. — [Liikkuva koulu](https://liikkuvakoulu.fi/en/home/) [R]
- Playing Out (UK) campaigns for street play and publishes how-to guides and a research list. — [Playing Out four steps](https://playingout.net/play-streets/your-play-street/playing-out-in-four-simple-steps) [L]; [research list](https://playingout.net/why/research) [L]

**Sport, physical activity and girls**
- The UK guideline is an average of at least 60 minutes of moderate or vigorous activity a day for ages 5–18, mixing activity types and breaking up long periods of sitting. — [NHS, reviewed 23 Oct 2024](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/physical-activity-guidelines-children-and-young-people/) [R]
- England, academic year 2024–25, ages 5–16: 49.1% meet the guideline and 28.4% get under 30 minutes a day. Boys 52%, girls 46%: a 5.9-point gap, down from 7.1 points in 2017–18. — [Sport England, 4 Dec 2025](https://www.sportengland.org/news-and-inspiration/childrens-activity-levels-rising-inactivity-remains-too-high) [R]
- Globally, 81% of adolescents aged 11–17 were insufficiently active: 85% of girls and 78% of boys. — [WHO fact sheet, 26 June 2024](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity) [R]
- Women in Sport says 4 in 10 girls who once loved sport disengage as teenagers. The homepage names no report, so trace this before citing it. — [Women in Sport](https://womeninsport.org/) [R]
- In the US, 55.4% of ages 6–17 played a sport in 2023, up from 53.8% in 2022. Youth sports costs are up 46% since 2019. The report calls free play "a lost art for many children" and says the lowest-income children play it the most. — [Project Play, State of Play 2025](https://projectplay.org/state-of-play-2025/) [R]
- The average child spends less than three years in a sport and quits by age 11 (2019 Project Play/Utah State survey). — [Project Play challenges](https://projectplay.org/youth-sports/facts/challenges) [R]
- Only 24% of US 6–12s from homes earning $25,000 or less play sport regularly, against 40% from homes earning $100,000 or more. — [Project Play challenges](https://projectplay.org/youth-sports/facts/challenges) [R]
- Early specialisation is linked to more injury, psychological stress and quitting young. High school athletes began specialising as young as age 10 on average, and Project Play's cited review says specialisation should wait until late adolescence. Trained coaches kept attrition at 5% against 26% with untrained coaches (Smoll & Smith 1992). — [Project Play challenges](https://projectplay.org/youth-sports/facts/challenges) [R]
- Project Play publishes separate parent checklists for 6–12s playing and not playing sport. — [Project Play parent resources](https://projectplay.org/parent-resources) [L]

**Reading aloud and reading for pleasure**
- UK ages 8–18: 32.7% enjoy reading in their free time, the lowest in 20 years, and 18.7% read daily. The recent drop was steepest among primary-aged children and boys. — [National Literacy Trust, June 2025](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/children-and-young-peoples-reading-in-2025/) [R]
- Scholastic reports declining reading enjoyment and frequency among US 6–17s, with marked drops by age 9, and says most parents and children agree reading aloud is or was "a special time". It gives no read-aloud rates for 6–11 on the page I could read. — [Scholastic KFRR key findings](https://www.scholastic.com/content/corp-home/kids-and-family-reading-report/key-findings.html) [R]

**Kindness**
- Randomised classroom experiment, 415 children aged 9–11, 19 Vancouver classrooms: three acts of kindness a week for 4 weeks raised peer acceptance more than a pleasant control task (+1.57 vs +0.71 peer nominations). Well-being rose in both groups with no significant difference between them. — [Layous et al., PLOS ONE 2012](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0051380) [R]

**Cooking and food**
- Review of 8 studies of cooking programmes for ages 5–12: programmes may improve willingness to try new foods, cooking confidence and attitudes, with mixed results on BMI. Only 2 of 8 were rated high quality, and long-term effects are unknown. — [Hersch et al., Preventing Chronic Disease 2014](https://www.cdc.gov/pcd/issues/2014/14_0267.htm) [R]
- Meta-analysis of 17 studies, 182,836 children and adolescents: at least 3 shared family meals a week was linked to 12% lower odds of overweight, 20% lower odds of unhealthy eating and 24% higher odds of healthy eating. Only 5 studies were longitudinal, and they showed small associations. — [Hammons & Fiese, Pediatrics 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3387875/) [R]

**Coding without screens**
- CS Unplugged lists seven topics with age bands (most 5–10, one 11–14, sorting networks 5–14). Its website content is licensed CC BY-SA 4.0, and the materials are open source on GitHub. This is the most reusable activity source I found. — [CS Unplugged topics](https://www.csunplugged.org/en/topics/) [R]

**Family meetings**
- The Positive Discipline format is a weekly 15–30 minute meeting: compliments, an agenda, a talking stick, brainstorming, choosing a solution and a fun ending, with children rotating the roles. The article cites no research, only anecdote and an unnamed group of Adlerian practitioners. — [Positive Discipline](https://www.positivediscipline.com/articles/family-meetings/) [R]

**Money**
- The US consumer regulator's framework for ages 6–12 covers earning, saving, planning, shopping (comparing prices), borrowing (loans repaid with interest) and protecting personal information, and advises parents to think out loud about money decisions. It is guidance rather than an evaluation. — [CFPB Money as You Grow](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/money-as-you-grow/school-age-children-preteens/) [R]

**Rites of passage**
- Rite-of-passage reference pages are available for several traditions in or near this age band. They are tertiary sources, used only as prompts. — [Rite of passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rite_of_passage), [Upanayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanayana), [Navjote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navjote), [Bar and bat mitzvah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_and_bat_mitzvah), [Confirmation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation), [Old Enough! (Japan's first-errand TV show)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Enough!) [L]

### Inferences
- **Lead with independence and responsibility.** This is the clearest change from the under-8 catalogue, and it fits the "lead the family" format. The evidence that independence improves wellbeing is weaker than advocacy suggests, so PlayNest should sell it as a developmentally sensible opportunity, not a proven treatment for anxiety.
- **Design around a real safety tension.** Safe Kids says under-10s need an adult to cross streets, while independence advice encourages solo errands at 8–9. Independence activities for 8–9s should use routes with no road crossing, or a crossing where the parent watches from a distance. Solo crossing should start around 10, with a "you know your child" caveat.
- **Treat girls as a design audience.** Girls report less play and less activity, and the gap is already present in the 5–11 data. Non-competitive, friend-based and child-chosen movement should be well represented, without framing girls as a problem.
- **Avoid "brain-building" claims for games, music and chess.** Describe what is directly true: practising turn-taking, planning and losing gracefully, and time together.
- **Keep the kindness finding precise.** Layous supports "kindness helps children be liked more", not "kindness makes children happier than other pleasant activities".
- **Reading aloud should continue to age 12.** Falling enjoyment around age 9 and "a special time" sentiment make a reasonable case, though the read-aloud effect itself at this age was not verified.

## B2. What do the newest meta-analyses say about screens, technoference and background TV?

### Takeaway
Effects are small, mostly correlational and often run in both directions, but they cluster around heavy use, gaming, background TV and parents' own phone use. Context matters more than a single minutes figure: co-use and educational content look better, and children within guidelines show little or no effect in the largest new analysis.

### Cited Findings
- **Bidirectional effects (Psychological Bulletin, longitudinal meta-analysis, 292,739 children; 2,284 effects):** screen use predicts later socioemotional problems, and those problems predict later screen use, with small effects that the authors say matter as they accumulate. — [APA PDF](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000468.pdf) [S for headline numbers]
- From my own reading of that paper's text: effects were "much stronger for gaming" than for other types of use, and studies of children exceeding screen guidelines showed small significant effects, while those meeting recommendations showed no significant association (b = −0.01). The authors also say attentive, supportive parenting, rather than lax or controlling styles, may reduce the risks. — [APA PDF full text](https://psycnet.apa.org/fulltext/2026-24348-001.pdf) [R, partly garbled extraction; author names and full effect table not confirmed]
- **Context matters (under-6s; 100 studies, 176,742 participants):** co-use was positively associated with cognitive outcomes (r = 0.14). Background TV was negatively associated with cognition (r = −0.10). Caregiver screen use during routines was negatively associated with psychosocial outcomes (r = −0.11). Most included evidence was cross-sectional with high risk of bias. — [Mallawaarachchi et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2024](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2821940?guestAccessKey=d9ef3589-dc0a-4a60-8704-9cfabb94ca76) [R]
- **Technoference (birth to 4.9 years; 21 studies in meta-analysis, 14,900 participants, 10 countries):** parents' tech use in the child's presence was linked to poorer cognition (r = −0.14), more internalising (r = 0.13) and externalising problems (r = 0.15), lower prosocial behaviour (r = −0.08), weaker attachment (r = −0.10) and more child screen time (r = 0.23). The effects were small and correlational. Language was not significant. — [Toledo-Vargas et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2025](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/article-abstract/2833506) [R]
- **Technoference in older children:** a 2025 meta-analysis found parental technoference linked to children's problematic media use (r = 0.282, though another version reports 0.296), and stronger as children get older. — [JMIR 2025](https://jmir.org/2025/1/e57636/PDF) [S]
- **Parental phubbing:** linked to internalising (r = 0.27) and externalising problems (r = 0.21) in a meta-analysis. — [Dovepress](https://dovepress.com/parental-phubbing-and-child-social-emotional-adjustment-a-meta-analysi-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-PRBM) [S]
- **Children 12 and under (87 studies):** more screen time was weakly linked to behaviour problems. — [Eirich et al., JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2790338) [S]
- **Young screen ownership:** 40% of US children have a tablet by age 2, nearly 1 in 4 have a phone by age 8, and average use for 0–8s is about 2.5 hours a day. — [Common Sense Census, 26 Feb 2025](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/the-2025-common-sense-census-media-use-by-kids-zero-to-eight) [R]

### Inferences
- **Parents' own phone use is the most actionable, and the most uncomfortable, finding.** The UK and Sweden both added it to guidance in 2026. PlayNest should treat this kindly: "your phone in the bag" as a two-way family rule, not as blame. The evidence is correlational.
- **Gaming needs nuance in copy.** The stronger gaming effect needs care, since families often use multiplayer games socially, and the paper's direction is two-way.
- **Say "linked to" rather than "causes" throughout**, and avoid fear-based language.

### Gaps
Named in the brief; not opened, because publishers blocked access. Summaries are **prior knowledge only**. [U]
- **Sanders et al. 2024** (Nature Human Behaviour umbrella review): [blocked](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01712-8).
- **Madigan et al. 2020** (JAMA Pediatrics, screen use and language): [blocked](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2762711). I recall background TV and early exposure being linked to poorer language, and co-viewing to better.
- **Takahashi et al. 2023** (screen time at age 1 and later developmental delay): [blocked](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2809097).
- **Hutton et al.** (brain white matter): [blocked](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2754101). The study is small and correlational, so use with caution, if at all.
- **Radesky and McDaniel's original technoference studies:** not retrieved. The meta-analyses above stand in for them.

## B3. AI chatbots and companion toys: what's the current guidance?

### Takeaway
Guidance has converged quickly: no AI companions or toys for young children, supervised and limited use for 6–12, and no reliance on AI for friendship or emotional support at any age under 18. The AAP says outright avoidance is unrealistic for teens and recommends talking about it instead.

### Cited Findings
- **Common Sense:** 5 and under, no AI chatbots. Ages 6–12, "limited use, alongside a parent or caregiver". 13+, supervised for homework and creativity. All under 18, "No AI for emotional support, mental health advice, or 'friendship.'" — [Common Sense, 8 June 2026](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/articles/ai-chatbots-and-your-child-age-and-stage-guidance) [R]
- **Common Sense on AI toys:** avoid for 5 and under; "extreme caution" for 6–12. 27% of toy outputs tested were inappropriate for children. Devices collect voice recordings, transcripts and behavioural data, and deliberately build bonding. — [Common Sense, 22 Jan 2026](https://about.commonsensemedia.org/press-room/press-releases/common-sense-media-warns-against-ai-toy-companions-after-research-reveals-safety-risks) [R]
- **AAP clinicians' Q&A:** "avoid all chatbots" is "likely not realistic". Make AI a routine topic, stay curious and nonjudgmental, build AI literacy, use protective privacy defaults, and add generative AI to the Family Media Plan. Warning signs: sleep changes, social withdrawal, more time with AI than other interests, mentions of an "AI friend". Written mainly with teens in mind. — [AAP, updated 19 June 2026](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/counseling-patients-and-families-on-using-ai-chatbots/) [R]
- **UNICEF policy brief:** conversational and relational AI pose "distinct and heightened risks for children". The brief compares six jurisdictions as of May 2026. — [UNICEF, June 2026](https://www.unicef.org/documents/when-ai-becomes-friend-child-rights-risks) [R]
- **UNICEF Guidance on AI and Children 3.0:** ten requirements, including safety, data and privacy, non-discrimination, transparency, children's wellbeing and preparing children for AI. — [UNICEF Innocenti, Dec 2025](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children) [R]
- **APA:** June 2025 health advisory on AI and adolescents covers regulation, disclaimers and AI literacy. — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2025/06/protect-adolescent-ai-users) [S]
- **AACAP:** AI companions are "not safe tools" for children, and it tells parents to discourage them. — [AACAP](https://aacap.org/AACAP/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/AI_Chatbots-148.aspx) [S]
- **Fairplay's AI toys advisory** was endorsed by more than 150 experts and groups, including Sherry Turkle and Jenny Radesky. It is an advocacy document. — [KAXE](https://www.kaxe.org/news/2025-11-20/ahead-of-the-holidays-consumer-and-child-advocacy-groups-warn-against-ai-toys) [S]
- **Regulation:** California's SB 243, effective 1 Jan 2026, requires crisis protocols and protections for minors, and one 2026 parent guide reports twelve US states with companion-chatbot laws. — [Cyber Safety Guy](https://www.cybersafetyguy.com/p/ai-companions-child-safety-parents-guide) [S, low-authority source; verify before use]; broader overview: [Industry self-regulation blog](https://industryselfregulation.org/media-resource/media/blog/ai-chatbot-regulations) [S]. The UK plans to raise certain chatbots to a minimum age of 18. — [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/uk-under-16s-social-media-ban-brings-comprehensive-curbs-wide-range-online-services) [R]

### Inferences
- **For PlayNest's 8–12 band, the practical rule:** use a chatbot alongside a parent, for curiosity or a task, not for feelings. Keep AI toys off gift lists.
- **Offer AI literacy as a game:** "catch the chatbot's mistake" together is a natural PlayNest conversation prompt, though the activity itself would involve a screen, so it belongs in the guide rather than the screen-free catalogue.
- **Mention home "voice" toys explicitly.** Parents may not count them as screens.

### Gaps

- **Independent evidence of harm or benefit for under-13s:** none found in this session. Most guidance is precautionary, and much of the AI toy evidence comes from advocacy groups' testing.
- **PIRG "Trouble in Toyland" specifics:** not retrieved.
- **Cambridge's March 2026 AI toy study:** only seen via a [secondary summary](https://www.brightsurf.com/news/LMJGNGNL/report-calls-for-ai-toy-safety-standards-to-protect-young-children.html). [S]

## B4. Phone-free movements, first-phone readiness and school bans: what does the evidence support?

### Takeaway
Delaying a smartphone is a reasonable choice that families can make together, but the evidence is associational and the experts still disagree. School bans reliably cut phone use, but their effect on test scores and wellbeing is mixed and takes time to appear. PlayNest should give readiness signs rather than a single magic age.

### Cited Findings
- **Smartphone Free Childhood** (UK) asks parents to delay a smartphone until 14 and social media until 16, and says "hundreds of thousands" of families have joined (no exact number). — [SFC](https://www.smartphonefreechildhood.org/) [R]
- **Wait Until 8th** (US): delay until at least the end of 8th grade, with basic phones allowed; the pledge activates when 10 families in a grade sign up. More than 147,000 pledges as of 9 Apr 2026. — [Wait Until 8th FAQ](https://www.waituntil8th.org/media-faqs) [R]; [pledge page](https://www.waituntil8th.org/sign-the-pledge) [S]
- **Smartphone by 12 (Barzilay et al., Pediatrics, Dec 2025):** ABCD Study, more than 10,000 US children, median age of first smartphone 11. Owning one at 12 was associated with more depression, obesity and insufficient sleep, and reported odds were about 31%, 40% and 62% higher. Younger acquisition tracked worse sleep and obesity outcomes. The authors did not examine what children did on phones. This is association, not causation. — [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/children-smartphones-increased-risk-of-health-problems-study/) [R]; [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/12/01/smartphones-age-12-worse-health-study?stream=top) [S]
- **Contradicting signal:** a Child Development study reported no link between age of first device and grades, sleep or depressive symptoms, as noted in a secondary article. — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/raising-resilient-children/202512/when-childhood-meets-the-glow-of-a-phone) [S, original not opened]
- **Critique (Odgers):** her Nature review of *The Anxious Generation* says the "rewiring" claim isn't supported by the science. She cites a National Academies committee finding of "small effects and weak associations" and says violence exposure, school stress, economic hardship and parental deaths are stronger predictors. — [Odgers, 29 Oct 2026](https://candiceodgers.substack.com/p/no-i-was-not-paid-to-critique-the) [R]
- **Haidt's reply:** he argues experimental evidence supports a causal role for social media. — [After Babel](https://www.afterbabel.com/p/phone-based-childhood-cause-epidemic) [S]; [Platformer on the debate](https://www.platformer.news/anxious-generation-jonathan-haidt-debate-critique/) [S]
- **School phone pouches (national US study):** phones used substantially less. Discipline incidents rise and wellbeing may dip in year one, with later recovery. Average test score effects "close to zero", with modest high school gains and small middle school losses. — [Allcott et al., NBER 35132, revised Sep 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132) [R]
- **Florida bans:** suspensions rose in the short term, especially among Black students. Test scores improved in the second year, and unexcused absences fell. Effects were larger in middle and high schools. — [Figlio & Özek, NBER 34388, Oct 2025](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34388) [R]
- **AAP on a first phone:** gives no age, and suggests considering the child's interest in responsibility and past media habits, using its PhoneReadyQuiz. — [AAP 12 Tips, 16 Jan 2024](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Tips-for-Parents-Digital-Age.aspx) [R]

### Inferences
**A readiness checklist PlayNest could adapt** (my synthesis, not a published instrument):
1. Can they follow existing family rules about time and content without constant reminders?
2. Do they tell you when something goes wrong online or at school?
3. Is there a real need, such as travelling alone?
4. Can they sleep with the phone outside the bedroom?
5. Would a basic phone cover that need? AEP, Wait Until 8th and France's commission all point this way.
6. Are their friends' families making a similar choice? Collective delay removes much of the social cost.

**Other inferences:**
- The ages on offer differ widely: France's report (none until 11, no internet until 13), Wait Until 8th (about 14), SFC (14). PlayNest can say "no consensus on an exact age, but a shared direction: later, and simple first."
- PlayNest's stance can be even-handed: "researchers disagree about how much smartphones cause problems, and all agree sleep, movement and relationships matter. Protect those first."
- Schools' phone rules may increase after-school phone demand or conflict in year one, but I found no evidence on this.

### Gaps
- **Goodyear et al. 2025 (SMART Schools, Lancet Regional Health Europe):** [blocked](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanepe/article/PIIS2666-7762(25)00003-1/fulltext). I recall no wellbeing difference between restrictive and permissive policies. [U]
- **Effects of phones on 8–10s specifically:** the studies above focus on 12 and up.
- **Common Sense "First Phones" guide:** exists per site navigation, not read.
- **Haidt's book itself** and the Nature review were not opened. Claims above come from their authors' posts.

## B5. What should a family media agreement contain?

### Takeaway
Current best-practice agreements cover whole-family rules including parents, device-free times and places, content and privacy, what to do when things go wrong, and regular review. I found no evaluation showing any particular agreement works. These are consensus recommendations.

### Cited Findings
- **AAP's plan topics:** screen-free zones and times; one screen at a time; autoplay and notifications off; content quality; privacy and advertising; balancing with reading, outdoor play, family games and hobbies; parental controls across devices; a plan for every family member including parents; revisit at the start of the school year or during breaks. — [AAP, updated 20 Jan 2026](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/How-to-Make-a-Family-Media-Use-Plan.aspx) [R]
- **Add generative AI to the plan.** — [AAP Q&A, 19 June 2026](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/counseling-patients-and-families-on-using-ai-chatbots/) [R]
- **CPS's 4 Ms:** regularly revised plan; know logins; discourage homework multitasking; screen-free meals; recreational screens out of bedrooms; no screens for an hour before bed. Warning signs include distress without technology and interference with sleep, school or friendships. — [CPS](https://www.cps.ca/en/documents/position/digital-media) [R]
- **Sweden's advice to parents:** tell children why you must use your phone, so they understand you aren't choosing it over them, and think before posting their pictures. — [1177](https://www.1177.se/Vastmanland/liv--halsa/sunda-vanor/barn-och-skarmar/) [R]
- **AAP's tips:** children should know they can come to you about problems even if embarrassed; learn to spot ads and edited content; set privacy to the highest level on new accounts. — [AAP 12 Tips](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Tips-for-Parents-Digital-Age.aspx) [R]
- **Common Sense** offers a dedicated Age 9–12 Tech Planner in English and Spanish, built around "What will I do? How much time will I spend? Am I making thoughtful choices?" — [Common Sense planners](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/family-tech-planners) [R]
- **Spain's AEP** runs a Family Digital Plan and undertakes to update it annually. — [Médicos y Pacientes](https://www.medicosypacientes.com/articulo/uso-pantallas-aep/) [R]

### Inferences
**A draft 8–12 agreement skeleton** (my synthesis, for PlayNest writers):
1. **Our no-screen times and places:** meals, car talks, bedrooms, the hour before bed. Parents included.
2. **What comes first:** sleep, moving, homework, friends and family time. Then screens.
3. **What we use and with whom:** the shows and games we've agreed, autoplay and notifications off, shared spaces for now.
4. **Privacy and people:** no personal details, no strangers, ask before posting anyone's photo, including parents asking children.
5. **AI:** use it with a grown-up, for curiosity or tasks, never as a friend or for feelings.
6. **If something goes wrong:** "Come to us. You won't lose your device for telling us." This needs careful wording. The rule is an inference from the AAP's encouragement to come forward, and not itself a published rule.
7. **Parents' promises:** phones away when together; say why if we must use one.
8. **First phone plan:** what readiness looks like (B4), and a basic phone first if needed.
9. **Review date:** start of each school year or term, with the child leading, which fits the "lead the family" theme.

### Gaps
- **No evaluation** of family media agreements' effect on outcomes was found. They rest on expert consensus.
- **The AAP tool itself and the Common Sense PDFs** could not be read in full. Item lists above come from their summary pages.

## C. What's the evidence for family talk, and what conversation themes should PlayNest offer?

### Takeaway
Family talk at meals has a good, if mostly preschool-focused, research record for vocabulary and storytelling, and knowing family stories is linked with wellbeing. The evidence is correlational, and I found no research on "high-low" or "rose-thorn-bud" specifically. PlayNest must write its own questions, because the Family Dinner Project's terms bar reuse.

### Cited Findings

**Evidence**
- Dinner conversation builds vocabulary in young children, according to the Home-School Study of Language and Literacy Development. Uncommon words appear about ten times more often at dinner than in storybooks. Kindergartners who could tell stories later had stronger reading comprehension in 4th and 7th grade. This is a general-audience summary by Anne Fishel, a Harvard clinical psychologist, not the primary study. — [Fishel, The Conversation, 17 Sep 2015](https://theconversation.com/dinnertime-storytelling-makes-kids-voracious-readers-47318) [R]
- In one study, parents given simple prompts (reminisce about shared experiences, ask open-ended "how" and "why" questions, expand on what the child says, follow the child's lead) had children with bigger vocabularies and more complex stories a year later. — [Fishel](https://theconversation.com/dinnertime-storytelling-makes-kids-voracious-readers-47318) [R]. This appears to match Peterson, Jesso & McCabe 1999, listed in the project's references. — [FDP references](https://thefamilydinnerproject.org/uncategorized/family-dinner-references/) [R for the citation; match is my inference]
- Shared meals: at least 3 a week is linked to better nutritional health in a meta-analysis of 182,836 children and adolescents, with limited longitudinal evidence. — [Hammons & Fiese, Pediatrics 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3387875/) [R]
- Citations confirmed from the project's list, content not read: Snow & Beals 2006 (mealtime talk and literacy); Fiese, Winter & Botti 2011 (observational mealtime study, Child Development); Fiese 2006 (*Family Routines and Rituals*); Duke, Lazarus & Fivush 2008 (knowledge of family history as an index of wellbeing). — [FDP references](https://thefamilydinnerproject.org/uncategorized/family-dinner-references/) [R]
- FDP's own "benefits" page lists academic, emotional and health benefits but does not tie specific claims to specific studies. It should not be cited for any individual claim. — [FDP benefits](https://thefamilydinnerproject.org/about-us/benefits-of-family-dinners/) [R]
- PlayNest already cites conversational-turn research (MIT, Romeo et al. 2018) and emotion coaching (Tuning in to Kids, Havighurst et al. 2010), which can anchor copy on back-and-forth and emotional talk. These come from its existing source list and were not re-read here.

**Reuse terms**

### Inferences
- **Technique matters more than question lists.** The most supported prompts are open-ended ("how", "why"), expanding on the child's answer, following their lead and reminiscing. Each stem card could carry a small tip such as "ask one follow-up before you add your own view".
- **Use high-low as a format, not a claim.** It's a sensible structure, but copy should not claim research support for the ritual itself.
- **Include a two-way rule for ages 9–12.** Parents answer too. Fitting the "lead the family" theme, the child draws or writes the questions, and anyone may pass.
- **Age-band differences are deliberate.** 3–5 stems are concrete, about here and now. 6–8 stems ask for reasons. 9–12 stems ask for perspective, values and the future.
- **Sensitive themes need a parent note.** Themes 3, 8, 19, 32, 35, 36 and 37 need a short note about passing, tone and when to seek help, including for families where illness, separation, money stress or bereavement are live.
- **Don't claim outcomes for older children.** Most of the evidence is from preschool and early primary, so PlayNest should describe talk at 9–12 as connection and practice, not as a proven academic booster.

- **"High-low" and "rose-thorn-bud":** I found no research on these specific rituals. This is an absence in a limited search, not proof that none exists.
