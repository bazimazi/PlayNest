## 1. The first weeks at home

### 1.1 WHO 2022 postnatal care guideline: newborn recommendations

Source: *WHO recommendations on maternal and newborn care for a positive postnatal experience*, published 30 Mar 2022. The guideline page was [opened] on who.int. I could not open the 242-page PDF (403). The individual recommendations below were [opened] in the BIGG-REC database, which copies WHO wording.

| Topic | WHO 2022 recommendation | Strength / certainty | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cord care** | "Clean, dry umbilical cord care is recommended for the prevention of neonatal infection." Daily **4% chlorhexidine** is now **context-specific**: only where **harmful traditional substances (e.g. animal dung) are commonly put on the cord**. Where cord care was already hygienic, chlorhexidine made **no difference to deaths**. The evidence comes mostly from Asia and Africa between 2000 and 2010, in settings with many home births and high newborn death rates. Cord infection (omphalitis) has become less common since then. | Recommended. Low certainty (built on moderate-certainty mortality evidence). | [opened] BIGG-REC |
| **First bath** | "The first bath of a term, healthy newborn should be **delayed for at least 24 hours** after birth." The group found **no evidence for an early bath** for meconium staining or for preventing infection passing from the mother. To limit heat loss: keep the room warm, dry the baby straight away, dress the baby in **1–2 more layers than an adult** plus a hat, and keep mother and baby together. | Recommended. Low certainty. | [opened] BIGG-REC |

WHO's **parent-facing** danger-sign list (fact sheet, 14 Mar 2024) [opened]: **feeding problems, reduced activity, difficult breathing, fever, fits or convulsions, feeling cold.**

### 1.2 Cord and bathing: what other bodies say

- **AAP (HealthyChildren, updated 6 Jul 2025)** [opened]
  - Keep the stump clean and dry. "There is **no need to use alcohol**" on it. Fold the nappy below the cord.
  - The stump should dry and fall off **by about 3 weeks**. See a doctor if it is still attached after that.
  - A few drops of blood when it falls off are normal. **Active bleeding** needs a call right away.
  - Signs of infection: **foul-smelling yellowish discharge**, **red skin at the base**, or **crying when the cord or nearby skin is touched**.
  - A small lump (granuloma) may form and usually goes within about a week. If it doesn't, the doctor may treat it.
- **NHS (washing and bathing, reviewed 23 Jul 2024)** [opened]
  - Babies don't need a daily bath. "Topping and tailing" (face, neck, hands, bottom) is enough.
  - Use warm water, not hot. Test it with your wrist or elbow.
  - Plain water, or a baby liquid cleanser.
  - Clean around the cord **only if it is dirty**: cotton wool and warm water, then pat dry.
- **Delayed first bath evidence** [search: J Glob Health 2022 systematic review, PMC9380966, 16 studies, 39,020 newborns]
  - Delaying the bath **at least 24 h** *may* reduce hypothermia. The evidence on deaths is **very uncertain**.
  - Delaying it **at least 6 h** may prevent hypothermia and low blood sugar.
  - WHO's 24-hour figure rests partly on expert consensus.
- **Iran (local data only)**
  - Kerman, 316 mothers (2013): **13.7% mentioned ethanol** and 3.8% povidone-iodine for cleaning the cord. 56.3% mentioned using "drugs" at home (the snippet doesn't say which). [search]
  - A Tehran study grouped mothers by whether they believed in bathing before the cord falls off. This suggests that **"no bath until the cord falls" is a belief some Iranian mothers hold**. [search]
  - Iranian hospital practice on chlorhexidine is unknown. [recall, unverified]
- **App note on bathing:** the first bath usually happens in hospital in Iran. For PlayNest, the useful message is about **the first weeks at home**:
  - sponge baths or short baths are both fine
  - keep the baby warm
  - put nothing on the cord (see §4.6 on kohl)

### 1.3 Newborn danger signs: combined list for parents

Built from WHO 2022 and the WHO fact sheet [opened], the NHS jaundice page [opened], the AAP cord page [opened], and the phase-6 fever note.
- Not feeding, or feeding much less than usual
- A fit or seizure (stiffening, jerking, staring spells)
- Fast breathing, or the skin between or under the ribs pulling in
- Floppy, very sleepy, hard to wake, or not moving normally
- Fever (38°C or more at any time under 3 months; see the fever guide), **or feeling cold / a low temperature**
- Yellow skin **in the first 24 hours**, or **yellow palms or soles at any age**
- The cord bleeding, or red, smelly or oozing at the base
- Blue or grey lips or skin (from the fever and first-aid guides)

## 2. Newborn jaundice (زردی نوزاد)

### 2.1 Normal vs urgent

| | NHS (reviewed 24 Mar 2026) [opened] | AAP / HealthyChildren (updated 8 Aug 2024) [opened] | CPS Caring for Kids (updated May 2025) [opened] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Normal course** | Common and usually harmless. Most noticeable around **day 3**. Usually clears in about **2 weeks**. Easier to see in bright light, harder to see on darker skin. | Most babies get some jaundice in the first few days. It starts on the face and spreads down the body. | — |
| **Urgent / same-day** | Urgent GP, midwife or 111 if the baby is over 24 h old and: you think they have jaundice, it isn't improving or is getting worse, **pee is dark yellow or brown**, or **poo is pale or creamy** (keep a sample to show). | Call if: the skin gets more yellow, the **tummy, arms or legs are yellow**, the **whites of the eyes are yellow**, or the baby is **hard to wake, fussy, or not feeding well**. | Call if the baby: refuses feeds, is very sleepy all the time, has **lost more than 10% of birth weight**, has **yellow or orange arms and legs**, or the jaundice seems to be getting worse. |
| **Emergency** | 999 / A&E if: **jaundice in a baby less than 24 h old**, or a jaundiced baby who is sleepier than normal, not feeding, **floppy or stiff**, has a high or low temperature, **no wet nappies**, or has difficulty breathing. | — | — |
| **Treatment** | Feed **8–12 times a day** and wake sleepy babies for feeds. Phototherapy (special light) for about 48 h, usually in hospital. Rarely, a blood transfusion. | Babies discharged before 48 h should be seen **within 2 days**. | Follow-up and a repeat test if the bilirubin level is near the treatment line. |
| **Sunlight** | Not given as a treatment. | "Putting your baby into **sunlight is not a safe way to treat jaundice**." | "Exposing your baby to sunlight (directly or indirectly) **can be harmful**." |
| **Risk factors named** | — | Early birth (more than 2 weeks early), jaundice in the first 24 h, breastfeeding not yet going well, bruising or a scalp bleed from the birth, a parent or sibling who needed light therapy, and **"eating fava beans or using mothballs, antibiotics, henna, or herbal remedies"** | — |

**AAP 2022 clinical guideline** (Kemper et al., *Pediatrics* 150(3):e2022058859). Full text 403. [opened] via the AAFP summary (Espinosa & Brown, *Am Fam Physician* 2023;107(6):661–4).
- **Jaundice seen in the first 24 hours** means measuring bilirubin **immediately**.
- After 24 h, don't rely on looking at the skin. **Visual estimates can be off by up to 15 mg/dL.**
- Risk factors for brain injury from bilirubin (neurotoxicity) include **G6PD deficiency** or other causes of red cells breaking down (haemolysis), and birth **before 38 weeks**.
- Consider **G6PD testing** in babies with severe, late-onset or unexplained jaundice. A test done close to a haemolytic episode can be **falsely normal**.
- Support breastfeeding from the first hour, aiming for **at least 8 feeds in 24 h**.
- **Do not give dextrose (sugar) water or plain water** to breastfed babies to prevent jaundice. If intake really is too low, use formula or donor milk.
- **Prolonged jaundice** (still yellow at 3–4 weeks if breastfed, or 2 weeks if formula-fed) needs a check of total and direct bilirubin.

**NICE CG98** (2010, updated 2016). Page 403.
- [search] Suspected or obvious jaundice in the **first 24 h** needs serum bilirubin **within 2 hours** and medical review within 6 h.
- [search] Parents should be told to check nappies for **dark urine or pale chalky stools**.
- [search] Clinicians check the naked baby in bright, ideally natural, light. This is about *spotting* jaundice, not treating it.
- [search, needs verification on the live page] NICE says **not** to use any of the following to treat jaundice: agar, albumin, barbiturates, charcoal, cholestyramine, clofibrate, D-penicillamine, glycerin, **manna**, metalloporphyrins, riboflavin, traditional Chinese medicine, acupuncture or homeopathy. **This is directly relevant to ترنجبین and شیرخشت (both are "manna").**
- [recall, unverified] NICE defines prolonged jaundice as **more than 14 days in term babies and more than 21 days in preterm babies**.

### 2.2 Why "pale poo / dark pee" matters

All three bodies above flag pale stools and dark urine. The reason is that they can signal a **liver or bile-duct problem** (for example biliary atresia), where early surgery matters. [recall, unverified for the biliary-atresia timing. Standard teaching is surgery ideally before about 60 days.] App wording: "Pale, chalky or cream-coloured poo, or dark yellow or brown pee, in a yellow baby needs a doctor the same day."

### 2.3 Iran: traditional remedies for newborn jaundice

**How common** (local studies only; there is no national figure):
- **Qom, 2015** (Heydari et al., *J Ethnopharmacol* 2016;193:637–42) [opened abstract via Europe PMC]
  - 212 parents of jaundiced babies. **78% (165) used traditional methods**, mostly herbal.
  - Plants most cited: **Cotoneaster nummularioides** (the source of **شیرخشت shirkhesht**), **Cichorium intybus** (chicory, کاسنی), **Alhagi maurorum** (camel thorn, the source of **ترنجبین taranjebin**) and **Descurainia sophia** (flixweed, **خاکشیر**).
  - **Manna was the most used plant part**. The commonest preparations were distillation (عرق) and soaking.
  - Use was linked to parents' past experience with traditional medicine and to how risky they thought it was.
- **Mashhad 2008–2012** (Boskabadi & Bagheri, *Avicenna J Phytomed* 2015;5(6):479–84, PMC4678493) [opened]
  - 454 term babies. 211 had been given breast milk plus traditional supplements: **camel thorn (taranjebin) 63%, sugar water 32.3%, flixweed 4.7%**, given **3–10 times a day**.
  - These babies had **fewer feeds** (10.3 vs 12.3 a day), **shorter feeds** (22.7 vs 30.5 min) and a **later first breastfeed** (195 vs 90 min). More of them had poor positioning.
  - This is a case-control study, so it shows association, not cause. It did not enrol jaundiced babies, and its weight-gain claim goes beyond its data.
  - **Why it matters:** poor breastfeeding intake is itself a recognised cause of early jaundice ("breastfeeding jaundice", AAP/AAFP [opened]). So remedies that displace feeds may make things worse, not better.
- **Saravan:** 77.5% of 59 mothers had given a jaundiced baby "antibiotics, remedies or herbs". [search, small sample]
- **Neyshabour** (Mehdizadeh et al., *Iran J Obstet Gynecol Infertil* 2021;24(5):48–58) [opened abstract]
  - Qualitative study, 103 mothers.
  - Beliefs fell into **preventive** (nutrition) and **therapeutic** groups (herbal, non-herbal, nutrition and alternative remedies).
  - The authors flag **delayed referral for treatment** as a concern.
- **Shahrekord ethnobotany:** flixweed (خاکشیر) seed is listed as an "anti-jaundice" remedy. [search]

**Do the remedies work?**
- **Purgative manna (شیرخشت, Cotoneaster)** (Sajedi & Fatollahierad, *Iran J Pharm Res* 2019, PMC6706735) [opened]
  - Systematic review and meta-analysis: **7 RCTs, 812 newborns**, 6 pooled.
  - **Every trial gave manna together with phototherapy**, never instead of it.
  - Pooled reductions in bilirubin were about **1.5 to 2.8 mg/dL at 12–48 h**, with a hospital stay about **0.9 days shorter**.
  - The trials were weak: three used alternation instead of true randomisation, only one described allocation concealment, and only three were double-blind.
  - **Two trials reported listlessness from water loss (osmotic diarrhoea).** No long-term safety data. G6PD status is not discussed.
  - Products varied, including the commercial Iranian drop "**Bilineaster**".
  - Authors' conclusion: it "could be used in combination with phototherapy", and better trials are needed.
- **Manna in a Tehran RCT** (97 babies, phototherapy alone vs phototherapy plus oral manna): **no difference** in bilirubin at 0, 24 and 48 h. [search: magiran/JIMS abstract snippet]
- **Taranjebin (Alhagi) as prevention** (Mashhad trial, 10/20/30% solutions from 4 h of age for 3 days vs placebo): outcome not visible. A rat study exists. [search]
- **NICE lists "manna" among treatments not to use.** [search; see §2.1]
- **Sugar water / dextrose water:** AAP 2022 says **do not** give it to prevent jaundice. [opened via AAFP]
- **G6PD and taranjebin or shirkhesht:** **I found no study either way.** Do not claim that these remedies trigger haemolysis in G6PD-deficient babies. Do not claim that they are safe for them either.

**Bottom line for the app:** the best Iranian evidence (an adjunct trial of shirkhesht alongside phototherapy) is low quality and never tested the remedies **instead of** medical care. The real dangers are:
1. **delay** in getting the bilirubin checked
2. **fewer breastfeeds** when other drinks fill the baby's stomach
3. **diarrhoea and dehydration** from purgative manna

The respectful line is: "These remedies have been used for generations. Studies so far haven't shown they can replace a bilirubin test or light treatment, and drinks other than milk can mean fewer feeds, which can make jaundice worse."

**Sunlight in Iran:** putting a yellow baby by a sunny window is widely described in Iran, but I have no opened source on how common it is. [recall, unverified] AAP and CPS both say it is **not safe** (risk of sunburn and overheating, and it delays proper testing). NHS is silent.

## 3. G6PD deficiency (کمبود آنزیم G6PD / فاویسم)

- **What it is (MedlinePlus, updated 12 Apr 2023)** [opened]
  - An inherited, **X-linked** enzyme deficiency. Red blood cells break down early when exposed to triggers.
  - Triggers: **infections**, **some drugs** (certain antibiotics and antimalarials), and **fava beans** (favism), including inhaled fava pollen.
  - G6PD deficiency is "a significant cause of mild to severe jaundice in newborns". About **400 million** people have it worldwide, and it is most common in Africa, Asia, the Mediterranean and the **Middle East**.
- **healthdirect Australia (reviewed Aug 2025)** [opened]
  - Avoid **fava (broad) beans**, **naphthalene (mothballs)**, some medicines (including **aspirin** and large doses of vitamin C), tonic water and blueberries.
  - Affected babies may develop jaundice, treated with phototherapy, or rarely exchange transfusion.
  - "If you are breastfeeding a child with G6PD deficiency, **avoid eating fava beans**."
  - Haemolysis signs that need prompt care: **dark urine, yellow skin or eyes, very pale skin, fast heartbeat, breathlessness**, tiredness, fever.
- **Other hospital leaflets** [search]
  - Singapore General Hospital, Sidra (Qatar), AboutKidsHealth (Canada): avoid **mothballs** and **henna**.
  - SGH: people in contact with the baby should not store their clothes with mothballs.
  - InfantRisk: a breastfeeding mother **who has** G6PD deficiency needs no special steps, but **fava beans should be avoided while breastfeeding a G6PD-deficient baby**.
  - **Henna** appears on AAP's jaundice risk list [opened] and on several leaflets [search].
- **Iran**
  - Iran runs newborn screening for G6PD deficiency through the Ministry of Health. The heel-prick sample is also used for hypothyroidism and PKU. [search: Mazandaran 2007–2010 study and a 2020 review]
  - **Mazandaran, 2007–2010:** 115,622 newborns screened, **6.1% deficient**, below the 12–13% the authors expected. They recommended continued screening and **parent and media education against fava beans and naphthalene**. [search: magiran abstract snippet; magiran 403]
  - **Sari** (Kosaryan et al., *Pediatr Hematol Oncol* 2014;31(1):95–100) [opened abstract]: the **fluorescent spot test misses many affected babies, especially girls**. In 365 newborns the spot test found 13 deficient boys and **no girls**. The quantitative assay found 19 boys and **28 girls**.
  - **Prevalence varies widely by province** [search: various regional studies; not directly comparable]:
    - about **6.7% nationally** (2014 meta-analysis: men 8.8%, women 2.2%)
    - **8.65–16.4%** in Mazandaran and Gilan
    - about **12%** in Shiraz and the south
    - up to **19%** in Larestan and the southeast
    - about **1.4–2%** in Ardabil and Tehran
- **App implications**
  - "A normal G6PD screen" is reassuring but not a guarantee, **especially for girls**. Any jaundice red flag still means a check.
  - If a baby **is** G6PD-deficient: no fava beans for the child, keep **mothballs (نفتالین)** out of the home and away from baby clothes, avoid **henna** on the baby, and **tell every doctor and pharmacist before any medicine**. A breastfeeding mother of an affected baby should avoid fava beans (healthdirect).
  - **Do not** give a medicine list or say "these drugs are safe". Point the parent to the child's doctor or pharmacist.
  - **Fava-bean season** (spring, باقلا) and Nowruz-season cleaning with mothballs are worth a seasonal reminder. [recall, unverified that this is a documented seasonal pattern in Iran; Iranian favism literature should confirm]

## 4. Swaddling and قنداق

### 4.1 What bodies say

| | Stance | Hips | When to stop | Other | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AAP** (HealthyChildren "Swaddling: is it safe?", 15 Jul 2022; Safe Sleep guide, 11 Sep 2026) | Optional. **Swaddling does not reduce SIDS risk.** | Wrapping that **straightens and tightly wraps the legs** can contribute to hip dysplasia. AAP supports **"hip-healthy swaddling"** (legs able to bend up and out). | At **any sign of trying to roll**, usually 3–4 months but **as early as 2 months**. | Always on the back. Watch for overheating (sweating, damp hair, flushed cheeks, fast breathing). **No weighted swaddles** or weighted objects. A loose, unwrapped swaddle in the cot is a hazard. Sleep sacks without arm wrapping can be used as long as you like. | [opened] |
| **Lullaby Trust (UK)** (reviewed 1 Feb 2025; dates on the page are inconsistent) | Optional: "If you choose to swaddle…". | "Keep swaddles **looser around the hips**." Tight swaddling can cause DDH. | When the baby **shows signs of rolling**, stop swaddling with the arms inside. | **Not above the shoulders.** **Thin material, not blankets.** Room 16–20°C. **Never swaddle when bed-sharing, or if the baby has an infection or fever.** | [opened] |
| **Red Nose (Australia)** (16 Apr 2025, updated 26 Jun 2025) | Wrapping is acceptable when done safely. | "Must allow for **hip and chest wall movement**". | At **signs of rolling or 3 months, whichever comes first**. | **Only lightweight cotton or muslin.** Bunny rugs and blankets are not safe. Don't cover the face or head. No wrapping on a shared sleep surface. | [opened] |
| **IHDI / POSNA / AAP joint position** | — | Hips in **slight flexion and abduction** (bent up and out), knees slightly bent. **Firm around the arms, loose around the legs.** | — | Tight straight-leg wrapping is linked to dysplasia. A news quote from an IHDI director says about 30% of tightly wrapped babies develop a dislocated hip. **Treat that as an informal estimate.** | [search] (hipdysplasia.org down) |
| **NHS DDH page** (reviewed 11 Mar 2026) | Swaddling not mentioned. | Risk factors: **girls, family history, breech**. Signs: a click, **one leg moving differently**, **legs of different lengths**, dragging a leg or a limp. Diagnosed by newborn exam and ultrasound. | — | — | [opened] |

**SIDS evidence** (Pease et al., *Pediatrics* 2016, individual-data meta-analysis of 4 case-control studies, 760 SIDS cases) [search]:
- Overall OR **1.58 (95% CI 0.97–2.58)**, which is not statistically significant.
- Risk was much higher if a swaddled baby was placed or rolled onto the **side (OR 3.16)** or **front (OR 12.99, very imprecise)**.
- On the back: OR 1.93.
- Limited evidence that risk **rises with age**, about double over 6 months.
- There was no common definition of swaddling across the studies.

### 4.2 Hip evidence and Iran

- Animal study: **straight-leg swaddling raised DDH rates** in newborn rats, most when swaddling was early or prolonged (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2012). [search]
- Turkey case-control study (2019): **family history, breech and swaddling** were the three significant risk factors, and swaddling was also linked to more severe dysplasia. [search]
- Historical populations (Japan, Navajo / Native American, Turkey): moving from straight-leg to safe swaddling was followed by roughly a **sixfold fall** in DDH. [Cited in the Iranian paper below; primary studies not opened]
- **Tehran** (Vafaee et al., *Arch Bone Joint Surg* 2017, Imam Khomeini Hospital) [opened PDF]
  - Ultrasound of **1,073** healthy term newborns (Mar 2013–Mar 2014). DDH was confirmed in **86 (8%)**, which the authors call "significantly high".
  - Significant links with **breech**, **torticollis** and **metatarsus adductus**.
  - The introduction says "**Swaddling is the most related factor in developing DDH after birth**".
  - These babies were scanned as newborns, so the study **did not measure swaddling exposure**.
  - The authors note that **Iran has no nationwide DDH screening programme** of the kind they recommend.
- *Med J Islam Repub Iran* 2024: DDH in **25.8% of infants with risk factors vs 2.8% without**. No swaddling finding in the abstract. [search]
- *Iran J Radiol* 2020 lists swaddling among possible postnatal factors. [search]
- **Gap:** **I found no Iranian study measuring how common traditional tight قنداق is today, or linking it directly to DDH.** Tight straight-leg قنداق (legs pulled straight and bound, sometimes with a band, «قنداق سفت / بند قنداق») is widely described as traditional in Iran. [recall, unverified]

### 4.3 App angle

Respect the tradition: wrapping calms many babies and Iranian grandmothers know it well. Change only the **legs**: "arms snug, legs free like a frog". Then add the safety rules: on the back, not over the shoulders, a thin wrap, never with a fever, never in the parents' bed, and stop at the first sign of rolling.

## 5. Other traditional and early practices (Iran and the region)

### 5.1 Prelacteal feeds and water before 6 months

- **WHO Q&A "Breastfeeding"** (2 Oct 2024) [opened]
  - "No, you should not give water to a baby under six months of age, **even on hot days**."
  - Breast milk has all the water a baby needs. If the baby seems thirsty, offer an extra breastfeed.
  - Water "can cause diarrhoea and lead to malnutrition" and can mean the baby drinks less breast milk or stops breastfeeding early.
- **WHO definition of exclusive breastfeeding:** no other food or drink, not even water, except ORS and drops or syrups of vitamins, minerals or medicines. [search: WHO EMRO page]
- **Iran: Shiraz cohort** (Zarshenas et al., *Birth* 2019;46(1):137–45) [opened abstract via Europe PMC]
  - **40.8%** of hospital-born babies received a **traditional prelacteal food**, 34.9% formula, and only **32.2%** were breastfed within an hour.
  - **Babies without skin-to-skin contact were more likely to get traditional prelacteal foods.** Caesarean births were *less* likely to get them.
  - The same group found prelacteal and in-hospital formula feeds were **linked to shorter breastfeeding** (*IJERPH* 2020, PMC7068444) [opened abstract].
  - Their thesis defines prelacteal feeds as including **sugar water, formula, herbal tea, honey or dates**. [search]
- **Mashhad:** the supplements study in §2.3 (camel thorn, sugar water, flixweed linked to fewer and shorter feeds) [opened].
- **Gap:** national prevalence of prelacteal feeds in Iran was not found.

### 5.2 Herbal waters, gripe water, «نبات داغ», «عرق نعناع», «گل گاوزبان»

- **Use in Iran:** in Kerman (Bagherian et al., 2021, PMC8512939), **herbal medicines were the most common thing mothers used for colic**. [search; sample size unclear (150 vs 18)]
- **A Mashhad trial** compared **fennel extract with gripe water syrup** for colic and found both improved crying time with no difference between them. There was no placebo arm in the snippet. [search]
- **Iranian review** (Nourolahi & Ghiasi 2025) of herbal distillates (عرقیات) in Iranian traditional medicine: herbal products for infants should be **supervised by qualified health professionals**. [search]
- **Gripe water:** no clinical studies (2005 review). In the US it is an unapproved drug. [search]
- **Star anise tea:** 7 babies aged 2–12 weeks developed neurological reactions, including **seizures**, likely from contamination with toxic Japanese star anise (*Pediatrics* 2004). FDA warned in 2003 (about 15 infants affected). [search] It is relevant if «بادیان ستاره‌ای» tea is given to babies in Iran. [recall, unverified whether it is used]
- **«نبات داغ» (hot rock-sugar water), «گل گاوزبان» (borage/echium flower tea), «عرق نعناع» (mint distillate): no specific studies found for infants.**
  - What *is* established: anything other than milk before 6 months goes against WHO advice (§5.1). Sugar water is specifically advised against by AAP for jaundice [opened].
  - **Borage-family plants can contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are liver-toxic.** [recall, unverified for Iranian گل گاوزبان (Echium amoenum). An expert check is needed before saying anything about this in the app.]
- **Wording rule:** say "**not before 6 months, and ask a doctor after that**". Don't call specific Iranian herbs toxic unless an opened source says so.

### 5.3 Honey (including on the dummy) and botulism

- **AAPD Policy on Pacifiers** (revised 2024, Reference Manual 2026) [opened]: "The US Food and Drug Administration recommends that infants and young children **not be given pacifiers containing or dipped in honey**." Infant botulism cases in Texas (2018) were traced to honey-filled pacifiers.
- **Lullaby Trust** [opened]: never put anything sweet, such as honey, on a dummy.
- **CPS** [opened]: don't dip a pacifier in sugar or honey (harms teeth, and honey carries a botulism risk under 1).
- **NHS** (phase 6) [opened]: no honey under 12 months.
- **Infant botulism signs:** constipation (sometimes for days), **weak or unusual cry**, poor sucking, **floppiness, drooping eyelids**. It is a medical emergency. Pasteurising honey does not kill the spores. [search: CDC archived copy, poison.org]
- Iran context: honey, sometimes mixed with butter or dates as a first taste (تحنیک / کام برداشتن), is a known custom. [recall, unverified for prevalence. **Take care: tahnik is a religious practice. Copy must stay respectful, for example "if your family follows this custom, ask about a version without honey" (a tiny smear of date has also been discussed by Muslim scholars).** Do not rule on religious matters.]

### 5.4 Kohl / surma (سرمه) on babies' eyes

- **FDA**, "Kohl, Kajal, Al-Kahal, Surma, Tiro, Tozali, or Kwalli: By Any Name, Beware of Lead Poisoning" (content current as of 28 Feb 2022) [opened]
  - These products "often contain high levels of lead". Lead sometimes makes up **more than half the weight**. One tiro sample linked to an infant's lead poisoning was **82.6% lead**.
  - Lead is "especially serious for children": anaemia, kidney problems, **brain damage**, and learning and behaviour problems from low-level exposure.
  - Some people **paint the newborn's umbilical stump with kohl**.
  - Advice: stop using the product and ask a doctor to test the child for lead.
  - None of these products is FDA-approved, and there is an import alert on them.
- **Iranian FDA-related review** (*J Dermatol Cosmet*, jdc.tums.ac.ir, 2025) [search; page redirect loop]
  - Kohl has the **highest lead** of the eye cosmetics studied.
  - It is "widely used in Middle Eastern countries, **especially among infants**", because people believe it protects or heals.
  - Repeated use in children can raise blood lead.
- **Meta-analysis on kohl and blood lead in children:** the evidence is limited by bias and small studies. [search] → keep the wording proportionate ("can contain lead", not "will poison").
- Practice: kohl or surma is applied to babies to "strengthen the eyes" or against the evil eye (چشم‌زخم). [search: J Cosmet Sci] Prevalence in Iran today is **unknown**.

### 5.5 Salting the newborn (نمک زدن)

- **Turkey:** salting the skin or adding salt to the bath was the **most common traditional practice (59.9%)** in a single-hospital survey of 305 mothers. Case series describe **severe hypernatraemia, seizures, developmental delay and deaths**, plus **burns and skin ulcers**. [search]
- **Iran:** salting is described as continuing in "**western Iran**" among other regions (Hektoen International essay, 2019) [opened]. The essay itself cites no Iran-specific data. A figure that "**50% in an Iranian survey believe salt strengthens the muscles**" appeared in a search summary but **could not be traced to a source** [search, untraced].
- **Bottom line:** evidence of the practice in Iran is **thin**. Include it only as a short "if your family does this" note, not as a headline.

### 5.6 Things applied to the cord

- WHO [opened]: harmful substances on the cord (animal dung is the example) are the main reason chlorhexidine is still recommended in some places.
- FDA [opened]: kohl on the stump.
- AAP [opened]: not even alcohol is needed.
- Iranian practices on the cord (oil, powders, kohl, ash): **no Iranian data found**. [gap]

## 7. Teething (دندان درآوردن)

The app already has a teeth guide. These are the points to add or confirm. The phase-6 note covered the NHS and FDA 2024 points.

- **AAP** "Teething Pain Relief" (updated 9 Jul 2025) [opened]
  - **Linked to teething:** fussiness, drooling, chewing, and a drool rash around the mouth.
  - **Not caused by teething:** **fever, diarrhoea, unusually heavy crying, nappy rash**. It doesn't weaken immunity, although mouthing objects spreads germs.
  - **What helps:**
    - soft rubber teethers
    - a **cold, damp washcloth (not frozen solid)**
    - from 6 months, a sippy cup of cold water
    - gum massage with a clean finger
    - comfort routines
    - paracetamol only if the doctor recommends it (no doses in the app)
  - **Avoid:**
    - numbing gels (drool washes them away)
    - **benzocaine and lidocaine**
    - **alcohol on the gums**
    - **homeopathic tablets or gels, especially with belladonna or coffea cruda**
    - **liquid-filled teethers**
    - **teething necklaces**
    - **frozen-solid objects**
  - **Call the doctor for:** a fever, diarrhoea for more than a day, or more crying than usual. These are not caused by teething.
- **NHS** (phase 6, reviewed 14 May 2026) [opened in phase 6]: allows a "slightly raised temperature **below 38°C**". There is **no evidence that teething causes diarrhoea**.
- **FDA benzocaine** (23 May 2018) [search]
  - OTC oral benzocaine should **not be used for infants and children under 2**. The risk is **methaemoglobinaemia**, which lowers the blood's oxygen.
  - FDA asked makers to stop marketing these products for teething.
  - 119 cases reported from 2009 to 2017, 11 of them in children under 2. 4 deaths across all ages.
- **FDA teething jewellery** (20 Dec 2018) [search; the FDA URL returned 404]
  - Covers necklaces and bracelets made of amber, wood, marble or silicone.
  - An **18-month-old was strangled** by an amber necklace during a nap. A **7-month-old choked** on wooden bracelet beads while supervised.
  - Amber's "succinic acid" claim is unproven.
  - FDA advises parents not to use these products, including for sensory needs.
- **FDA homeopathic teething tablets** [search]
  - Lab tests in 2010 and 2016–17 found **inconsistent belladonna levels, sometimes far above the label**.
  - In Jan 2017 FDA said to stop using and throw away Hyland's teething products. The maker later recalled them.
- **Teething biscuits and rusks:** no opened source. Standard advice is to watch for choking and avoid sugary rusks. [recall, unverified]
- **Iran:** «آش دندونی» (first-tooth soup party) is a joyful custom the guide can celebrate. [recall] Teething gels sold in Iranian pharmacies: ingredients unknown. [gap]

## 8. Settling methods for babies 6 months and older (آموزش خواب)

### 8.1 The evidence

- **Mindell et al. 2006, AASM review** (*Sleep* 29(10):1263–76) [opened abstract]
  - 52 studies. **94%** found behavioural interventions effective, and more than 80% of children improved, with gains lasting 3–6 months.
  - Strongest support: unmodified extinction and **preventive parent education**.
  - Also supported: graduated extinction, **bedtime fading / positive routines**, scheduled awakenings.
  - The companion **AASM practice parameters** (Morgenthaler 2006, *Sleep* 29(10):1277–81) cover ages **0 to 4y 11m**:
    - "**Standards**": unmodified extinction, **extinction with parental presence** (≈ camping out) and preventive parent education
    - "**Guidelines**": graduated extinction, bedtime fading and scheduled awakenings
    - **Not enough evidence to prefer any one method.**
- **Hiscock et al. 2007** (*Arch Dis Child* 92(11):952–8; Melbourne) [opened abstract]
  - Cluster RCT, 328 mothers reporting a sleep problem at 7 months. Nurses taught **controlled comforting or camping out**.
  - Sleep problems: **56% vs 68% at 10 months**, **39% vs 55% at 12 months**.
  - **Lower maternal depression scores** and lower costs.
- **Price et al. 2012, 5-year follow-up** (*Pediatrics* 130(4):643–51) [opened abstract]
  - 225 of 326 families at child age 6.
  - **No differences on any outcome**: emotional and conduct problems, sleep, **chronic stress (cortisol) 29% vs 22% (not significant)**, child–parent closeness and conflict, attachment, maternal depression and anxiety, parenting style.
  - Conclusion: **"no marked long-lasting effects, positive or negative"**. The methods can be used to reduce short- to medium-term sleep problems.
- **Gradisar et al. 2016** (*Pediatrics* 137(6):e20151486) [opened abstract]
  - 43 babies aged **6–16 months**: graduated extinction vs **bedtime fading** vs sleep education (control).
  - Both methods cut the time taken to fall asleep. Graduated extinction also cut night wakings.
  - **Cortisol fell slightly** in both groups compared with controls.
  - No 12-month differences in emotional or behavioural problems or **attachment (Strange Situation)**.
  - **A small trial.**
- **Critiques**
  - **Middlemiss et al. 2012** (*Early Hum Dev* 88(4):227–32) [opened abstract]: 25 babies aged 4–10 months in a 5-day inpatient extinction programme. By day 3 the babies had stopped crying, but **their cortisol stayed high** while the mothers' fell. Small, no control group, and an inpatient setting. It is often cited, so it should be acknowledged honestly.
  - **Douglas & Hill 2013** (*J Dev Behav Pediatr* 34(7):497–507) [opened abstract], systematic review of **the first six months**: behavioural sleep interventions **have not been shown to reduce crying, prevent later sleep problems or protect against postnatal depression**. Used across whole populations, they risk unintended harms: more problem crying, **earlier stopping of breastfeeding**, maternal anxiety, and **higher SIDS risk if babies sleep apart from carers**.
  - **This is why the app should say "not before 6 months".**
- **Culture:** Mindell et al. 2010 (*Sleep Med* 11(3):274–80) [opened abstract] surveyed 29,287 children.
  - **Bed-sharing ranged from 5.8% (New Zealand) to 83.2% (Vietnam)**, with **much higher room- and bed-sharing in Asian countries**.
  - Parents' perception of a "sleep problem" ranged from 11% to 76%, so **what counts as a problem is partly cultural**.
  - **No Middle Eastern country was included.**
  - Iranian room-sharing and bed-sharing rates: [gap; recall, unverified that both are the norm].

### 8.2 What bodies say

- **AAP** "Getting your baby to sleep" (4 Jun 2024) [opened]
  - From **4 months**, put the baby down **drowsy but awake**.
  - Babies don't have regular sleep cycles until about 4 months.
  - Don't rush in at every brief waking, but feed, change or comfort a baby who needs it.
  - **Named methods aren't covered on this page.**
  - AAP safe sleep: **room-share for at least the first 6 months**, and **no bed-sharing** (AAP's stance). [opened]
- **NHS** "Helping your baby to sleep" (28 Jan 2025) [opened]
  - Calm, dim nights and active days. A simple bedtime routine (bath, nappy, story, cuddle).
  - In the early weeks babies may settle only when held.
  - **Room-share for every sleep for at least 6 months.**
  - **No mention of controlled crying.**
- **Raising Children Network** (controlled comforting, camping out): **403, not covered.** [recall, unverified] RCN presents controlled comforting and camping out (gradual withdrawal) as options **from 6 months**, not for younger babies, and says **camping out suits parents who don't want to leave the baby to cry**. Verify before quoting.
- **AASM** (see 8.1): the methods work, and no single method is preferred.

### 8.3 The methods in plain words (for the app)

- **Bedtime routine and positive routines:** same order each night, calm, 20–30 min. Low effort, and no crying needed. Evidence: good as part of packages and as prevention.
- **Bedtime fading:** temporarily move bedtime later to when the child naturally falls asleep, then bring it earlier by 15 minutes every few nights. **No planned crying.** Evidence: Gradisar 2016 (small), AASM "Guideline" level.
- **Camping out / chair method (extinction with parental presence):** the parent stays in the room, close by, offering minimal attention, and moves the chair further away every few nights. **Fits families where room-sharing is the norm.** Rated an AASM "Standard". Used in Hiscock 2007.
- **Controlled comforting / graduated extinction:** brief checks at lengthening intervals. Fastest effect on wakings (Gradisar). No long-term harm found (Price 2012). Many parents find it emotionally hard. **Optional, never required.**
- **Responsive settling / "do nothing differently":** a legitimate choice. Sleep consolidates with age for most children, and a problem is only a problem if it is a problem for the family.
- **Not before 6 months** (Douglas & Hill; RCN [recall]). **Not when the baby is ill, teething badly, or the family is going through a big change.** Check for medical causes first (snoring or pauses in breathing, reflux, eczema itch, ear pain).
- **Safety overlay:** whatever the method, keep safe sleep rules (link to the safe-sleep guide). **If the baby is in the parents' bed**, the safe-sleep guide's bed-sharing risk list applies (NHS conditions and the AAP stance).

## 9. Disagreements (summary for writers)

1. **Cord care:** WHO says dry cord care by default, with chlorhexidine only where harmful substances are commonly used. AAP says not even alcohol. Some Iranian mothers use ethanol (Kerman). → App: "Clean and dry. Nothing on it unless your health worker tells you."
2. **First bath:** WHO says wait at least 24 h (low certainty). NHS and AAP give no such rule for home. → Mostly a hospital decision. At home: "There's no rush. Keep the baby warm."
3. **Sunlight for jaundice:** AAP says "not safe", CPS says "can be harmful", NHS gives no treatment advice. NICE says check babies in natural *light* (for spotting jaundice, not treating it). → App: no sunlight treatment.
4. **Manna (shirkhesht / taranjebin):**
   - An Iranian meta-analysis says it may lower bilirubin when added to phototherapy (low-quality trials).
   - A single Tehran RCT found no effect.
   - NICE lists manna among treatments **not** to use [search].
   - → App: say plainly that the studies disagree and none tested manna *instead of* treatment.
5. **Breastfeeding mothers and fava beans** (if the baby is G6PD-deficient): healthdirect and InfantRisk say avoid. Some leaflets go further (all beans, soya). → App: "Ask your baby's doctor. Many advise avoiding fava beans while breastfeeding a G6PD-deficient baby."
6. **G6PD screening reliability:** Iran screens newborns, but a Sari study found the spot test **misses many girls**. → App: "a normal screen doesn't rule out every case".
7. **Swaddling stop point:** Red Nose says signs of rolling **or 3 months, whichever is first**. AAP says signs of rolling (usually 3–4 months, can be 2). Lullaby Trust says signs of rolling. → App: "stop at the first sign of rolling, and by about 3 months at the latest if you want the most cautious rule".
8. **Swaddling and SIDS:** AAP says swaddling doesn't reduce SIDS. Lullaby Trust calls it optional. The Pease meta-analysis found risk mainly when on the side or front, and possibly in older babies.
9. **Dummy start:** AAP, Lullaby Trust and CPS say wait until breastfeeding is established (about 3–4 weeks). A Cochrane review (cited by AAPD) found no effect on breastfeeding to 4 months. BFHI moved from "no dummies" to "counsel". → App: "Many experts suggest waiting until breastfeeding is going well, usually 3–4 weeks."
10. **Dummy stop age:**
    - Lullaby Trust: 6–12 months.
    - AAPD: ear-infection risk after 12 months, bite effects after 18 months, stop by 36 months.
    - AAP: teeth can be affected past 2–4 years.
    - CPS: by 3–4 years.
    - → App: "Using it only for sleep after 6 months, and stopping somewhere between 1 and 3 years, is in line with most advice."
11. **Dummy clips:** Lullaby Trust says no attachments. CPS says short-ribbon clips are OK. AAP says nothing attached during sleep. → App: "**Never** a cord around the neck. **Nothing attached during sleep.**"
12. **Teething and temperature:** AAP says teething doesn't cause fever. NHS allows a slightly raised temperature below 38°C. → App: "38°C or more is not teething."
13. **Sleep training:**
    - AASM, Price 2012 and Gradisar 2016 found the methods effective with no long-term harm.
    - Douglas & Hill found no benefit under 6 months.
    - Middlemiss raised a short-term physiological concern (small study).
    - The NHS page is silent. The AAP parent page covers only "drowsy but awake".
    - → App: present it as **options, including not doing it**.
14. **Room-sharing and bed-sharing:** already in the safe-sleep guide. AAP says never bed-share. NHS gives conditions for safer bed-sharing. Iranian norms are likely closer to the NHS harm-reduction approach. Just link to the existing guide.

  - "A dummy is a personal choice. If you use one, offering it when you put your baby down to sleep may lower the risk of sudden infant death. If it falls out once they're asleep, you don't need to put it back."
  - "If you're breastfeeding, many experts suggest waiting until feeding is going well, often around 3–4 weeks."
  - "Never dip a dummy in honey, sugar, نبات or syrup. Never tie a dummy around your baby's neck, and don't attach anything to it during sleep. Check it often and replace it if it's cracked."
  - "Using a dummy for a long time can affect how teeth meet and can add to ear infections. Most advice suggests limiting it to sleep after 6–12 months and stopping by about 3 years. Gentle, positive steps work better than shaming."
  - "Thumb-sucking is normal comfort. Most children stop on their own. Talk to a dentist if it continues strongly after 3–4 years or once adult teeth arrive."

**Guide G — "Teething: what's real and what's risky" (4–24 months)**
- Best merged into the existing teeth guide.
- Sections:
  1. Signs
  2. Myths
  3. What helps
  4. What to avoid and why
  5. When it's not teething
- Safe wording:
  - "Teething can make babies drool, chew and be grumpy. It does **not** cause a real fever (38°C or more), diarrhoea or serious illness. If your baby has these, look for another cause and call a doctor if worried."
  - "Try a clean finger to rub the gums, a firm rubber teether, or a cold (not frozen) damp cloth."
  - "Avoid amber or bead necklaces and bracelets (choking and strangulation), numbing gels with benzocaine or lidocaine, homeopathic teething tablets, and liquid-filled or frozen-solid teethers."
  - "Celebrate the first tooth! «آش دندونی» is a lovely family tradition."

**Guide H — "Settling to sleep after 6 months: your options" (6 months–3 years)**
- Sections:
  1. Is there a problem? (Only if it's a problem for your family)
  2. Rule out medical causes
  3. A good routine first
  4. Gentle, no-cry options: bedtime fading, the chair method
  5. Check-and-comfort (controlled comforting), if you want it
  6. What the research says about harm
  7. Room-sharing families
  8. Why not before 6 months
  9. Safety
- Safe wording:
  - "There's no single right way. Many families room-share or bed-share and are happy. If your nights are working for you, you don't need to change anything."
  - "If you'd like to change things, start with a calm, predictable bedtime routine. Many families see improvements from that alone."
  - "Gentle options without planned crying include moving bedtime later for a while and then earlier step by step, or sitting by your child's bed and moving your chair a little further away every few nights. These work well when your child sleeps in your room."
  - "Some parents choose to check and comfort at gradually longer gaps. Studies that followed children to age 6 found no lasting harm to emotions, behaviour, stress or the parent–child bond, and no lasting benefit either. It's a choice, not a requirement."
  - "These methods aren't for babies under 6 months, who still need night feeds and quick responses. Pause any plan if your child is unwell."
  - "Snoring with pauses in breathing, constant night waking with pain or itch, or poor weight gain are reasons to see a doctor rather than try a sleep method."

### 10.2 Claims the app must NOT make

- ❌ "Jaundice is normal, wait and see". Not without the first-24-hours and stool/urine checks attached.
- ❌ "Put your baby in the sun to treat jaundice." AAP and CPS say it is not safe.
- ❌ "ترنجبین / شیرخشت / خاکشیر cure or prevent jaundice", **or** "they are poisonous" or "they cause haemolysis in G6PD". There is no opened evidence for either.
- ❌ "Give water or sugar water to flush out jaundice." AAP 2022 says no.
- ❌ "A normal G6PD screen means your baby doesn't have it." The spot test misses cases, especially girls.
- ❌ Any list of "safe medicines" for G6PD, or any dose.
- ❌ "Swaddling prevents SIDS" (AAP says it doesn't). Also never "you must swaddle" or "never swaddle".
- ❌ "Traditional قنداق causes hip dislocation" as a blanket statement. Say: "tight wrapping **with legs held straight** raises the risk".
- ❌ "Dummies prevent SIDS", stated as cause and effect. Say "may lower the risk". The mechanism is unknown (Lullaby Trust).
- ❌ "Dummies ruin breastfeeding." The Cochrane review found no effect in healthy term babies.
- ❌ "Orthodontic dummies protect teeth." AAPD says the evidence is insufficient.
- ❌ "Teething causes fever or diarrhoea."
- ❌ "Amber necklaces release pain-relieving succinic acid." This is unproven, and the necklaces are dangerous.
- ❌ "Sleep training is harmful or damages attachment." The RCT evidence doesn't show this.
- ❌ "Sleep training is necessary or best", or "babies must learn to self-soothe early".
- ❌ "Sleep train from 3–4 months." Say not before 6 months.
- ❌ "Gripe water, star anise, mint or borage teas are safe for colic." No evidence, and there are documented harms for star anise.
- ❌ "All kohl will poison your baby." Say it "often contains lead" and "is best avoided".
- ❌ Any ruling on religious practice (tahnik). Offer a safety note and leave the rest to the family.
- ❌ Anything implying a parent caused jaundice, DDH or SIDS by following tradition. **No guilt.**

### 10.3 Red flags that must send parents to a professional

**Emergency or same day:**
- Yellow skin **within 24 hours of birth**
- Yellow **palms or soles**, or yellow arms and legs
- A yellow baby who is **very sleepy, floppy or stiff, not feeding**, or has no wet nappies
- **Pale or chalky poo**, or **dark yellow or brown pee**
- Not feeding
- A fit
- Fast breathing or chest in-drawing
- No movement or very floppy
- Temperature of **38°C or more**, or **feeling cold**
- Weight loss of more than 10%
- Cord: **active bleeding**, a red, swollen or smelly base, or crying when touched
- G6PD child: **sudden pallor, yellowing, or dark cola-coloured urine**, extreme tiredness, fast breathing
- **Constipation with weak cry, poor sucking, drooping eyelids or floppiness** (possible infant botulism, especially after honey)
- Choking or strangulation incident with teething jewellery or a dummy cord
- A swaddled baby found face-down and unresponsive → **emergency (115 in Iran; verify)**

**Book a check:**
- Jaundice still present at **2 weeks** (or 3 weeks if born early)
- The cord still attached after about 3 weeks
- **Signs of hip problems:** uneven leg length or thigh creases, one leg moving less, a click with limited movement, or later a limp. Also: family history, breech, or a history of tight straight-leg swaddling.
- Kohl or surma used regularly → ask about a **blood lead test** (FDA advice)
- Sleep: **loud snoring with pauses**, laboured breathing, poor growth, or night pain or itch
- Dummy or thumb habit continuing strongly past 3–4 years or affecting the teeth → dentist
