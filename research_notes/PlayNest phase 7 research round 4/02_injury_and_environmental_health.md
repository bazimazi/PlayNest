# 02 — Injury prevention and environmental health ("Safety" and "Health and body" shelves)

Research notes for PlayNest phase 7, round 4. Researched 17 Nov 2026.
House rules for this shelf: teach, don't diagnose. No medicine doses or dosing amounts. Practical, non-judgemental, never guilt-tripping: most of these risks are about homes, roads and products, not "bad parents". Where bodies disagree, say so. Never state a law we have not verified: say "check local law".

Scope: deeper material for gaps the app does not cover yet. The app already has "Home safety by stage" (tip-over, stair gates, button batteries, magnets, blind cords, pools, trampolines, helmets, a one-line car-seat mention), "Safe sleep essentials", "Safe play checklist", first-aid cards (CPR, choking, burns/scalds, poison, battery, drowning, heatstroke/hot car, head injury and others) and a hard-moment card on refusing the car seat. None of that is repeated here except where a new guide must link to it.

## 1. Child passenger safety

### 1.1 Why it matters (global)
- **WHO:** road traffic injuries are the **leading cause of death for children and young people aged 5–29**. About 1.16 million road deaths a year. "Using child restraints can lead to a **71% reduction in deaths among infants**." [opened] WHO fact sheet "Road traffic injuries", 20 Jul 2026.
- **WHO Global status report 2015 figures (via GRSP fact sheet):** child restraints reduce the likelihood of death in a crash by **about 70% among infants** and **54–80% among young children**. Rear-facing restraints for babies under 1 have been shown to reduce death or injury by **90%** compared with no restraint. Forward-facing child restraints reduce serious injury by **almost 80%** compared with seat belts alone. Children aged roughly 4–10 in boosters have a **77%** lower injury risk than unrestrained children. Child restraints are **used incorrectly in 15–80% of cases**. Cost is a barrier in low- and middle-income countries; subsidy and loan schemes raise use. [opened] GRSP "Child Restraints Fact Sheet", Dec 2015 (cites WHO GSRRS 2015).
- WHO materials based on the 2018 report say restraints cut the risk of death by "at least 60%". [search] Use **"about 70% for babies"** in copy; it is the most consistently stated figure.

### 1.2 Stages compared across bodies

| Stage | AAP / HealthyChildren (US) | UK (gov.uk) | CPS (Canada) | Australia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rear-facing | From the first ride home. Keep rear-facing **until the seat's highest rear-facing weight or height limit**. Most convertible seats allow **2 years or more**. [opened, updated 16 Apr 2026] | Height-based (R129, "i-Size") seats must stay **rear-facing until the child is over 15 months**. [opened, gov.uk, undated] | Until the seat's limits, which "may be until **2, 3, or even 4** years old". Always use the top tether. | Allowed after 15 months in R129 seats; weight-group seats (R44) are still described. | Only after **at least age 2** and outgrowing rear-facing; keep until **at least 18 kg** and the child can sit properly (often 4–6). | Required to about age 4. [recall, unverified] |

[recall, unverified] |

- **The UK 12 years / 135 cm is a legal minimum, not best practice.** AAP and CPS use belt fit at about **145 cm**. The app should teach the **5-step belt-fit test** idea from CPS: lap belt low on the hips, shoulder belt across the chest (not the neck), knees bending at the seat edge, able to sit like that for the whole trip. [opened, CPS]
- **ISOFIX:** gov.uk says a child seat can only be used with a diagonal-strap belt unless designed for a lap belt or **fitted using ISOFIX anchor points**. [opened] Wider claims (ISOFIX reduces misuse) are [recall, unverified].
- **Lap-holding:** no source I opened says it in those words, but every body requires a restraint for every child. The physics argument (an adult cannot hold a child in a crash) is [recall, unverified]; the Iranian 2026 study below found lap-holding is a common cultural practice, so the app needs this message in gentle words.
- NHTSA blocked (403); its age chart is [recall, unverified] but matches AAP.

### 1.3 Iran
- **Very low car-seat use, measured several ways:**
  - **Tehran, roadside observation (2015):** 2,178 private cars carrying a child under 12. Child-seat use **4.3%**. Higher with women drivers and on freeways. Moradi et al., *Traffic Inj Prev* 2017. [abstract]
  - **Tehran, parent survey:** **18.7%** (self-report; not comparable with observation). Moradi et al., 2019. [abstract]
  - **Tabriz (2019):** child-seat use **15.1%**, **booster use 0.6%**. Harzand-Jadidi et al., *BMC Pediatr* 2023. [abstract]
  - **Gorgan:** **13%** always use, **80%** never. Tavakoli et al., *Traffic Inj Prev* 2019. [abstract]
  - **National STEPS survey 2021 (27,874 people, 31 provinces):** seat-belt use 90% for drivers and front passengers, but **child-seat use 9.4%**. Shahmohamadi et al., *Heliyon* 2023. [abstract]
  - **Tabriz, 750 parents of 1–3-year-olds (2026):** 92% owned a car but only **47% owned a child seat**; **55%** rarely or never used one. Use was **45% on city trips vs 82% on intercity trips**. Barriers: **cost (62%)**, lack of awareness (55%), and **cultural practices such as holding children on laps (50%)**. Only 62% knew how to install correctly. Nasirabadi et al., *PLoS One* 2026. [abstract]
- **"Only on long trips" is a key Iranian pattern** (45% vs 82%). Most crashes and injuries happen close to home is a common message in Western materials [recall, unverified]; for Iranian copy, simply say "every trip, even short ones".
- **Road traffic as a cause of child death:** in southern Iran 2004–2019 (6,590 child deaths from unintentional injury, ages 0–19), **transport injuries caused 67% of years of life lost**, then drowning (7%) and burns (7%). Rates roughly halved over the period. Azarbakhsh et al., *BMJ Paediatr Open* 2023. [abstract]
- Traffic Police chief (May 2019): about 46 road deaths a day, **about 7 of them children aged 10 and under**; children ≤10 are "13%" of road casualties. [opened] Tehran Times, 22 May 2019. Treat as an official claim, not data.
- **Law:** In Sep 2018 the traffic police said a child-seat rule for children **under 10** was planned but "yet to be implemented; for now we just recommend parents use car seats". [opened] Tehran Times, 15 Sep 2018. In May 2019 the bill was "awaiting final approval" in the Majlis. [opened] Financial Tribune reported proposed rules (under 12 not in the front; under 8 in a size-appropriate seat; a fine similar to the seat-belt fine). [search] **I could not confirm that any child-seat law is in force in Iran in 2026. App copy must say "check local law" and not name ages or fines.**
- **Children on motorcycles:** Iranian studies show head injury dominates child motorcycle injuries and helmet use is very low: Darab (212 injured children/adolescents) — **90% of injured riders had no helmet**, head the most injured region. [search] An Iranian all-age study (2020–22) found head injuries more common in **passengers (13%) than riders (5.7%)**, linked to passengers' lower helmet use. [search] **No verified Iranian minimum age** for child pillion passengers. Mexico City banned under-12s as motorcycle passengers in 2023, and WHO country profiles track minimum-age rules, but I found **no explicit WHO age recommendation**. [search]

## 2. Child pedestrians and bikes

### 2.1 When can a child cross alone?

| Source | Position | Status |
|---|---|---|
| AAP / HealthyChildren | "Most children are not ready to cross the street alone until they are **at least 10 years old**." Children with disabilities may need longer. Hold your child's hand at crossings. Make eye contact with drivers. No phones or earbuds when crossing. Young children shouldn't play on driveways; drivers backing up can't see them. | [opened] "Pedestrian Safety Tips for Families", updated 2 Mar 2026 |
| AAP policy 2023 | Adults should accompany most children under 10. | [search] |
| RoSPA (UK) | **No fixed age.** Depends on the journey and the child. Young children should **never** be near a road alone, **even with an older child**. **Holding hands is the most important rule**, every time; reins or a wrist strap can help; child can hold the bag or pushchair if your hands are full. Teach "Stop, Look, Listen, Think". Be seen: fluorescent by day, reflective in the dark. | [opened] RoSPA child pedestrian hub, © 2026 (no date) |
| Wann, Poulter & Purcell 2011 (*Psychological Science*) | Primary-school children could not reliably judge approaching vehicles above about **20 mph (≈32 km/h)**: faster cars "loom" less, so a speeding car can look like it isn't coming. A **perceptual** limit, not inattention. | [search] |
| O'Neal, Plumert et al. 2017 (*J Exp Psychol Hum Percept Perform*), virtual-reality crossings | Children aged 6–12 chose gaps like adults but timed their entry into traffic worse; **only by 14** did children cross without incident; 6-year-olds had collision rates as high as about 8%. | [search, press coverage] |

- **Disagreement:** AAP gives "at least 10"; RoSPA refuses a fixed age; lab research suggests skills keep maturing to about **14** in busy, fast traffic. **PlayNest wording:** "Most children aren't ready to cross busy roads alone until about 10, and many need longer. Speed is the hard part: children can't judge fast traffic well until their early teens."
- Iranian context: younger children in Tehran trauma data were more prone to pedestrian injuries, teenagers to motorcycle injuries (Zargar et al. 2003). [search]

### 2.2 Bikes
- Cochrane review (Thompson, Rivara, Thompson; 1999, conclusions unchanged in 2010): helmets reduce the risk of head, brain and severe brain injury by **63–88%**, with similar protection in crashes with and without motor vehicles. Based on five case-control studies; no trials. [search] Helmets are already in the app; only add a cross-link.

## 3. Scalds and burns at home (Iran focus)

### 3.1 Iranian burn data
- **Tehran, Motahari burn centre, 2005–2014, 1,717 children:** mean age **4.1 years**; **2–4-year-olds were 45.7%** and ≤1 year 21.8%. **Hot liquids caused 73.2%** (scald/boiling water 54.7%, other hot liquid 17.9%), hot food 12.6%, petrol 6.6%, flame 5.2%, electricity 3.2%. Boys 63%. Deaths 4.5%, mostly burns over 20% of the body. Kazemzadeh et al., *Iran J Public Health* 2018. [abstract, full text via Europe PMC]
- **Shiraz, 2008–2017, 1,893 inpatients and 12,431 outpatients under 18:** scalds **49%** overall and **74% of inpatients aged 0–4**; flame burns rise with age (61–66% at 10–18). More than half of inpatients were rural. Recommends media campaigns and school education, with special attention to **under-5s**. Keshavarz et al., *World J Plast Surg* 2020. [abstract, full text via Europe PMC]
- **Rasht (2013–2018):** children aged 1–5 were 56% of burns; scalds 76%. [search]
- **Samovar and kettle:** in 265 rural households (Meshkinshahr, Ardabil, Iran–Sweden project), the samovar was the main water boiler in **75%**; it was **within reach of preschool children in about 60%**, in walkways in at least 21%, on an uneven floor in 15%; most had technical faults. [search] A north-east Iran burn-centre series found the most common hot-liquid containers were the **kettle (42%) and samovar (16%)**. [search] Another Iranian unit: hot water such as tea 80% of sources. [search]
- **Kermanshah case-control (198 children each):** larger households, **storing petrol at home**, and the lowest income quartile (OR 7.2) were linked to burns. [search]
- **Cooking at floor level** (sofreh, low gas burners): plausible and widely described, but I found **no Iranian study measuring it as a risk factor**. WHO lists "open cooking fires and unstable stoves" as risks. [opened] Use only as a practical tip, not a statistic.

### 3.2 Prevention (all bodies agree)
- WHO burns fact sheet: risks include **hot drinks, cooking liquids and excessively hot tap water**, unstable stoves, unsafe gas; prevention includes **safer water temperatures and safeguards for young children**. [opened] WHO, 21 Oct 2026. Children under 15 accounted for about 35,600 deaths from fire, heat and hot substances in 2023 (about 22% of the total). [opened]
- Hot drinks can still scald **about 15 minutes after being made**, even with milk added. [search: HSE Ireland and school handouts; CAPT page not reachable]
- Bath water: **37–38 °C** for children (about 36 °C for newborns per HSE Ireland); mix well; don't bathe a child with the tap running. [search: RCN, HSE Ireland]
- Water heaters: setting stored hot water to about **49 °C (120 °F)** is the US advice; UK new builds use mixing valves (about 46 °C at the bath). [recall, unverified]. In Iran many homes use gas water heaters (آبگرمکن) with a manual thermostat; a cautious line: "If you can, turn your water heater down so tap water can't scald."

### 3.3 First aid and Iranian home remedies
- **NHS:** cool running water for **20 minutes**, as soon as possible and **within 3 hours**; remove clothing and jewellery unless stuck; cover loosely with cling film; **no creams, oils or butter**; don't burst blisters. Under 5: call for advice. [opened] reviewed 31 Mar 2026.
- **WHO:** cool with running water about 20 minutes; **no ice, no oils, butter, toothpaste, traditional pastes or other household substances**; keep the rest of the body warm (babies get cold quickly). [opened]
- **Iran:** in a Guilan burn-centre survey most participants had limited first-aid knowledge; substances applied included **ice, herbal medicines, oil, honey, vinegar, flour, toothpaste and eggs**. [search] An Ardabil focus-group study found strong belief in traditional remedies, especially **pennyroyal (پونه)** and **grated potato**. [search] Yogurt and toothpaste were common in a Turkish mothers' study; only water was linked to better healing. [search]
- The app's existing burns card covers the 20-minute rule. The new piece should add the **"what not to put on"** list with Iranian items named.

## 4. Carbon monoxide (گازگرفتگی)

### 4.1 What the bodies say

| Point | NHS (UK) | CDC (US) |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms | Headache, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, weakness, tiredness, confusion, breathlessness. **Symptoms may ease when you leave and return when you come back.** No smell, no colour. | "Flu-like": headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion. **Sleeping people can die before they have symptoms.** |
| Who is most at risk | Not stated | **Infants**, older adults, people with heart or breathing problems or anaemia. |
| Alarms | A CO alarm in **each room with a gas, oil, coal or wood appliance**. | Battery or battery-backup detectors **near every sleeping area**; replace per maker or about every 5 years. |
| Appliances | Professional installation and regular servicing; ventilate; keep chimneys and flues clear; no BBQs or camping stoves indoors. | Service heaters, **water heaters** and fuel appliances **every year**; vent gas appliances; **never heat the home with a gas oven**; never burn charcoal indoors; no camp stoves indoors. |
| If suspected | Turn appliances off if safe, **get outside**, get medical advice; emergency if breathing trouble, confusion or collapse. | (Not on this page.) |
| Status | [opened] reviewed 16 Dec 2025 | [opened] updated 26 Oct 2026 |

- AAP earthquake page adds: never use fuel-burning equipment indoors after a disaster; use a battery CO detector. [opened]

### 4.2 Iran
- **Legal Medicine Organization data, 2011–2018:** **6,078 CO deaths** (74% male). Rate fell from **1.26 to 0.91 per 100,000**. Highest in northern and western provinces (North Khorasan, Semnan, Qom, Qazvin, Zanjan, Chaharmahal-Bakhtiari, Tehran). Authors name **gas heaters** as key sources and recommend education on safe heater installation, appliance maintenance, **CO alarms**, and regulation. No age breakdown. Alimohamadi et al., *Health Sci Rep* 2024. [abstract + full text via Europe PMC]
- ILMO reported **over 700 CO deaths in 2017**. [search] Latest national figures: **not found** (search budget ran out).
- **Sources in a sample of 156 deaths from ILMO records:** gas heater 34%, **picnic gas cylinder (گاز پیک‌نیکی) 21.2%**, water heater 11.5%. [search] **Children in Ardabil (2008–2013):** water heater **48.6%**, gas heater **31.4%**. [search] In Qazvin's children's hospital (2009–2012) CO was **the deadliest poisoning agent**. [search] Tehran studies say child CO poisoning is **often misdiagnosed** because headache, nausea and dizziness look like other illness. [search]
- **Charcoal braziers (منقل) and korsi (کرسی):** I found **no paper isolating them**. They are well-known traditional CO sources [recall, unverified]. Hookah charcoal is also a CO source (see §5). Include them in the checklist as "anything that burns charcoal indoors".
- **CO alarm availability and price in Iran:** unknown. Copy should recommend an alarm "if you can get one" and never make safety depend on it.

## 5. Second-hand and third-hand smoke, waterpipe (قلیان), vapes

### 5.1 Evidence
- **CDC:** no safe level of second-hand smoke. In infants and children it raises the risk of **SIDS**, **pneumonia and bronchitis**, **ear infections** (more fluid and more ear-tube operations), more frequent and severe **asthma attacks**, wheeze, cough, and slower lung growth. [opened] updated 31 Jan 2025.
- **WHO tobacco fact sheet:** "There is no safe level of exposure to second-hand tobacco smoke"; more than **1.6 million** premature deaths a year from second-hand smoke; e-cigarettes are "harmful to health and not safe". [opened] 26 Jun 2026.
- **Open windows don't fix it (NHS):** smoke hangs in the air for hours even with a window open; smoking in another room doesn't protect children; cars reach hazardous levels even with windows down; smoking in a car with anyone under 18 is illegal in England. [search: NHS "Passive smoking" page and NHS trust leaflets]
- **Third-hand smoke (AAP fact sheet):** residue sticks to dust, furniture, carpets, **car seats, hair and clothes**; babies are exposed when they crawl, sit in cars or **are held by adults**; smoking in another room, using fans or smoking at an open window **doesn't prevent it**; e-cigarette aerosol also contains chemicals, so no vaping in the home, car or near children; the only complete protection is quitting. [opened, AAP Richmond Center PDF, undated]
- Children's homes in Cincinnati: nicotine residue on surfaces in **every home tested**, including some with smoking bans. [search]

### 5.2 Waterpipe (قلیان)
- **WHO (EMRO):** a typical **1-hour session = inhaling 100–200 times the smoke volume of one cigarette**; waterpipe smoke is a mix of tobacco smoke and **charcoal** smoke and "poses a serious risk for those inhaling it, **especially children**". [search; emro.who.int returned 502]
- Charcoal adds carbon monoxide: estimates per session range from about 6.5× to 30× a cigarette's CO depending on method. [search] A manufacturer-linked analysis disputes the volume comparison. [search] **Use "much more smoke than a cigarette" rather than a precise multiple.**
- WHO TobReg Advisory Note (2nd ed., 2015) has a section on second-hand waterpipe smoke and recommends banning indoor waterpipe smoking in public places. [search]
- The belief that "the water filters it" is common [recall, unverified]; WHO materials reject it [search].
- **Iran, CASPIAN-IV (2011–12, 13,486 students aged 6–18, 30 provinces):** **43.9%** exposed to second-hand smoke at home; **hookah 21.5%**, cigarettes 34.5%. Higher parental education → less exposure. Authors call for smoke-free homes and family counselling. Kelishadi et al., *Iran J Pediatr* 2016. [opened]
- Ardabil primary-school children: 7.7% home waterpipe smoke, 19.8% cigarette. [search]

### 5.3 Vapes and nicotine liquid
- US poison-centre calls about e-cigarette exposures in children under 6 rose from **14 to 223 a month** (Jan 2012 – Apr 2015); most were **swallowing liquid nicotine**; children exposed to e-cigarettes were about **5× more likely to be admitted** than those exposed to cigarettes; one death. *Pediatrics* 2016 (Smith and colleagues, Nationwide Children's; first author Kamboj [recall, unverified]). [search, news coverage] Skin contact can also poison. [search]
- Message: treat refills and pods like medicines — **closed, locked, out of reach**. [search, AAP quote]

## 6. Air pollution and dust days

### 6.1 Why children
- **WHO:** air pollution is "one of the greatest environmental risks to child health" (fact sheet 24 Oct 2024) [opened]; in children it can mean **reduced lung growth and function, respiratory infections and worse asthma** (WHO AQG news release, 22 Sep 2021) [opened]. 2021 AQG values: PM2.5 **5 µg/m³ annual, 15 µg/m³ 24-hour**. [recall, unverified: numbers not in the pages I opened]
- WHO 2018 "Air pollution and child health": **93%** of the world's children live with air above WHO guideline levels; **543,000** deaths in under-5s in 2016 from respiratory infections linked to air pollution (press used "600,000 children"). [search]

### 6.2 What to do on bad-air days

| Body | Advice | Status |
|---|---|---|
| AirNow (US EPA) | AQI bands: 0–50 Good (green); 51–100 Moderate (yellow); **101–150 Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups (orange)**; 151–200 Unhealthy (red); 201–300 Very Unhealthy (purple); 301+ Hazardous (maroon). | [opened, undated] |
| AirNow school guidance | Children, including teens, are more sensitive than adults; check daily AQI; keep kids active but **cut intensity and duration**, move activity indoors when needed. | [opened intro, Aug 2014]; detailed table [search] |
| State guides built on AirNow | At very unhealthy levels children avoid all outdoor exercise; at hazardous, stay indoors. Children with asthma: limit further. | [search, NY / WA] |
| Health Canada (wildfire smoke) | **No respirator masks under 2**, or for anyone who can't remove it themselves or has trouble breathing. Masks don't remove gases. **Reducing exposure (indoor, cleaner air) is the main protection.** | [opened] May 2024, updated Oct 2026 |
| PEHSU (US paediatric environmental health units) | From age 2, a snug small N95 or surgical mask may help if it fits; fit is hard under about 7. Cloth masks don't filter fine particles. | [search] |
| EPA air cleaners | Portable HEPA cleaners cut indoor PM2.5 by roughly **29–82%** in studies; health benefits are **mixed** and EPA says they may not reduce health effects in sensitive groups. One trial in children with asthma showed improved outcomes. | [search] |

- **Disagreement on masks:** Health Canada and several US states: none under 2, a well-fitted one may help older children. A vendor blog claims the EPA children's page now says not to rely on masks at all [search, unverified]. **PlayNest wording:** "Masks are not the main protection for children. Never put a mask on a child under 2. For older children, only a well-fitting mask helps, and staying indoors with cleaner air matters more."

### 6.3 Iran
- Tehran school closures follow AQI around **150+** (e.g., 156 in 2016, 158 in Nov 2019, 153 in a later December). In Nov 2025, schools stayed open but **school sports were suspended**; closures are decided by an **Air Pollution Emergency Working Group** when sensitive groups are threatened and no wind or rain is forecast. [search, news] Iran's official index appears to follow the US 0–500 scale [recall, unverified].
- **Ahvaz dust storms:** during storms PM10 reached 10× the 150 µg/m³ standard; schoolchildren had more respiratory symptoms and absences, and at least a third saw a doctor after each storm. Respiratory admissions (all ages) rise on dusty days. Ahvaz asthma prevalence about twice the national average. [search]
- Iranian Red Crescent home page carries a "staying healthy in polluted air" article and a CO-from-heaters article. [opened rcs.ir, content not read]

## 7. Medicines safety at home (no doses)

### 7.1 Accidental ingestion and storage
- **Up and Away (CDC partnership):** about **35,000 young children** go to US emergency rooms each year after getting into medicines left within reach. Put medicines (including **vitamins and gummies**) up, away and out of sight **after every use**; twist caps until they click; many pill organisers aren't child-resistant; ask guests to keep bags up high; **never call medicine sweets**. [opened, © 2026]
- **CDC:** children finding and taking medicines unsupervised is the **main cause** of medicine-related emergency visits in children 5 and under. [opened] 17 Apr 2024.

### 7.2 Measuring
- **AAP:** use only the syringe, dropper or cup that comes with the medicine; **never kitchen spoons**; prefer **mL**; watch the decimal point (0.5 mL vs 5 mL is a tenfold error). [opened] updated 21 Feb 2024.
- **Yin et al. 2014 (*Pediatrics*):** about **40%** of parents made dosing errors; teaspoon/tablespoon labels pushed parents towards kitchen spoons. [search]
- **Yin et al. 2016 (*Pediatrics*, randomised):** **84%** of parents made at least one error and 21% a large error; errors were **over 4× more likely with cups than oral syringes**; mL-only labels and tools cut errors. [search]

### 7.3 Cough and cold medicines (bodies disagree on age)

| Body | Position | Status |
|---|---|---|
| FDA (US) | No OTC cough/cold products with decongestants or antihistamines **under 2**. Makers relabelled to "do not use **under 4**". | [opened] content of 8 Feb 2018 |
| MHRA (UK) | OTC cough and cold medicines **should not be used under 6**; age 6–12 only from pharmacies with advice. Covers named cough suppressants, expectorants, decongestants and antihistamines. | [opened] gov.uk, 2014 (from 2009 update) |
| NHS | Some cough medicines aren't for under-12s; **honey not under 1**; no hot drinks for small children. | [opened] reviewed 8 Dec 2023 |
| NICE NG120 | Honey is a reasonable self-care option for acute cough (low-quality evidence), **not under 1** (botulism). | [search] |
| WHO | Honey as a soothing remedy for cough. | [recall, unverified] |
| Aspirin | **Never under 16** (NHS), Reye's syndrome (CPS). | [opened in round 3 note 03] |

**PlayNest wording:** "Cough and cold medicines aren't recommended for young children. The age limit is 2, 4 or 6 depending on the country. Check with a pharmacist before giving any, and check every label for the same active ingredient twice."

### 7.4 Iran: self-medication and opioid poisoning
- Self-medication with antibiotics for children is common in Iranian local studies (Arak children 0–6 "remarkable prevalence"; Arak mothers of under-2s **54%** self-medicating; Tehran ~half of parents who skipped the doctor for a cold self-medicated). [search] A global review found about **24%** of parents had self-medicated a child with antibiotics, higher in low- and middle-income settings. [search]
- **Opioid poisoning in Iranian children (handle with great care):**
  - Gorgan/Tehran, **214 poisoned children: illicit drugs were 45%**; of these, **methadone 44% and opium 41%**; 3 methadone and 2 opium deaths. Barati et al., *Sci Rep* 2025. [abstract]
  - Tehran, 82 poisoned children under 12: **methadone 49%**. Rezazadeh et al., 2025. [abstract]
  - Kerman, 5,826 methadone poisonings 2013–23: **17.7% under 6**, mainly **accidental ingestion linked to unsafe storage**. Soltani et al., 2026. [abstract]
  - Urmia, 45 children with opioid poisoning: methadone 40%, **3 deaths (6.7%)**. Razmi et al., 2025. [abstract]
  - Loghman-Hakim (Tehran): the main cause of methadone poisoning was **methadone stored in water or drink bottles**; syrup forms caused the two deaths in 2010–12; symptoms can return after treatment, so children need hospital observation. [search]
  - **Framing:** methadone and other opioid treatment is a healthy step for a parent and must not be shamed. The message is storage: **"original child-resistant bottle, locked away, never in a drink bottle or cup, never taken in front of children."** A very small amount can kill a small child [recall, unverified wording; supported by the death data above].
- **Iranian poison line:** **1490** is reported as the national Drug and Poison Information Centre number (2012 news, 2015 paper). [search] **The round-3 note listed "190"; 190 appears to be a different Food and Drug Organization line [recall, unverified]. Resolve before shipping.** Emergency ambulance: **115**.

## 8. Family emergency preparedness: earthquakes

### 8.1 During shaking
- **Earthquake Country Alliance:** **Drop** (before the quake drops you), **Cover** (under a sturdy table, or by an interior wall, arms over head and neck), **Hold On**. **Don't run outside or to other rooms** during shaking; **don't stand in a doorway**; reject the "triangle of life". **In bed: stay there, pillow over head.** [opened, © 2026]
- **AAP earthquake page:** same; also stay away from windows, outer walls and hanging objects; no lifts; outdoors, keep away from buildings, wires and trees; in a car, pull over and stay buckled. [opened] updated 10 Nov 2025.
- **Disagreement / local caution:** ECA's advice is written for engineered US buildings. Some Iranian homes are older unreinforced masonry or adobe (Bam 2003). Whether to get out on the ground floor if an exit is seconds away is debated in some low-income settings [recall, unverified]. **Have an Iranian disaster expert (IIEES / Red Crescent) review the wording.** The Iranian school drill teaches sheltering and evacuation [search], but I could not read its exact steps.

### 8.2 Before: plan and kit
- **AAP kit:** at least 3 days of food and water, medicines, documents, first-aid kit, torch, radio, whistle, batteries; fix shelves and heavy items; know how to shut off water, gas and electricity; practise drop, cover, hold as a family; know the school's plan. [opened]
- **AAP child go-bag:** prescription medicines (ask the paediatrician about a 2-week supply), a **written medicines list**, allergy list, emergency information form, doctor's numbers, **recent photos of each child and one of the whole family**, nappies/wipes, comfort items; review **every 6 months**. [opened] updated 14 Jan 2025.
- **AAP reunification tips:** a backpack emergency card in the school bag; teach names, parents' numbers and an out-of-area contact; a **family password**; phone number on clothing or a bracelet in crowds; a meeting spot; photo of the child on your phone. [opened] updated 19 Sep 2022.
- Let children choose some items (a book, a toy, a snack). [search, state Ready.gov material]

### 8.3 After: helping children cope
- **NCTSN "Parent Guidelines for Helping Children after an Earthquake":** common reactions include fear of separation, clinginess, **fear of aftershocks**, anxiety about buildings, physical complaints, sleep and appetite changes, **"playing" the earthquake**, strong reactions to reminders and sounds, regression (baby talk, bedwetting, tantrums). Help: talk and let them ask (repeatedly); answer **briefly and honestly**; follow with a favourite story for young children; stay calm as a role model; **tell them what to do in an aftershock**; keep routines and family rules; **limit news**; watch what adults say in front of them; let them help; extra time at bedtime; involve teachers; stay hopeful. Seek help if difficulties continue **for a couple of months** — "a doctor or traditional healer, religious or community leader, even a counselor". Parents: water, food, sleep, lean on others, put off big decisions. [opened PDF, 2007]
- **AAP:** don't avoid the topic; don't promise things will be exactly as before or that no worse quake will come; allow a temporary different sleeping place; let them help others. [opened]
- **WHO Psychological First Aid (2011):** Prepare, then **Look, Listen, Link**; look out for separated children. [search]

### 8.4 Iran
- **National Earthquake and Safety Drill** in schools every **late November** (26th drill on 27 Nov 2024, slogan "Safe School, Resilient Community"); run by IIEES, the Ministry of Education, the National Disaster Management Organization, the **Iranian Red Crescent**, IRIB and Tehran Municipality; sirens on national radio; schools and neighbourhoods drill together; 2024 was the first year with special attention to children with mobility limits. [opened] IIEES. The drill began decades ago and reached ~13–14.5 million students [search]. **Timing idea:** surface the preparedness guide in November around the drill.
- Bam 2003: all 131 schools in the area destroyed or badly damaged; ~4,000 children lost one or both parents (UNICEF evaluation); death-toll estimates vary (26,000–43,000). [search]
- rcs.ir home page had **no public earthquake guidance** that I could find (links to first-aid training at learn.rcs.ir). [opened]

## 9. Falls from windows and balconies

- **England, NCMD (Apr 2019 – May 2025):** **16 children under 11** died after falling from a window or balcony; 44% were under 5; 44% from the most deprived areas. In **92% of falls from flats, window or balcony locks/restrictors were missing, broken or not in use**. Overcrowding sometimes left nowhere safe for furniture. CAPT advice: keep low furniture away from windows and balconies **even on the ground floor**, fit catches and locks, keep keys handy for fire, lock balcony doors. [opened] University of Bristol release, 13 Aug 2025.
- **RoSPA:** a lock or **restrictor on all upstairs windows** (opens enough for air, not for a child); furniture away from windows, especially in bedrooms ("amazing what toddlers can use as a ladder"). [opened, undated] UK HSE uses **100 mm** as the restricted opening. [search]
- **US:** window stops to **4 inches (10 cm) or less**; guards with bars ≤4 in apart (ASTM F2090), quick-release for fire escape; **screens keep bugs in, not children**. [search: Massachusetts, CPSC] Balcony rails more than 10 cm apart and low windows were risk factors in a 2003 *Injury Prevention* study. [search]
- **New York "Children Can't Fly" (from 1972):** education plus free window guards; Health Code required guards where children ≤10 live (1976); reported falls fell from **217 in 1976 to 9 in 2016**, deaths from 24 to 2. Observational, other changes happened too. [search]
- **Iran:** at a Tehran children's trauma centre (2018, 564 injured children), **falls from height were 57.8%**, the most common cause. [search] Ahvaz (1999 data): 77.5% of child falls happened at or near home. [search] National STEPS 2021: falls were 44% of all injuries (all ages). [abstract] No Iranian data on windows vs balconies vs roofs were found. Apartment living, flat roofs (پشت‌بام) and balconies used for drying clothes or summer sleeping are worth naming [recall, unverified].

## 10. Disagreements (summary for writers)

1. **Rear-facing:** AAP and CPS: as long as the seat allows (often 2–4 years). UK R129: at least 15 months. Australia: legal minimum 6 months [unverified].
2. **When the belt alone is OK:** UK law 12 years or 135 cm; AAP/CPS about 145 cm and the belt-fit test (often 10–12).
3. **Crossing alone:** AAP at least 10; RoSPA no fixed age; research suggests skills mature to about 14 in fast traffic.
4. **Cough/cold medicines:** FDA under 2 (labels under 4); MHRA under 6.
5. **Masks:** none under 2 everywhere; beyond that, from "may help if well-fitted" to "don't rely on them".
6. **HEPA cleaners:** reduce particles clearly; health benefit for children mixed.
7. **Earthquakes:** Drop-Cover-Hold-On vs. local debate about unreinforced buildings (needs Iranian expert).
8. **Waterpipe numbers:** "100–200 cigarettes' worth of smoke" is WHO's figure but contested; use qualitative wording.
9. **Window openings:** UK 100 mm vs US 4 in (≈10 cm): effectively the same.

## App takeaways

### A. Proposed guides

**Guide 1 — "Every trip, every child: car seats by stage" (0–12)**
- Sections: Why it matters (one line: "A correctly used car seat cuts a baby's risk of dying in a crash by about 70%"). Stage ladder (rear-facing → harness → booster → belt) with "move up only when the child outgrows the seat's limits, not on a birthday". Belt-fit test (5 checks). Back seat until about 13. Airbags. Coats and blankets. Second-hand seats. Short trips count. "Holding on a lap isn't safe" (gentle). Taxis and family cars (practical ideas). Motorcycles (helmet that fits; check local law on age). Link to the hard-moment card on refusing the car seat.
- Safe wording:
  - "Keep your baby rear-facing for as long as the seat allows. Many seats allow this until 2 years or more. Guidance differs by country; UK rules say at least 15 months."
  - "Most children need a booster until they are about 145 cm tall, often between 10 and 12."
  - "The back seat is safest for all children under about 13. Never put a rear-facing seat in front of an airbag."
  - "Even a loving grip can't hold a child in a crash. A child on a lap can be thrown forward or crushed. A seat or belt for every child, every trip, even short ones."
  - "Car seat laws differ by country and change over time. Check local law."
  - Cost-sensitive line: "If a new seat isn't possible right now, a seat from someone you trust, with known history, that's not expired or crash-damaged, is better than none."

**Guide 2 — "Walking and crossing roads" (2–12)**
- Sections: Holding hands (toddlers to about 8). Stop–Look–Listen–Think. Why children can't judge speed. Being seen. Driveways and parking. Phones down. When they're ready to go alone (a checklist, not an age).
- Safe wording: "Most children aren't ready to cross roads alone until at least 10, and fast or busy roads are hard until the early teens. Readiness depends on the child and the road." / "Young children shouldn't be near a road without an adult, even with an older brother or sister."

**Guide 3 — "Hot drinks, samovars and stoves: preventing scalds" (0–5, most important 1–4)**
- Sections: Why toddlers (most burns in Iranian children are scalds, peak 2–4). Tea, samovar and kettle (out of reach, cord tucked, stable surface, not on the floor). Cooking (back burners, handles turned in, child out of the cooking area, extra care with floor-level cooking). Never hold a child while drinking something hot. Baths (cold first, then hot; check 37–38 °C; never leave tap running). Water heater temperature. Petrol and fuels stored outside. What NOT to put on a burn. Link to burns first-aid card.
- Safe wording: "A cup of tea can still scald a small child 15 minutes after it's made." / "Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. Don't use ice, toothpaste, egg, yogurt, butter, oil, flour or herbal pastes. They can make burns worse and hide the damage." / "Get medical help for any burn on a baby or young child that is bigger than a small coin, blistered, on the face, hands, feet, genitals or a joint, or caused by electricity or chemicals."

**Guide 4 — "Heaters, water heaters and invisible gas (carbon monoxide)" (all ages; winter)**
- Sections: What CO is (no smell). Signs (headache, sleepiness, vomiting, confusion, several family members ill at once, better outside). Heaters and water heaters: flue/chimney checked before winter, never block vents, yearly service. Never use charcoal braziers, picnic gas burners or hookah charcoal in closed rooms or tents. Never heat the room with the oven or stove. CO alarm near sleeping areas if you can get one. What to do: open doors and windows, get everyone outside, call 115.
- Safe wording: "Carbon monoxide has no smell, so you can't rely on noticing it. Babies and children can become ill faster than adults." / "If several people at home have headaches, sleepiness or sickness at the same time and feel better outside, think carbon monoxide: get everyone out into fresh air and call for help."

**Guide 5 — "A smoke-free home and car" (0–12, pregnancy)**
- Sections: What smoke does to children (SIDS, chest infections, ear infections, asthma). Why windows and fans don't fix it. Hookah at home (charcoal smoke and CO too). Third-hand smoke on clothes and sofas. Vapes and refills (poison risk). Small steps that help (smoke outside and away from the door, a smoke-free car, ask guests, change top layer before holding a baby). Support to quit (no shaming).
- Safe wording: "Smoke stays in the air for hours, even with a window open. Smoking outside, away from the door, protects your child much more." / "Hookah smoke includes charcoal smoke, which also contains carbon monoxide. Keep the hookah out of rooms where children live and sleep." / "Keep vape liquids and pods locked away like medicines. Swallowing even a small amount can poison a young child."

**Guide 6 — "Bad-air and dust days" (0–12, children with asthma)**
- Sections: Reading the air-quality index (colour bands). What to change at orange/red/purple (shorter, gentler outdoor play; indoor active games; keep windows closed at peak times). Cleaner indoor air (no smoking or burning indoors; an air purifier with a HEPA filter can help). Masks (not under 2; fit matters). Children with asthma (follow their plan; know warning signs). School closure days as play days (link to indoor-play content).
- Safe wording: "On days when the air-quality index is orange or worse, keep outdoor play short and gentle. Children are more sensitive to polluted air than adults, and their lungs are still growing." (sourced: AirNow, WHO. Don't add "children breathe faster" unless a source is found.) / "Masks are not the main protection for children and should never be used under 2."

**Guide 7 — "Medicines at home: storing, measuring, and when not to" (0–12)**
- Sections: Up, away and locked (including vitamins, gummies, grandparents' and guests' bags). Every adult medicine, including methadone and opium, locked and in its original bottle. Measuring with the syringe that comes with it (never kitchen spoons; mL). Cough and cold medicines in young children. Honey for cough over 1. No aspirin under 16. Antibiotics only when prescribed; no leftovers. Keep a medicines list. Poison help number.
- Safe wording: "Use the syringe or cup that comes with the medicine, not a kitchen spoon." / "Cough and cold medicines are not recommended for young children. Ask a pharmacist first." / "Never give honey to a baby under 1. For children over 1, a little honey can soothe a cough." / "Some medicines adults take, such as methadone or opium, can stop a small child's breathing even in a tiny amount. Keep them in their original child-resistant bottle, locked away, and never in a water bottle or cup. If a child might have swallowed any, call 115 at once, even if they seem fine."

**Guide 8 — "Ready for an earthquake, together" (2–12)**
- Sections: Practise Drop–Cover–Hold On as a game. Safe spots in each room; secure heavy furniture (link to tip-over). Family plan (meeting place, contact person, family password, child knows names and numbers). Go-bag with children's items. During shaking (stay in, take cover; in bed stay put). After: CO and gas safety, aftershocks. Helping children feel safe afterwards. Timed for the national drill in late November.
- Safe wording: "Practise like a game, calmly and briefly, so your child knows what to do without being frightened." / "During shaking, drop, take cover under a sturdy table and hold on. Don't run outside or stand in a doorway." / "After an earthquake, children may be clingy, scared of aftershocks, or play the earthquake over and over. This is common. Keep routines, limit news, answer questions simply and honestly, and get help if worries last more than a couple of months."

**Guide 9 — "Windows, balconies and roofs" (0–8, add to home-safety by stage)**
- Sections: Window locks/restrictors (about 10 cm). Furniture away from windows and balcony rails (including beds and sofas). Balcony doors locked; balcony rail gaps; no climbable objects on balconies. Flat roofs. Screens don't hold children.
- Safe wording: "A child can climb onto a bed or sofa and out of a window in seconds. Keep furniture away from windows and fit window locks so they open no more than about 10 cm." / "Insect screens keep bugs out, not children in."

### B. Claims the app must NOT make
- Any **Iranian law** on car seats, front-seat age, motorcycle passengers, fines or smoking in cars. Not verified.
- Any **specific dose, product or dose by weight** for any medicine, including honey amounts.
- That **any traditional burn remedy** helps, or that "a bit of toothpaste is fine".
- That **opening a window, a fan or another room** makes smoking or hookah safe for children.
- That **water in a hookah filters out the harm**.
- That **masks protect babies** or that any mask makes outdoor play safe on bad-air days.
- That **an air purifier prevents asthma attacks** (evidence is mixed).
- That a **CO alarm replaces** servicing and ventilation, or that you can **smell** carbon monoxide.
- That a child is **ready to cross roads at a fixed age**.
- That **standing in a doorway** or the **"triangle of life"** is safe in an earthquake.
- Precise multiples like "a hookah = 200 cigarettes" as fact.
- Anything that implies parents on methadone or other treatment are bad parents.
- Statistics from news quotes (police figures) presented as research.

### C. Red flags (send to emergency care, 115)
- Any burn on a baby; burns larger than a small coin, blistered or deep, or on the face, hands, feet, genitals or joints; electrical or chemical burns.
- Possible CO exposure with headache, vomiting, drowsiness, confusion, fainting, or several people ill at once.
- Any possible swallowing of methadone, opium, other adult medicines, nicotine liquid or button batteries, **even if the child seems fine** (opioid effects can be delayed and return).
- Sleepiness, slow or noisy breathing, or pinpoint pupils after a possible ingestion. [recall, unverified for pupils; have a clinician check]
- Breathing difficulty on a bad-air day, especially in a child with asthma who isn't helped by their usual plan.
- Any fall from a window, balcony or roof, or a child who is drowsy, vomiting or acting strangely after a fall (link to head-injury card).
- A child injured in a crash, even if they look fine.
