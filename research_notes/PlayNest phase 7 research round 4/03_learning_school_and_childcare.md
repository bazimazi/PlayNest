## How to read these notes

**Source tags**
- **[opened]**: I fetched and read the page or PDF itself. Publisher and date are given in the source list.
- **[search]**: I only saw search snippets, an abstract, or a secondary summary. I did not read the paper.
- **[recall, unverified]**: background knowledge I did not re-check this session. Verify it before quoting it in the app.

**Effect sizes** are reported only where I saw them in a source, and the tag says which kind of source.

**Evidence scale**
- **Strong**: replicated RCTs or meta-analyses.
- **Moderate**: several longitudinal studies, or small RCTs.
- **Weak**: correlational, single-study, or small sample.
- **Contested**: good studies disagree.

## 1. Helping with homework and school (ages 6–12)

**Key findings**
- **Hill & Tyson (2009), *Developmental Psychology*.** [opened: HarvardX course summary of the paper; HGSE Usable Knowledge article, 30 Oct 2009]
  - A meta-analysis of 50 studies, all on **middle school (grades 6–8)**.
  - The correlation between parental involvement and achievement averaged **r = .18**, ranging from −.49 to .73 across the 32 correlational studies.
  - Academic socialization was positive and significant, and stronger than school-based involvement. It means talking about the value of education, linking schoolwork to the child's goals and interests, and discussing learning strategies and plans.
  - School-based involvement was also positive and significant.
  - Home-based involvement overall was **not** significant. Within it, enriching activities (books at home, museums, libraries) were positive, while **homework help had the strongest negative association**.
  - Hill told HGSE that homework help "actually had a negative impact." She recommends "scaffolding independence": let the child try first, then step in.
  - **Caveats:**
    - The data are correlational. Reverse causation is likely: struggling children get more help.
    - The sample is grades 6–8, so applying it to 6–10-year-olds is an extrapolation.
- **Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008), *Review of Educational Research* 78(4).** [search]
  - A synthesis of studies since 1987.
  - **Training parents to help with homework** (14 studies) was linked to higher homework completion, fewer homework problems, and *possibly* better achievement for primary-age children.
  - Non-experimental links between involvement and achievement were "small and often not significant," and moderated by grade, subject and type of involvement.
  - Parents setting rules (time, place) showed a stronger association than other strategies.
  - The association was **negative for maths** and positive for verbal outcomes.
  - A later meta-analysis (Xu et al.) reported an overall weak negative link. Contested. [search]
- **Cooper, Robinson & Patall (2006), *RER* 76(1).** [opened: Duke Today, 7 Mar 2006; plus search]
  - The homework–achievement link is much stronger in grades 7–12 than in primary school.
  - SEDL's summary says the 35 correlational studies show little or no relationship for primary students. [search]
  - Cooper's advice:
    - Primary homework should be short, lead to success without much struggle, and only occasionally involve parents.
    - Even in high school, overloading students is not linked to better grades.
    - At primary age, homework may mostly build study habits and time management.
  - The "10 minutes per grade" rule is a common teacher convention that Cooper says his findings are consistent with. It is not an experimentally derived limit.
- **Moroni et al. (2015), *Journal of Educational Research* 108(5).** [search]
  - 1,685 Swiss sixth-graders, followed longitudinally.
  - **How often** parents helped was *negatively* linked to achievement growth.
  - Help the child **perceived as supportive** predicted gains; help perceived as **intrusive** predicted declines.
  - Without controls for prior achievement and family background, effect sizes are overestimated.
- **Pomerantz, Moorman & Litwack (2007), *RER* 77(3), "More is not always better".** [search: citing papers only]
  - Quality dimensions of involvement:
    - autonomy support versus control
    - a focus on process versus a focus on the person ("you're smart")
    - positive versus negative feeling
    - positive versus negative beliefs about the child's potential
  - Controlling help (orders, pressure, doing the task for the child, rushing) is linked to disengagement, especially in struggling children (Ng, Kenney-Benson & Pomerantz 2004).
- **EEF, "Working with Parents to Support Children's Learning" guidance report (2018), recommendations relevant to parents.** [opened: full PDF via DERA]
  - Shared book reading should be central for young children. Playing with letters and numbers at home is also linked to better outcomes.
  - Tips that prompt longer, more frequent conversations during reading make home reading more effective. Gifting books *alone* is unlikely to help.
  - "Support parents to create a regular routine and encourage good homework habits, but **be cautious about promoting direct parental assistance with homework (particularly for older children)**."
  - Parents can help children "set goals, plan, and manage their time, effort, and emotions". This "will often be more valuable than direct help with homework tasks."
  - Box 3 (homework tips): quality matters more than quantity; know about the homework and show interest and encouragement. For a child who is struggling, it may be better to send them back to the teacher than for the parent to become the instructor.
  - Listening to a child read: "**pause, prompt, praise**". Pause so they can work out the word; give a clue, not the answer; praise concentration and problem-solving.
  - The EEF's honest framing: evidence on how schools can engage parents to raise attainment is "mixed", and "interventions designed to engage parents in homework have generally not been linked to increased attainment."

**Iranian context**
- **Homework policy.** In Shahrivar 1397 (Sept 2018), the Ministry of Education's deputy for primary education (Rezvan Hakimzadeh) announced that grades 1–3 would have **no مشق شب (nightly copy-work homework)**. It would be replaced by "skill-based homework" (تکالیف مهارت‌محور), such as:
  - joining a shopping trip and tracking the bill
  - noting environmental hazards on the walk home
  - tidying their own room and books.

  [opened: Asriran, 28 Shahrivar 1397] Other officials said reading and some copy-work would remain, and the minister favoured changing homework rather than abolishing it. [search] **Current enforcement is unknown.** Many parents still report heavy copy-work. [recall, unverified]
- **Entrance-exam pressure starts in primary school.** The Sampad (استعدادهای درخشان / تیزهوشان) **6th→7th-grade entrance exam was still held in 1404 and 1405**, with results announced by the testing organisation in Mordad 1405. [search: Mehr News, Aug 2026] So prep classes (کلاس تقویتی / آمادگی تیزهوشان) during grades 4–6 are a real pressure point.
- **Konkur and earlier pressure.** Iranian authors describe the university exam (کنکور) as the main source of adolescents' academic stress, and say it pushes families and schools to intensify study in the primary years. [search: Springer EPAG 2021; Qazvin study]
- **Shadow education (private tutoring).** Iranian studies are mostly about high-school students. Higher-income families tutor more, and authors raise equity concerns. **I found no prevalence figure for primary-age tutoring.** [search]
- **Children's stress.** Valizadeh et al. (2012, *Journal of Caring Sciences*) studied 839 Tabriz children in grades 3–5:
  - worry was the most common stress symptom, at 69.4%
  - the authors note that Iranian research on primary-school stress is very limited.

  [search]
- **Homework anxiety.**
  - A Khorasan Razavi intervention for "اضطراب تکلیف شب" (homework anxiety) had only **n = 24**; it reported a reduction. [search]
  - A qualitative COVID-era study found homework arguments causing parent–child conflict, and parents often doing the homework themselves. [search]
  - Evidence quality: **weak** (small and qualitative). It still fits the international picture.

**Evidence strength**
- "Academic socialization > homework help": *moderate* (meta-analytic but correlational, middle-school).
- "Quality (autonomy-supportive, warm) > quantity of help": *moderate* (several longitudinal studies, consistent with SDT literature).
- "Homework has little achievement benefit in primary years": *moderate* (correlational).
- Iranian data: *weak*.

## 2. Parents' maths anxiety and "maths talk"

**Key findings**
- **Maloney, Ramirez, Gunderson, Levine & Beilock (2015), *Psychological Science*.** [opened: UChicago News, 10 Aug 2015]
  - 438 first- and second-graders and their caregivers, measured at the start and end of the school year.
  - Children of maths-anxious parents learned less maths over the year and became more maths-anxious, **but only when those parents helped with maths homework frequently**.
  - Reading achievement, used as a control, was unaffected.
  - Beilock: parents who say things like "This stuff makes me nervous" pass the attitude on.
  - Maloney: "We need to develop better tools to teach parents how to most effectively help their children with math."
- **Berkowitz et al. (2015), *Science*.** [opened: UChicago News, 8 Oct 2015]
  - 587 first-graders were randomised to an iPad app with short maths story-problems, or to a reading-app control.
  - Frequent users finished **almost three months ahead** in maths. Even about once a week helped.
  - Gains were largest for children of highly maths-anxious parents.
- **Schaeffer et al. (2018), *J Exp Psych: General*.** [search] A follow-up of the same sample:
  - Children of maths-anxious parents who had used the maths app kept pace for years after use dropped.
  - Parents' anxiety itself did *not* change, but their expectations and the value they placed on maths rose.
  - This is the same sample, not an independent replication.
- **Teachers' maths anxiety and girls.** Beilock et al. (2010, *PNAS*): female first- and second-grade teachers' maths anxiety was linked to girls endorsing "boys are good at maths" and to lower maths achievement. [recall, unverified; the search budget ran out]

**What to take from it**
- The problem is *anxious, frequent, explaining-style help*, not the parent's own maths level.
- Low-pressure, playful number talk (stories, games, everyday maths) seems to bypass the problem. The app already has an everyday maths guide; link to it.
- Avoid "I was never a maths person" or "maths is hard for our family". These are plausible routes of transmission (Beilock's quote), but no trial has tested scripts directly. [inference]

**Evidence strength**
- Parents' anxiety × frequent homework help → less learning: *weak–moderate* (one longitudinal study).
- Playful at-home number stories helping, especially for children of anxious parents: *moderate* (one RCT plus follow-up, same team).

## 3. Learning to read, ages 4–8, at home

**Systematic phonics**
- **US National Reading Panel (2000).** [search]
  - Systematic phonics had a mean effect of about **d = 0.44** across 38 comparisons (one summary says 36 studies).
  - Larger gains when started in kindergarten or grade 1.
  - Spelling gains were moderate to large in K–1.
  - Camilli et al.'s reanalysis gave **d ≈ 0.24**. Stuebing et al. replied that this is still practically meaningful.
- **Rose Review (England, 2006).** [search]
  - Recommended high-quality **systematic synthetic phonics** as the prime approach to decoding.
  - Adopted the "Simple View of Reading": word recognition × language comprehension.
  - Critics objected to one method for all children, and early-years specialists worried about starting by age 5.
- **EEF Teaching & Learning Toolkit, phonics.** [search: secondary pages quoting the toolkit]
  - About **+5 months**, very low cost, extensive evidence. Synthetic approaches average more than analytic ones.
  - Phonics improves **accuracy, not necessarily comprehension**. "Not a silver bullet."
- **Letter sounds vs letter names (Piasta & Wagner 2010, *Reading Research Quarterly*, meta-analysis).** [search]
  - Teaching names and sounds together, plus writing letters, appears to work best.
  - Letter names can help children learn sounds when the name contains the sound.
  - The studies were mostly North American.
  - **Practical answer:** sounds are what children blend into words, so prioritise *sounds* in play, but names are not harmful. **Follow the method the child's school uses.** (See the Persian note below.)

**Shared reading after children can read; reading for pleasure**
- EEF: "Shared reading is an important strategy from a very early age and continues to be so as children start to develop independent reading skills." As children get older, listen to them read ("pause, prompt, praise") and keep talking about the story for comprehension. [opened: EEF 2018]
- **OECD *Reading for Change* (PISA 2000).** [search]
  - Engagement in reading had the largest median correlation with achievement, larger than socio-economic status.
  - Highly engaged 15-year-olds from the lowest-status homes outscored poorly engaged peers from the highest-status homes.
  - Correlational.
- **PISA 2018:** across the OECD, the enjoyment-of-reading index fell between 2009 and 2018, and more students said they read only if they have to. [search: ACER, Education Counts]

**Persian-specific**
- **Orthography** (Bakhtiar et al., 2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*, PMC8353368). [opened]
  - Persian writes its 3 long vowels as letters, but **its 3 short vowels are not written** in standard script. Words with only long vowels are "transparent"; words with short vowels are "opaque".
  - Iranian schooling shows short-vowel diacritics (اِعراب / حرکت‌گذاری) **fully in grade 1, partly in grade 2, and removes them by grade 3**.
  - Iranian children in grades 1–4 read transparent words faster and/or more accurately than opaque ones (Baluch & Shahidi 1991; Rahbari & Sénéchal 2010).
- **Rahbari & Sénéchal (2010), *Developmental Psychology* 46:1514–27.** [search]
  - 140 children in grades 1–4.
  - The biggest reading and spelling jump was between grades 1 and 2.
  - The transparency effect shrank with grade, but opaque words stayed harder.
- **Rahbari, Sénéchal & Arab-Moghaddam (2007):** in 109 grade-2 children, phonological skills strongly predicted both reading and spelling. [search]
- **Implication for parents:** Persian decoding starts very regular, because grade 1 texts are fully vowelled. The hard step comes in grades 2–3, when children must use vocabulary and context to "fill in" the unwritten vowels.
  - Reading aloud to children and building spoken vocabulary therefore matter *more*, not less, after grade 1. [inference from the orthography and the Simple View; not directly tested]
- **First grade (کلاس اول) teaching.** [search]
  - The grade-1 Persian books are organised around picture pages (نگاره), letter-sound "signs" (نشانه‌ها) and fluent-reading passages (روان‌خوانی). One analysis describes the reading method as whole-to-part.
  - A qualitative study of grade-1 teachers in Khorasan Razavi listed **"interference of parents' incorrect teaching"** as a problem, alongside low teacher skill in teaching sounds.
  - Teachers commonly ask parents not to drill alphabet *names* (الف، بِ، پِ) or rote copy before school, and to use the sound of the sign instead. [recall, unverified; the qualitative study supports "parents' teaching interferes" but I did not see the specifics]
  - **«بازی با حروف» (playing with letters):**
    - I found no Iranian trial.
    - The EEF's "playing with letters and numbers" and environmental print (letters on shop signs, labels) are the nearest evidence, and they are correlational.
    - Safe framing: playful sound games (rhymes, "what sound does باران start with?", clapping syllables, hidden-letter hunts) with no drilling.
- **Dyslexia in Iran.**
  - A meta-analysis estimates developmental dyslexia (نارساخوانی) at **about 5.7%** of Iranian primary pupils, more in boys. [search]
  - Single-city studies range from about 5% (Qom, n = 1,562) to about 10% (Arak, Isfahan; smaller samples). [search]

**Signs of reading difficulty worth raising** (NHS "Dyslexia – symptoms", reviewed 11 Mar 2026) [opened]
- Learning to speak later than other children, or little interest in alphabet or letter games.
- Reading well below what is expected for their age. Slow, hesitant reading aloud.
- Messy writing with many spelling errors.
- Difficulty with sequences (days of the week), telling the time, or numbers.
- Trouble planning and organising schoolwork.
- NHS route in the UK: SENCO (the school's special-educational-needs coordinator), then school screening, a GP check for hearing and vision, and an educational psychologist. A GP cannot diagnose dyslexia.

Other early signs and risk factors (IDA/Reading Rockets, Neuhaus) [search]:
- trouble rhyming, or splitting and blending sounds
- trouble sequencing a story
- in kindergarten, difficulty reading single words
- **family history** of reading difficulty.

BDA: dyslexia is not only about reading; it can affect organisation and memory. Each person experiences it differently. [opened: BDA signs hub]

**Evidence strength**
- Systematic phonics for decoding: *strong*.
- Phonics improving comprehension by itself: *weak*.
- Shared reading continuing to matter after decoding: *moderate*.
- Reading for pleasure → achievement: *moderate, correlational*.
- Persian-specific home advice: *weak* (inference from orthography research).

## 4. Handwriting vs typing for early letter learning (brief)

- **Longcamp et al. (2005).** Children aged 3–5 copied letters by hand or by typing. In the older children, handwriting produced better letter recognition. [search]
- **James & Engelhardt (2012), *Trends in Neuroscience & Education*.** In preliterate 4–5-year-olds, *printing* letters, more than tracing or typing, was linked to adult-like letter-processing brain activity (fMRI). Small sample. [search plus recall; fMRI details unverified]
- **Seyll & Content (2022), *Frontiers in Psychology*.** [opened]
  - 61 kindergarteners learned invented symbols by **handwriting (about 76% correct)**, **composition, i.e. assembling the parts without writing (about 78%)**, or **typing (about 60%)**.
  - Handwriting and composition did not differ.
  - The authors conclude that **close visual attention to the letter's shape**, not the hand movement itself, may explain the advantage.
  - Artificial symbols; no reading outcomes.
- **Van der Weel & Van der Meer (2024, *Frontiers*), EEG.**
  - 36 adults showed more brain "connectivity" while handwriting. This was heavily press-covered as "handwriting boosts learning".
  - Pinet & Longcamp's 2025 commentary notes:
    - no learning was measured
    - the participants were adults
    - typing was done with one finger
    - connectivity ≠ better learning.

  [search]
- **Bottom line:** For *learning letter shapes* (ages 4–7), forming letters by hand (or otherwise attending closely to their shape) beats pressing keys. Evidence: *moderate* for letter recognition, *weak* beyond that.
  - It does **not** show that typing harms older children, or that handwriting "makes kids smarter".
  - Persian letters change shape by position (initial, medial, final, isolated) and have dots that distinguish letters (ب پ ت ث). Attention to the shape is plausibly extra useful here. [inference, untested]

## 5. Choosing childcare/preschool and settling in (0–6)

**Key findings**
- **NICHD Study of Early Child Care (SECCYD).**
  - **2003 release** [opened: NICHD press release, 16 Jul 2003]:
    - More hours in non-maternal care from birth to 4½ were linked to adult ratings of more assertiveness, disobedience and aggression at 4½ and in kindergarten. The link was stronger for centre care.
    - There was no hour threshold.
    - Higher-quality care went with fewer problems but did not erase the link.
    - **Maternal sensitivity was the strongest predictor** of behaviour. Family income and parental education also mattered.
    - Behaviour was "well within the normal range" for the vast majority.
    - The study is not causal.
  - **Effect sizes:** NICHD (2006) described the effects as small to modest. [search]
  - **Age-15 follow-up (Vandell et al. 2010, *Child Development*).** [search]
    - **Higher-quality care predicted higher cognitive-academic achievement at 15**, with escalating benefits at higher quality, and less self-reported externalising.
    - More hours predicted slightly more risk-taking and impulsivity, **mitigated by high-quality care**.
- **Process quality is what matters.** OECD *Engaging Young Children* (Starting Strong, 2018): staff–child interactions ("process quality") are the primary driver of developmental gains. Structural factors (ratios, group size, training) matter mainly because they make good interactions possible. [search; the "primary driver" wording was seen on a retailer excerpt, so verify it]
- **NAEYC recommended ratios and maximum group sizes.** [search: state and university copies of the NAEYC chart; naeyc.org blocked]
  - Infants: 1:3 to 1:4, groups of 6–8.
  - Toddlers 12–36 months: 1:4 to 1:6, groups of up to 12.
  - Children 2½–5: about 1:8 to 1:10, groups of up to 16–20.
  - These are best-practice targets, not law anywhere.
- **Grandparent care (UK Millennium Cohort; Hansen & Hawkes 2009, *Journal of Social Policy*).** [opened: CLS/IOE press release, 10 Feb 2009] 4,800 children of working mothers, assessed at age 3:
  - Grandparent care was linked to **better vocabulary** in some groups, e.g. highly educated mothers.
  - Nursery care was linked to higher school-readiness (basic concepts) scores.
  - Grandparent care was linked to slightly more mother-reported behaviour problems, especially peer difficulties in boys.
  - Observational. Each type of care has different strengths.

**Settling in**
- **Ahnert, Gunnar, Lamb & Barthel (2004), *Child Development* 75:639–50.** About 15-month-olds entering Berlin childcare. [search plus opened: NIH grant abstract]
  - Cortisol was elevated in the first weeks without the mother.
  - **Attachment was more likely to become or stay secure when mothers stayed longer during adaptation.**
  - Securely attached toddlers protested and cried **more** at separation than insecure toddlers. **Crying at drop-off is not a sign that something is wrong.**
- **Berlin settling-in model (Berliner Eingewöhnungsmodell).** [opened: Universität Ulm Kita handout]
  1. **Days 1–3:** the parent stays about an hour in the room with the child, passive but available. One key caregiver approaches gently through play. *No separation.*
  2. **Day 4:** after a few minutes, the parent says a **clear goodbye**, leaves the room, stays nearby and returns within 30 minutes. If the child is inconsolable, the parent is called back at once and the next attempt waits until about day 7.
  3. **Stabilisation:** separations lengthen. Half-days first. The parent stays reachable.
  4. **Finished** when the child accepts comfort from the caregiver: protests at the goodbye, but settles and plays. This usually takes **10–20 days** depending on age (the HABA overview says 2–3 weeks).
  - This is a practice model. The evidence for it is the attachment and cortisol work above, not a head-to-head trial. [recall, unverified]
  - It says "emphatically say good-bye". Sneaking away isn't in it.
- **Separation anxiety by age (AAP HealthyChildren, updated 11 May 2026).** [opened]
  - Some babies show it at 4–5 months; most develop more robust anxiety around **9 months**.
  - Many toddlers have a rough patch at **15–18 months**, worse when hungry, tired or ill.
  - Keep goodbye rituals **"short and sweet"**; lingering prolongs it. Keep promises about when you'll return. Be consistent, and don't come back in because of pleas.
  - It rarely persists daily after the preschool years. Talk to the paediatrician if the child isn't adapting.
  - The AAP page doesn't mention "sneaking out". The "don't sneak away" advice is practitioner consensus, consistent with keeping trust and predictability. [recall, unverified]

**Iranian context** [search]
- **مهدکودک** (ages about 2–5) is licensed by **Behzisti (the State Welfare Organisation)**. **پیش‌دبستانی** (preschool, about 5–6) is under the Ministry of Education.
- A policy paper rated Iranian preschool access as low compared with other countries. National enrolment figures are hard to find.
- Grandparents are a very common childcare arrangement. [recall, unverified; no Iranian prevalence study found]
- Iranian press advice often says mothers must care for children until age 2 and that early daycare causes "feelings of rejection". [search: Jam-e Jam] The NICHD evidence does not support this as a general rule (see Overclaims).
- Iran's national amblyopia (lazy eye) vision screening for ages 3–6 is often run through kindergartens (§7).

**Evidence strength**
- Quality and warm, stable caregivers matter: *strong* (consistent observational evidence across cohorts, OECD synthesis).
- Hours → small behaviour effects: *moderate, small effects*. Family factors matter more.
- Gradual, parent-accompanied settling-in: *moderate* (one key study plus practice consensus).

## 6. Video games (ages 6–12)

**Key findings**
- **Playtime and wellbeing.** Vuorre, Johannes, Magnusson & Przybylski (2022), *Royal Society Open Science*. [opened: Oxford Internet Institute, 27 Jul 2022]
  - About 39,000 **adult** players of 7 games, using industry telemetry over 6 weeks.
  - "Little to no evidence for a causal connection between gameplay and well-being."
  - **Motivation matters more than hours:** playing because you want to is fine; feeling you *have to* play is worse.
  - Adults only, so applying this to children is an extrapolation.
- **Violent games and aggression.**
  - Przybylski & Weinstein (2019, *Royal Society Open Science*), a preregistered study of 1,004 British 14–15-year-olds: violent-game time was not associated with carer-rated aggression. [search]
  - The **APA 2020 resolution** (updating 2015):
    - There is insufficient evidence of a causal link to *violent behaviour*.
    - It still describes a small link with *aggression* (e.g. hostile thoughts, lab aggression).
    - It warns against blaming mass violence on games.

    [search; apa.org returned empty]
  - Ferguson's meta-analyses dispute even the aggression link. **Contested.** [search]
- **Cognition.** Chaarani et al. (2022), *JAMA Network Open*, ABCD study: [search]
  - 9–10-year-olds who played **3+ hours/day** did better on inhibition and working-memory tasks than non-gamers.
  - **Cross-sectional.** The authors note that children with these skills may choose to game.
  - Not a reason to encourage 3 hours a day. That much play conflicts with sleep, activity and homework guidance. [inference]
- **Gaming disorder (ICD-11).** [opened: WHO Q&A]
  - Impaired control, increasing priority over other activities, and continuing despite harm.
  - It must cause **significant impairment** and normally be present **at least 12 months**.
  - WHO: it affects "only a small proportion" of people who game.
  - Pooled prevalence is about **3.05%** (Stevens et al. 2021; 1.96% in stricter-sampling studies), higher in adolescents and males. [search]
  - Estimates vary a lot with the definition. Many use looser DSM-5 "IGD" criteria.
- **Loot boxes.**
  - Zendle & Cairns (2018, *PLOS ONE*), 7,422 gamers: loot-box spending was linked to problem-gambling severity, more than other in-game spending. The direction of cause is unknown. [search]
  - UK DCMS (July 2022) decided **not** to bring loot boxes under the Gambling Act. It relied on industry measures, including that **children should not be able to buy loot boxes without parental approval** (industry guidance July 2023). [search]
  - Belgium's ban was poorly enforced: 82% of top-grossing iPhone games still had paid random items (Xiao 2023). [search]
- **Age ratings.**
  - **PEGI** [opened: pegi.info]:
    - Ages 3/7/12/16/18, with content descriptors and "in-game purchases".
    - The page says that for titles submitted **since June 2026**, paid random items → PEGI 16, unrestricted chat → PEGI 18, and "pressure to play" streaks → PEGI 7–12.
    - These rules are new as of June 2026; re-verify them before quoting.
  - **ESRB** is the US equivalent. [recall]
  - **Iran's ESRA (اسرا)**, run under the National Foundation of Computer Games (بنیاد ملی بازی‌های رایانه‌ای), uses +3/+7/+12/+15/+18 and considers religious and social norms. The age bands have changed over time. [search]
  - Ratings cover **content**, not difficulty, and not what other players say in chat.
- **Iran usage.** DIREC surveys (2018–19), as reported in English-language press [search]:
  - About 7–8.7 million under-12s play regularly. About 47% of children play daily.
  - The average is about 81 minutes a day, about 90% on phones or tablets.
  - About 42% of players ignore age ratings.
  - The figures are dated and inconsistent.

**What parents can do (consensus guidance; evidence weak but low-risk)**
- Co-play sometimes, and ask the child to teach you the game.
- Check the rating *and* the chat, purchase and loot-box settings.
- Turn off or restrict chat with strangers. Use platform parental controls and spending limits.
- Keep consoles and phones out of bedrooms at night. [recall, unverified]
- Use stopping points ("finish the level, then dinner") rather than abrupt cut-offs.
- Watch for *function* (sleep, school, friends, mood) more than minutes.

## 7. Myopia and eyes

**Key findings**
- **He et al. (2015), *JAMA*, Guangzhou.** A cluster RCT in 12 schools with about 1,900 first-graders. Intervention schools added **one 40-minute outdoor class each school day**, and parents were encouraged to take children outdoors at weekends.
  - **3-year myopia incidence: 30.4% vs 39.5%** (press: "23% less likely").
  - Editorialist Repka: "The effect is very modest and should not be overstated."

  [search plus opened: Sun Yat-sen University news]
- **IMI white paper, "Risk Factors for Myopia" (Morgan et al., *IOVS* 2021).** [opened]
  - More time outdoors **delays onset**; the effect on progression is mixed.
  - School programmes are validated in RCTs.
  - Recommends about **2 hours a day outdoors in daylight**, especially in preschool and early primary.
  - **Education and near work** are major risk factors. The paper notes that myopia-epidemic countries start homework **in preschool**, and suggests limiting homework at these ages.
  - Digital-device associations are commonly reported, but the epidemics **predate smartphones**. It is unclear whether screens are simply a new form of near work.
  - Parental myopia raises risk.
  - The mechanism is probably bright light → retinal dopamine. **Vitamin D is ruled out.**
- **20-20-20 rule** (every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds). It was designed for **eye strain** in computer users, not myopia prevention. **No myopia RCT evidence found.** [search] It is harmless as a break habit.
- **Global trend.** Holden et al. (2016, *Ophthalmology*) projected about **49.8%** of the world myopic by 2050 (95% CI 43–56%), with about 9.8% highly myopic. A projection, not a measurement. [search]
- **Iran is lower than East Asia.** Correct the brief's premise accordingly:
  - A 2024 WHO EMHJ meta-analysis covered 27 studies, 51,111 school-age children, in the Eastern Mediterranean region. Pooled childhood myopia was **5.23%** (7.5% at ages 11–17 vs 3.9% at 5–10). **Iranian studies ranged from 0.41% to 4.4%.** [opened]
  - Hashemi et al. found 3.04% myopia in 7-year-olds across 8 cities, 2.6% in rural children, and 14.9% in Dezful children aged 6–15. [search]
  - A national estimate reported **rising refractive errors over 28 years**. [search]
  - Framing for the app: Iranian children's myopia is not at East-Asian levels. Outdoor time is still the best-supported cheap habit, and it has other benefits.
- **Vision screening.**
  - **UK (NHS, page reviewed Mar 2023)** [opened]: checks at birth and 6–8 weeks, questions at 1 and 2–2½ years, and **screening at school entry, age 4–5**. Free NHS sight tests for under-16s.
  - **US (AAP/AAO)** [search]: instrument-based screening from age 1, by age 2 at the latest. Visual acuity charts from age 3–4. Then at 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 15. Check the current Bright Futures schedule.
  - **Iran** [search]: a national two-stage **amblyopia screening for ages 3–6** has run since 1996, through Behzisti/kindergartens. Coverage was 67% in 2005, about 90% in a later report.
- **Signs of a vision problem** (NHS) [opened]:
  - eyes not pointing the same way
  - headaches or eye strain
  - holding books very close, or losing their place
  - poor hand–eye coordination, or unusual clumsiness
  - frequent eye-rubbing
  - sitting very close to the TV.
- **Treatment.** Myopia-control treatments (low-dose atropine, special lenses) exist and are a matter for an eye doctor. Out of scope for the app beyond "ask". [recall, unverified]

**Evidence strength**
- Outdoor time delays onset: *strong* (RCTs plus observational).
- Near work: *moderate, mechanism unclear*.
- Screens specifically: *weak/contested*.
- 20-20-20 for myopia: *no evidence*.

## 8. After-school activities and over-scheduling (brief)

- **Mahoney, Harris & Eccles (2006), SRCD Social Policy Report.** [opened: SRCD brief]
  - "Very limited" support for the over-scheduling hypothesis. Only about **3–6% of youth do 20+ hours a week** of organised activities; the average is about 5 hours.
  - Participants generally fare better than non-participants (grades, school completion, less substance use, more time with parents). About 40% don't participate.
  - **Diminishing returns** at very high levels.
  - Some highly scheduled subgroups had **less time with parents**.
  - This is US data on adolescents, and correlational: children who join activities differ from those who don't.
- **Barker et al. (2014):** more less-structured time in 6–7-year-olds was linked to better self-directed EF. Correlational. [already in the phase 6 notes]
- **Ginsburg (2007), AAP clinical report:** a "hurried lifestyle" plus a focus on academics and enrichment is reducing free play. Loss of free time may contribute to stress. The report is expert opinion, not a trial. [search]
- **For Iran:** the realistic risk is **academic** over-scheduling (کلاس تقویتی, exam prep), more than sports. This links back to §1 and to the IMI note on near work and homework. [inference]
- **Sleep:** children aged 6–12 need about **9–12 hours** (AASM consensus). [recall, unverified] Activities that push bedtime late are a reasonable red flag.

## Disagreements / contested

1. **Homework help.**
   - Hill & Tyson and Patall's correlational results are negative or null.
   - Patall's trials suggest *trained* parents improve completion. Moroni shows quality is positive while quantity is negative.
   - Resolution: *how* you help matters more than *whether*. Reverse causation inflates the negative findings.
2. **Primary-school homework value.** Cooper: little achievement link. The EEF: short, focused primary homework has *some* support. Iran's 2018 policy shift to skill-based tasks is consistent with Cooper.
3. **Phonics effect size.** NRP d ≈ 0.44 vs Camilli's reanalysis ≈ 0.24. Rose's "synthetic phonics first" model is criticised as too narrow. Everyone agrees phonics is necessary but not sufficient.
4. **Letter names vs sounds.** UK practice is sounds-first. The meta-analysis favours teaching both together. Iranian schools teach sound-based نشانه. Follow the school.
5. **Handwriting.**
   - Recognition advantage: fairly consistent.
   - Mechanism (motor vs visual attention): disputed.
   - EEG "brain connectivity" claims in adults: strongly critiqued.
6. **Violent games → aggression.** The APA retains a small aggression link. Przybylski and Ferguson find none in preregistered and meta-analytic work.
7. **Gaming disorder** prevalence (about 2–10%) depends on the criteria. Some scholars dispute whether it should be a diagnosis at all. [recall]
8. **Childcare hours.** NICHD links hours to small behaviour effects. The UK Millennium Cohort found no formal-care–behaviour link. Quality buffers in some analyses but not others.
9. **Myopia and screens.** Associations are common, but causation is unclear, and the epidemic predates smartphones.

## Overclaims to avoid

- "Helping with homework improves grades." Or the opposite: "never help with homework".
- "Homework is useless" for all ages. Short, focused tasks and reading at home are fine.
- "Maths anxiety is genetic", or "your anxiety will ruin your child's maths". The effect was conditional and modest.
- Naming or promoting a specific maths app. The trial used one app, but the ingredient is short, playful number talk.
- "Phonics alone teaches reading/comprehension."
- "Teaching letter names early confuses children."
- "Every slow reader is dyslexic", or "dyslexia can be diagnosed by an app or by parents".
- "Handwriting makes children smarter / activates the whole brain."
- "Typing is bad for kids."
- "Daycare harms attachment / children need their mother exclusively until age 2." NICHD found small effects, family factors mattered most, and quality helped.
- "Crying at drop-off means the nursery is bad." Securely attached toddlers often protest *more*.
- "Video games cause violence."
- "Video games make kids smarter." The ABCD study is cross-sectional.
- "X minutes of gaming is safe/unsafe." Function, content and motivation matter more than a number.
- "Gaming addiction is common." The ICD-11 disorder is rare and needs 12 months of significant impairment.
- "Screens cause myopia", or "the 20-20-20 rule prevents myopia".
- "Iranian children have a myopia epidemic like East Asia." The data show lower rates, but rising refractive error.
- "Over-scheduled children are stressed." True only for a small minority.
- "Tutoring classes (کلاس تقویتی) are needed to keep up." No evidence for primary-age benefit found.

## App takeaways

### Proposed guides

**Guide A: "Homework without the battles" (ages 6–12)**
- **Sections:**
  1. Your job vs the child's job.
  2. Set up a routine (time, place, phone away).
  3. How to help when asked (hints, not answers).
  4. Talk about why learning matters.
  5. When it's too much.
  6. Iran note: مشق شب, کلاس تقویتی, تیزهوشان prep.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Research suggests *how* you help matters more than *how much*. Warm, hands-off support (being nearby, giving a hint, asking 'what's your plan?') is linked to better progress than sitting over a child or correcting every line."
  - "In primary school, homework has only a small link with how well children do. Its main job at this age is building habits. A short, calm routine beats a long, tense one."
  - "If your child is stuck, try: 'Show me what you've tried. What could you try next?' Give a clue, not the answer. If they're still stuck, it's fine to write a note to the teacher. That tells the teacher something useful."
  - "Talking about why learning matters, and linking school to things your child cares about, is one of the involvement types most consistently linked with achievement."
  - Iran: "Extra classes and entrance-exam prep in primary school are common, but we found no good evidence that they help young children. Keep free time, play, sleep and outdoor time protected. If you choose prep classes, watch your child's mood and sleep."
- **Avoid:** "Studies show helping hurts."

**Guide B: "Maths feelings: yours and theirs" (ages 4–12)**
- **Sections:**
  1. Why parents' maths feelings matter.
  2. What to say instead of "I'm not a maths person".
  3. Number stories and games.
  4. Homework help if maths makes *you* nervous.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "One study found that when maths-anxious parents helped *often* with maths homework, children learned a bit less maths that year. When parents didn't help often, there was no difference. The fix isn't to step away. It's to keep maths playful and low-pressure."
  - "Try 'This one's tricky. Let's figure it out together', not 'I was never good at maths'."
  - "A few minutes of playful number stories or games, even once a week, helped children in one trial, especially children of maths-nervous parents."
  - Link to the existing everyday maths guide.

**Guide C: "Learning to read at home: English and Persian" (ages 4–8)**
- **Sections:**
  1. How schools teach reading (phonics; Persian نشانه and sounds).
  2. Sound games before school.
  3. Listening to your child read: "pause, prompt, praise".
  4. Keep reading *to* them.
  5. Reading for fun.
  6. Persian: why grade 2–3 gets harder (vowels disappear).
  7. When to talk to the teacher.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Children learn to read by linking letters to sounds. Schools teach this step by step. At home, playful sound games help most: rhymes, 'what sound does *باران* start with?', clapping syllables. Ask the teacher how they teach letters, and use the same sounds."
  - "When your child reads to you: **pause** to let them try, give a **prompt** or clue rather than the word, and **praise** the effort and good guessing."
  - "Keep reading aloud *after* your child can read. Stories above their reading level build the vocabulary and understanding they need, especially in Persian. From grade 2–3, short vowels stop being written, and children rely on knowing the word."
  - "Children who enjoy reading tend to do better at school. Let them choose: comics, facts, stories."
  - Avoid "phonics guarantees reading" and "don't teach letter names".

**Guide D: "Starting nursery or preschool: choosing and settling in" (ages 0–6)**
- **Sections:**
  1. What quality looks like.
  2. Questions to ask on a visit.
  3. Grandparents as carers.
  4. Settling in gradually.
  5. Goodbyes.
  6. Crying is normal.
  7. When to worry.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "The most important thing is warm, responsive carers who talk and play with children, and who stay. Look for a small group, enough adults, low staff turnover, and one key person for your child."
  - "Large studies find that the *quality* of care and what happens at home matter much more than the number of hours."
  - "Grandparent care has strengths too, like rich conversation and deep love. Every arrangement has trade-offs."
  - "If you can, settle in gradually: stay with your child for the first few visits, then try short separations and build up over two to three weeks."
  - "Always say goodbye, even if it brings tears. Keep it short and the same each time, and say when you'll be back ('after snack'). Sneaking out can make the next goodbye harder."
  - "Tears at drop-off are normal and often a sign of a healthy bond. What matters is whether your child can be comforted and settles to play afterwards."
- **Visit questions:**
  - How many children per adult?
  - Who will be my child's key person?
  - How long have staff been here?
  - Can I stay during settling-in?
  - What does a day look like (play, outdoors, naps)?
- **Avoid:** "Daycare before 2 harms children"; ratio numbers stated as law.

**Guide E: "Video games: the good, the risky, and setting limits" (ages 6–12)**
- **Sections:**
  1. What research says (wellbeing, violence, cognition).
  2. Age ratings: PEGI, ESRB, Iran's ESRA, and what they *don't* cover.
  3. Chat and strangers.
  4. Loot boxes and spending.
  5. Play together.
  6. Limits that work.
  7. When gaming is a problem.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Large studies find little link between how long people play and how happy they are. *Why* and *how* children play, and what gaming replaces, matters more."
  - "There is no good evidence that video games make children violent. Researchers still disagree about small effects on short-term aggression, so age ratings are worth following."
  - "Ratings check content, not who your child might meet. Switch off or limit chat with strangers, and make sure your child knows to tell you if anyone asks for photos, secrets or meet-ups."
  - "Loot boxes and 'mystery' packs work a lot like gambling. Turn off purchases or require your approval."
  - "Gaming disorder is real but rare. It means gaming has taken over and damaged sleep, school, family or friendships for a long time (usually a year or more), despite attempts to cut back."

**Guide F: "Healthy eyes: outdoor time, screens and eye checks" (ages 0–12)**
- **Sections:**
  1. Why outdoor time matters.
  2. Near work and screens: what we know.
  3. Breaks.
  4. Eye checks by age (Iran 3–6 screening).
  5. Signs to watch.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Time outdoors in daylight is the best-proven habit for delaying short-sightedness. Eye experts suggest about two hours a day. In one large school trial, an extra 40 minutes of outdoor time a day modestly reduced new cases."
  - "Lots of close-up work (reading, homework, screens) is linked to short-sightedness, but researchers aren't sure screens are worse than books. Regular breaks to look into the distance are a sensible, harmless habit."
  - "Make sure your child has the free vision screening between ages 3 and 6 (in Iran, often through the kindergarten). Lazy eye is easiest to treat when found early."
  - "Short-sightedness is less common in Iranian children than in East Asia, but refractive problems are rising."
  - Avoid "screens ruin eyes".

**Short section, possibly inside Guide A: "How many activities is too many?" (ages 6–12)**
- **Safe wording:** "Most children benefit from one or two organised activities they enjoy. Research finds over-scheduling is less common than feared. Watch your child rather than the calendar: are they sleeping enough, still playing freely, and still enjoying it?"

### Red flags: when to talk to the teacher or doctor
- **Homework / school:**
  - homework regularly takes far longer than the teacher expects
  - nightly tears, stomach aches or headaches before school
  - refusal to go to school
  - a sudden drop in grades or mood.

  → Teacher first; doctor if there are physical symptoms or low mood. [inference plus EEF]
- **Reading** → teacher or school counsellor, and check hearing and vision (NHS, IDA):
  - still not linking letters to sounds by the end of grade 1
  - reading much slower or less accurately than classmates, despite practice
  - family history of dyslexia
  - persistent trouble with rhyme or sound blending
  - avoids reading, or very poor spelling by grade 2–3.
- **Maths:** persistent difficulty with number sense (comparing quantities, counting on) despite support → teacher. [recall: dyscalculia; not researched here]
- **Childcare:**
  - not settling after several weeks
  - new persistent regression (sleep, toileting, speech)
  - fear of a particular carer
  - unexplained injuries.

  → Speak to the setting; doctor if concerned. Separation anxiety that is still intense daily after the preschool years → paediatrician (AAP).
- **Games:**
  - gaming crowding out sleep, school, friends or meals for months
  - rage or distress when stopped
  - lying about play or spending
  - contact from unknown adults.

  → Doctor or counsellor; report contact from unknown adults to the platform and police.
- **Eyes** → optometrist or eye doctor. Missed the 3–6 screening → arrange a check (NHS, AAP):
  - eyes not aligned (a squint)
  - squinting at distance or sitting very close to the TV
  - headaches, eye-rubbing
  - holding books very close
  - clumsiness
  - a white or odd reflection in photos. [recall: leukocoria; urgent]
