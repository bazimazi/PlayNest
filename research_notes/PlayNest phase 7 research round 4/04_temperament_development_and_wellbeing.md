## 1. Temperament

### 1.1 Key findings

**Thomas & Chess, New York Longitudinal Study (NYLS, from 1956)** [search; consistent across textbook sources]
- They rated infants on nine dimensions, including activity, rhythmicity, approach/withdrawal, adaptability, intensity, mood, persistence, distractibility and threshold.
- About 65% of children fitted one of three clusters:
  - **easy:** about 40%
  - **difficult:** about 10%
  - **slow-to-warm-up:** about 15%
- About 35% did not fit any cluster.
- Sample sizes differ between secondary sources (133–141 middle-class children, plus a Puerto Rican working-class sample of about 95). Do not quote a number.
- **"Goodness of fit"** is the NYLS's lasting contribution. Outcomes depend on the match between a child's temperament and the demands and expectations of the environment, not on temperament alone. Chess & Thomas made this match the focus of their parent guidance. [search: AJP 1998 review abstract and textbook summaries]

**Rothbart's model** [search: CBQ literature]
The modern research standard measures temperament as three broad dimensions, using the Children's Behavior Questionnaire (CBQ, parent report, ages 3–7):
- **Surgency/extraversion:** activity, high-intensity pleasure, impulsivity, and low shyness.
- **Negative affectivity:** anger/frustration, discomfort, sadness, fear, and low soothability.
- **Effortful control:** attention focusing, inhibitory control, low-intensity pleasure, and perceptual sensitivity.

Effortful control is the temperament side of self-regulation and overlaps with executive function. This framing is more useful for an app than "easy/difficult" because it is dimensional: every child sits somewhere on each dimension.

**Heritability: moderate, and not destiny**
- Twin-study estimates for childhood temperament range from about .20 to .80 depending on trait, age and measure (Saudino 2005, cited in a review). [search]
- One twin study (Goldsmith et al., 1997, cited in a review) gave about .58 for effortful control, .42 for negative affectivity and .41 for surgency. [search]
- The Wisconsin Twin Project found heritability itself varies with the home environment. For example, effortful control and surgency were more heritable in chaotic homes. [search]
- Safe phrasing: "partly inherited, partly shaped by experience".

**Stability: moderate**
- Behavioural inhibition (BI) shows moderate stability from toddlerhood onwards. Correlations across sessions range from about r = .24 to .64, and stability is higher over short gaps (1 year) than long ones (7+ years). [search: review snippet]
- In Kagan's cohort:
  - About 15–20% of 4-month-olds were classed as "high-reactive". Sources differ: 15% or 20%. [search]
  - At about 4.5 years, 46% of high-reactive infants were classed as inhibited, versus 10% of low-reactive infants. [search; Fox/Kagan secondary sources]
- In other words, most high-reactive babies were *not* classed as inhibited by 4.5. Temperament is a tendency, not a fixed fate.

**Differential susceptibility ("for better and for worse")**
- **The theory.** Belsky & Pluess (2009, *Psychological Bulletin* 135(6)) argued that some children, often those with more negative emotionality in infancy, are more affected by their environment *in both directions*: worse in harsh settings, *better* in supportive ones. [search]
- **The meta-analysis.** Slagt et al. (2016, *Psychological Bulletin* 142(10)) gave partial support. [search: abstract]
  - "Difficult" temperament predicted more vulnerability to negative parenting *and* more benefit from positive parenting.
  - For negative emotionality, this held only when the trait was measured in infancy.
  - Surgency and effortful control gave little support.
  - Interactions were stronger with observed than with questionnaire-measured parenting.
  - A later review notes effects are modest and some later studies did not replicate. [search]
- **Why it matters for the app:** it gives a hopeful, evidence-based reframe for parents of intense or reactive children. "Children who react strongly often also respond strongly to warmth and calm." Present it as "research suggests", not as a law.

**Shy and behaviourally inhibited children: gentle exposure, not overprotection**
- **Rapee et al. (2005, *JCCP* 73(3)).** [search: abstract]
  - Randomised trial with inhibited preschoolers.
  - A 6-session parent education group led to fewer anxiety diagnoses at 12 months than no intervention.
  - Temperament itself did not change.
  - From recall [unverified]: the programme content was reducing parental overprotection, encouraging gradual step-by-step exposure, and parent anxiety management.
- **Rapee (2013) follow-up into middle adolescence.** n = 146. [search: abstract]
  - Girls whose parents attended had fewer internalising disorders and fewer mother-reported anxiety symptoms.
  - Boys showed few differences.
- **Cool Little Kids population trial, Melbourne (Bayer et al.).** [opened: ACAMH Research Digest, updated 19 Jun 2026]
  - 545 parents of inhibited 4-year-olds, identified by universal preschool screening.
  - Six 90-minute groups led by psychologists.
  - At age 6, child anxiety *symptoms* were lower than controls.
  - There was no significant effect on broader internalising problems, anxiety disorders, parenting practices or parent distress.
  - Engagement was uneven: lower among younger mothers and lower-income families.
- **Mid-childhood follow-up of the same population cohort:** 57% (246/430) of inhibited preschoolers had clinical-level anxiety problems. Parent distress and parenting practices were the main predictors. [search] This is a high-risk *selected* group (screened as most inhibited), not ordinary shy children. Do not quote this figure to parents of shy children.
- **NHS anxiety page:** "avoid overprotectiveness, and don't push your child into a stressful situation without discussing it first." [opened: NHS, reviewed 5 Aug 2026] This is the balance PlayNest should teach: small steps, prepared in advance, with support.

**"Highly sensitive child" (Aron's sensory processing sensitivity, SPS)**
- **Lionetti et al. (2018, *Translational Psychiatry*).** [opened: PMC5802697] 906 adults on the Highly Sensitive Person scale.
  - Sensitivity looked like a continuous, normally distributed trait, with roughly three groups:
    - about 30% low
    - about 40% medium
    - about 29% high
  - It correlated r = 0.56 with neuroticism.
  - The authors call the cut-offs "mere approximations" and the samples were mostly undergraduates.
  - The paper does *not* frame sensitivity as a disorder.
- **Child version.** Pluess et al. (2018, *Developmental Psychology*) built a 12-item Highly Sensitive Child scale, tested from about age 10. It gave a similar three-way split. [search]
- **Heritability.** Assary et al. (2020, *Molecular Psychiatry*), with 2,868 adolescent twins: heritability about 0.47. The overlap with neuroticism and extraversion was largely genetic. [search: QMUL release]
- **Critiques.** [search]
  - Item overlap with Big Five scales.
  - A vague definition: it is unclear whether SPS is a behavioural style or an ability.
  - Some predictive effects disappear after controlling for neuroticism and openness.
- **Verdict:** "Some children are more sensitive to their surroundings" is reasonable and research-backed as a *dimension*. "Highly sensitive child" as a type, a diagnosis, or an identity label is not supported. Avoid the label in the app, describe behaviours instead, and do not offer a quiz that sorts children.

### 1.2 What parents can do (temperament)
- **Observe rather than label.** Notice how quickly the child warms up, how intense their reactions are, how active they are, and how they handle change. Use descriptions ("takes time with new people"), not identities ("she's shy", "he's difficult").
- **Goodness of fit.** Adjust the *situation*, not the child: more warning before transitions, more movement breaks, smaller gatherings, a quiet corner.
- **Slow-to-warm-up or inhibited children:**
  - Prepare them for what's coming.
  - Arrive early to new places.
  - Stay nearby at first, then step back a little at a time.
  - Praise brave small steps.
  - Do not speak for the child every time, and do not force them.
  - Avoid letting avoidance shrink their world: gentle exposure.
- **High-intensity or very active children:**
  - Give daily vigorous movement.
  - Use clear and short instructions.
  - Hold limits calmly. Their intensity does not mean the parent is failing.
  - Coach them in naming feelings.
- **Children who react strongly to noise, textures or crowds:**
  - Plan recovery time.
  - Warn them about sensory events.
  - Validate the reaction without making a story about fragility.
  - If sensory reactions are extreme and limit daily life, ask a professional. This may relate to other developmental differences.
- **Look after your own reaction.** A child's temperament affects the parent too, and that is not a moral failing. Fit works in both directions.

### 1.3 Evidence strength
- Temperament dimensions exist, are partly heritable and moderately stable: **strong**.
- Goodness of fit as a parenting principle: **moderate** (long clinical tradition, mostly observational).
- Differential susceptibility: **moderate, contested in detail**.
- Brief parent programmes for inhibited preschoolers reduce later anxiety: **moderate** (several RCTs; benefits partial and sometimes sex-specific).
- SPS as a distinct trait separate from neuroticism: **weak–contested**.

## 2. What to expect at this age (ages 1–12)

### 2.1 Key findings

**ZERO TO THREE / Bezos Family Foundation, "Tuning In" National Parent Survey** [opened: overview PDF, © 2016]
- Survey details: 2,200 US parents of children aged 0–5, October 2015, online, margin of error ±2.1%.
- **The expectation gap. Self-control:** 36% of parents said children under 2 have enough impulse control to resist doing something forbidden, and 56% said this comes before age 3. ZERO TO THREE: "most children are not able to master this until between 3.5 to 4 years of age."
- **The expectation gap. Sharing:** 43% think children can share and take turns before age 2, and 71% before age 3. "In fact, this skill develops between 3 to 4 years."
- **Parents want this information:** 47% want to learn when self-control develops, and 42% want to know what skills to expect at different ages.
- **Discipline:** 57% struggle to find the most effective way to discipline. 42% don't want to yell as quickly as they do.
- **Feeling judged:** 90% of mothers and 85% of fathers feel judged.

This is a strong justification for a "what to expect" feature.
- A search snippet adds that 24% of parents think children can control their emotions at 1 or younger, and 42% at age 2. [search; not seen in the PDF text I extracted]
- ZERO TO THREE's toddler self-control guide for ages 24–36 months [opened: zerotothree.org, dated 19 May 2025]:
  - Older toddlers are "making great strides … but still have a ways to go to learn impulse control".
  - "Time-outs are fairly ineffective with children aged 2 and 3"; it suggests calm-down breaks or closeness instead.
  - Practise waiting with games, timers and visual countdowns.

**Executive function by age (Harvard Center on the Developing Child)** [opened: InBrief "Executive Function: Skills for Life and Learning", PDF text]
- Executive function skills "begin to develop shortly after birth, with ages 3 to 5 a window of opportunity for dramatic growth". Development "continues throughout adolescence and early adulthood".
- The InBrief says adults support EF development by:
  - supporting, modelling and practising the skills
  - providing a reliable presence
  - giving creative play and vigorous exercise
  - increasing complexity step by step.
- The Activities Guide (published 6 May 2014) has age chapters: 6–18 months, 18–36 months, 3–5, 5–7, 7–12, and adolescents. [opened: landing page only]

**Lying is normal and emerges around 2–4**
- **Evans & Lee (2013, *Developmental Psychology*)** [opened: PMC3788848]
  - 65 children aged 2–3 (41 two-year-olds, 24 three-year-olds); 52 peeked at a forbidden toy, and 21 of those lied.
  - About a quarter of 2-year-old peekers lied, versus the majority of 3-year-olds. In the 43–48-month quartile, 9/10 lied.
  - Controlling for age, children with *better* executive function were more likely to lie.
  - The authors suggest younger children may be less *able* to lie, not more moral.
- **Lee's review ("Little Liars"):** after 4–5 years most children will lie in such tasks, and this continues into mid-childhood. [search]

**Physical aggression peaks in toddlerhood (Tremblay)** [search]
- Large longitudinal samples put the peak frequency of physical aggression (hitting, biting, kicking) between the end of the second and the end of the fourth year, then a decline for most children.
- Some summaries say "around age 2" or "2–3". Use "around 2–3 years, then usually declining".
- There is heterogeneity: a small group stays high, and these children are the ones to get help for.

**Tantrums**
- **Potegal & Davidson (2003, *J Dev Behav Pediatr*)** [search: abstract and secondary sources]
  - Parent narratives on 335 children aged 18–60 months.
  - The most common (modal) duration was 0.5–1 minute, and 75% of tantrums lasted 5 minutes or less.
  - Secondary summaries report tantrum prevalence of about 87% at 18–24 months, 91% at 30–36 months and 59% at 42–48 months. Verify against the original before quoting.
- **Wakschlag et al. (2012, *JCPP*, "Defining the developmental parameters of temper loss in early childhood")** [opened: Northwestern/Feinberg press release 29 Aug 2012; search: abstract and coverage]
  - About 1,500 preschoolers aged 3–5, replicated in about 2,200.
  - About 84% had tantrums sometimes, but under 10% (8.6%) had them daily, across sex, income and ethnicity.
  - Typical: tantrums when tired or frustrated, or during routines (bedtime, meals, dressing).
  - Atypical when regular: tantrums "out of the blue", or so intense or long that the child is exhausted or struggles to recover.
  - Wakschlag: "There's been a real danger of preschool children with normal misbehavior being mislabeled."

**Separation anxiety and normal fears**
- **NHS anxiety in children page** [opened: nhs.uk, reviewed 5 Aug 2026]
  - Separation anxiety is common from about 6 months to 3 years and "should stop around age 2 to 3".
  - Common preschool fears are animals, insects, storms, heights, water, blood and the dark, and usually fade.
  - School-age worries are new school, tests, and shyness.
- **Gullone (2000, *Clinical Psychology Review* 20:429–451), "The development of normal fear: a century of research"** [search]
  - Fears become less frequent and less intense with age.
  - Content shifts from concrete or immediate (strangers, noises, separation) to imaginary (dark, monsters), then to anticipatory and social (school, health, death, social evaluation).
  - Fears of the dark and of animals and imaginary creatures decline with age.
- **Muris et al. (2000, *J Clin Child Psychol*)** [search: abstract]
  - 190 children aged 4–12: 75.8% reported fears, 67.4% worries and 80.5% scary dreams.
  - Fears and scary dreams peaked at 7–9, while worry was clearly more common from 7–12.
  - Normal children have fears. The app should say so.

**Other typical ages used in the tables**
- **Toilet training (AAP HealthyChildren.org, updated 24 May 2022)** [opened]
  - Signs of bladder and bowel control appear between 18 and 24 months.
  - Training typically starts between 2 and 3.
  - Most US children are trained by about 4.
- **Bedwetting (NHS, reviewed 11 Nov 2026)** [opened]
  - Bedwetting is common and "many children under 5" wet the bed.
  - See a GP if it persists after trying home measures, or if a child starts wetting again after more than 6 months dry.
- **Sleep: WHO 2019 under-5 guidelines** [search]
  - 1–2 years: 11–14 hours including naps.
  - 3–4 years: 10–13 hours.
  - No sedentary screen time under 2. Some summaries say not recommended at age 1, with up to 1 hour at age 2; check the WHO PDF.
  - Ages 2–4: no more than 1 hour, "less is better".
- **Sleep: AASM 2016 consensus** [search confirmed the statement; school-age figures recall, unverified]
  - 3–5 years: 10–13 hours.
  - 6–12 years: 9–12 hours.
- **Picky eating and food neophobia** [search]
  - Typically peaks at about 2–6 years.
  - Repeated exposure and modelling increase acceptance; this is RCT-supported.
  - One source cites "6–14 tries"; I found no primary source for the common "8–15 times". Say "many tries — often more than 10".
- **Sibling conflict** [search: Kramer 2010 citing Perlman & Ross; figures differ between secondary sources]
  - Young siblings (about 2–4 years) may have disputes several times an hour; one source says 7.65 per hour, each about 45 seconds.
  - Use "frequent squabbles are very common in young siblings", without a number.
- **Self-esteem in early childhood** [recall, unverified: Harter]
  - Young children's self-evaluations are typically very positive.
  - They become more realistic and comparison-based around 7–8. That makes 7–10 a typical age for "I'm bad at…" statements and peer comparison.
- **Language milestones** [recall, unverified: CDC/AAP 2022 revision, Zubler et al., *Pediatrics* 2022]
  - The 2022 milestones use a 75% criterion: what most children do by that age.
  - Examples:
    - about 2 years: at least two words together
    - about 30 months: about 50 words
    - about 3 years: conversation with back-and-forth exchanges
    - about 4 years: says sentences of four or more words.
  - Verify on cdc.gov/actearly before quoting; the URL I tried returned 404.

### 2.2 Per-topic "what's typical / worth asking about" tables

House notes for these tables:
- They are *ranges*, not checklists.
- Each "worth asking about" item is phrased as a reason to talk to a doctor, nurse or teacher, *not* as a sign of a disorder.
- "Worth asking about" always also includes "anything that worries you, or a skill your child had and lost". Loss of skills is a standard developmental red flag [recall, unverified: CDC Act Early].

**Closeness / connection**
| Age | Typical | Worth asking about |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Clingy with strangers; protests separation; checks back to parent while exploring | Rarely seeks comfort or shows things to you; little eye contact or response to name |
| 2–3 | Separation protest usually easing by about 3; wants "me do it" yet needs you close | Separation distress that is extreme and not easing after 3 |
| 3–5 | Enjoys special one-to-one time; may prefer one parent for a while | Persistent withdrawal from all adults; no interest in play with you |
| 5–8 | Shares stories at bedtime more than at pickup; wants you at events | Sudden change: secretive, fearful of a person or place |
| 8–12 | More privacy, friends matter more; still needs regular one-to-one time | Withdrawing from the family *and* friends, or low mood most days for 2+ weeks |

**Cooperation / listening**
| Age | Typical | Worth asking about |
|---|---|---|

| 5–8 | Can follow routines with reminders; argues about fairness | Constant defiance across settings that's harming school or friendships |

### 2.3 Evidence strength
- Expectation gap exists (ZTT survey): **moderate**. It is a single, large, US online survey.
- EF growth spurt at 3–5 with long maturation: **strong**.
- Lying emerging at 2–4 and linked to cognitive development: **strong** (replicated lab paradigm).
- Aggression peak in toddlerhood: **strong** (large longitudinal cohorts).
- Normal vs atypical tantrum features: **moderate** (large parent-report samples, one research group).
- The per-age table entries are syntheses. They are guides, not norms.

## 3. Positive childhood experiences (PCEs) and the HOPE framework

### 3.1 Key findings

**Bethell, Jones, Gombojav, Linkenbach & Sege (2019), *JAMA Pediatrics* 173(11):e193007** [opened: jamanetwork.com]

Sample: the 2015 Wisconsin Behavioral Risk Factor Survey, n = 6,188 adults, phone survey, weighted.

The seven PCEs, as items "before age 18":
1. Felt able to talk to their family about feelings.
2. Felt their family stood by them during difficult times.
3. Enjoyed participating in community traditions.
4. Felt a sense of belonging in high school.
5. Felt supported by friends.
6. Had at least 2 non-parent adults who took genuine interest in them.
7. Felt safe and protected by an adult in their home.

**Dose-response: depression and/or poor mental health** (lifetime diagnosis or 14+ poor mental-health days in the past 30)
| PCEs | Prevalence | Adjusted OR |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | 48.2% | reference |
| 3–5 | 25.1% | 0.50 (95% CI 0.36–0.69) |
| 6–7 | 12.6% | 0.28 (95% CI 0.21–0.39) |

**Dose-response: "always" getting needed social and emotional support as an adult**
| PCEs | Prevalence | Adjusted OR |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | 33.0% | reference |
| 3–5 | 43.6% | 1.31, not significant |
| 6–7 | 67.9% | 3.53 (95% CI 2.60–4.80) |

- The associations held at each level of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs).
- **Authors' limitations:**
  - cross-sectional, so not causal
  - retrospective self-report
  - a Wisconsin sample, mostly White
  - only relational PCEs were measured
  - they could not test whether depressed adults recall childhood differently.

**HOPE (Healthy Outcomes from Positive Experiences)** [opened: positiveexperience.org, HOPE National Resource Center, Tufts Medical Center]

The four building blocks, as the site words them:
- **Relationships:** "Safe and supportive relationships within the family and with other children and adults."
- **Environment:** "Safe, equitable, and stable environments where children can live, learn, and play."
- **Engagement:** "Opportunities for social and civic engagement to develop a sense of belonging and connectedness."
- **Emotional growth:** "Opportunities for emotional growth where children feel supported through difficult events and emotions."

The site claims PCEs "can reduce the lifelong effects of ACEs". It gives no outcome data on the page; this is the organisation's own framing. Sege & Bethell received the 2022 Richard Krugman award. [search]

**Why the app should frame protective factors, not ACE scores**
Anda, Porter & Brown (2020), *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, "Inside the Adverse Childhood Experience Score: Strengths, Limitations, and Misapplications". [search: secondary summaries; journal page 403]
- Anda co-led the original ACE Study.
- **Their argument:**
  - The ACE score is a crude, unstandardised measure of cumulative childhood stress.
  - It was designed for population research, not individual screening.
  - It has no clinical cut-points (unlike blood pressure).
  - Applying population risk estimates to individuals is a misapplication.
  - Screening programmes using ACE scores should face the same evidence review as other screening (USPSTF standard).
- **Implication:** never show parents an "ACE score" for their child or themselves, and never imply that a number predicts a child's future.

### 3.2 What parents can do
Translate the 7 PCEs and HOPE into everyday, doable actions. All of them are things PlayNest already supports.
- **Talk about feelings.** Use feelings words and a daily check-in ("What was the best and hardest part of today?").
- **Stand by them in hard times.** "We'll get through this together"; repair after conflict.
- **Family and community traditions.** Nowruz rituals, Yalda night, weekly family dinners, religious or cultural gatherings, a family game night.
- **Belonging at school.** Know the teacher's name, encourage a club or team, help the child connect with one friend.
- **Friends.** Make time for play dates and unstructured play with peers.
- **Two other caring adults.** Grandparents, aunts and uncles, a teacher, a coach. In Iranian families this is often already rich (extended family). Present it as a strength.
- **Safe and protected at home.** Calm, predictable routines. No violence or humiliation at home.

### 3.3 Evidence strength
- PCEs are associated with better adult mental health in a dose-response pattern: **moderate**. Replicated in some other samples [recall, unverified: e.g., the related "benevolent childhood experiences" work by Narayan et al., 2018], but retrospective and cross-sectional.
- Building PCEs *causes* better outcomes: **not established**, though it is consistent with the relationship and resilience literature (round 3, §1).
- ACE-based individual screening: **contested and cautioned against by the ACE authors themselves**.

## 4. Children's mental health (5–12): when to worry

### 4.1 Key findings

**How common**
- **US (CDC, National Survey of Children's Health 2023–24, ages 3–17, current diagnosis):** [opened: cdc.gov, updated 20 Aug 2026]
  - anxiety 11%
  - depression 4%
  - behaviour disorders 8%
- **Iran (IRCAP national survey, Mohammadi et al., *Iranian Journal of Psychiatry* 2019; 14(1):1–15):** [opened: abstract on publish.kne-publishing.com]
  - 30,532 children aged 6–18 from all provinces, assessed in person with the Persian K-SADS-PL.
  - 22.31% had at least one psychiatric disorder.
  - Anxiety disorders were the most common at 14.13%; behavioural disorders were 8.3%.
  - A parent's mental disorder was linked to higher odds: mother OR 1.96, father OR 1.33.
  - Use carefully, but it shows that children's anxiety in Iran is common and worth talking about without stigma.
- **RCPsych (Jan 2025):** about 2 in 100 children and young people experience depression; it is more common in older adolescents. [opened]

**What depression looks like in children (it is often not "sadness")**
- **NHS** [opened: nhs.uk "Children: depression signs", reviewed 16 May 2023; review was due May 2026, so check the current version]
  - ongoing low mood or *irritability/grumpiness most of the time*
  - loss of interest in things they used to enjoy
  - tiredness
  - sleep and appetite changes
  - poor concentration
  - pulling away from friends and family
  - feelings of guilt or worthlessness
  - physical complaints (headaches, stomach aches)
  - school or behaviour problems
  - thoughts of suicide or self-harm.
- **AACAP Facts for Families "The Depressed Child"** [opened: aacap.org, updated Oct 2018]
  - Similar list.
  - "Children who say they want to hurt themselves, be dead, or kill themselves should be taken seriously."
  - Seek help if sadness, irritability or loss of interest occurs "day after day".
- **Child Mind Institute, "Depression at Different Ages"** [opened: childmind.org, updated 5 Jun 2025]
  - Look for *a change from the child's usual self*.
  - unusual irritability even without sadness
  - sudden new stomach aches or headaches
  - crying over mistakes they used to shrug off
  - asking to quit activities
  - frequent "I can't / I don't want to".
- **Duration rule of thumb:** "most days for more than about two weeks" appears in several secondary sources [search]. The NHS and RCPsych note that symptoms must persist and affect daily life. RCPsych says a GP may "wait and watch" for a couple of weeks. [opened]

**Anxiety signs** [opened: NHS, reviewed 5 Aug 2026]
- **Younger children:** irritability, tearfulness, clinginess, sleep problems, night waking, bedwetting, bad dreams.
- **Older children:** low confidence, poor concentration, sleep or eating problems, angry outbursts, persistent negative thoughts, avoiding friends, outings or school.
- **When to get help:** anxiety that is severe, persistent, or interfering with everyday life. Start with the GP; involve the school.

**School refusal** [opened: Child Mind Institute, Rachel Busman PsyD, updated 4 Feb 2025]
- It is not a diagnosis. It often comes with separation anxiety, social anxiety or depression, or with bullying or school problems.
- Signs: frequent unexplained physical complaints, lateness, leaving early, visits to the school nurse, texting parents all day.
- **What parents should do:**
  - Have a doctor rule out medical causes.
  - Return to school promptly after short absences.
  - Avoid letting morning battles end in staying home, since each missed day makes return harder.
- **When to seek professional help:** avoidance lasting many days or weeks, or partial attendance.
- CBT with gradual return is the usual treatment.

**Suicidal thoughts can occur under 12**
- **ABCD Study (US, 9–10-year-olds, about 11,875 children):** lifetime suicidal ideation was 14.33% and attempts 1.26% (Lawrence et al., 2021, *Translational Psychiatry*). [opened: figures cited in Spencer 2022, *Biological Psychiatry*, PMC9873473; primary paper not opened]
  - Most ideation is passive or fleeting.
  - Child and parent reports agree poorly. Parents often don't know.
- **Implication for the app:** say calmly that children sometimes have these thoughts, that it is OK to ask, and how to respond. Do not cite the 14% figure to parents; it would alarm more than help.

**Asking directly is safe**
- **Dazzi, Gribble, Wessely & Fear (2014), *Psychological Medicine*.** [search: abstract]
  - 13 studies (2001–2013), none of which found a significant increase in suicidal ideation from asking.
  - Asking may reduce distress in some groups.
  - Caveat: the studies measured ideation, not deaths, and were mostly adolescents and adults.
- **Later meta-analyses (2018):** no iatrogenic effect of assessing suicidality (DeCou & Schumann, *Suicide Life Threat Behav*), and small *reductions* in ideation after exposure to suicide-related questions (Blades et al., g = −0.13). [search]

**Wording guidance**
- **AFSP (American Foundation for Suicide Prevention)** [opened: afsp.org "Teens and Suicide: What Parents Should Know", undated]
  - Start with "Are you okay?"
  - Then ask directly: "Does it ever get so tough that you think about ending your life?"
  - If yes: stay calm, keep listening ("Can you say more about that?"), say you will support them no matter what, and get professional help right away.
  - For ages 8–12, use age-appropriate language. Watch for stomach aches and more upset or anger. If they seem hopeless, trapped or overwhelmed, ask whether they ever think about hurting themselves or ending their life.
- **Other children's hospitals' guidance** (CHOC, Children's Health, Seattle Children's): ask calmly and directly; "when in doubt, it's better to ask"; if the child says yes, ask whether they have a plan and remove access to medicines and other means; a plan or means calls for urgent help. [search]

**Iran: where to get help** (all [search], from secondary sources; behzisti.ir opened but the numbers were not visible)
- **123, اورژانس اجتماعی (Behzisti social emergency)**
  - 24/7 and free.
  - Covers domestic violence, child abuse, running away, and threat of suicide.
  - Staff can attend in person.
  - Sources: TherapyRoute Iran crisis-lines article (says it was checked Aug 2026, citing Behzisti pages), and Iranian news reports.
- **1480, صدای مشاور (Behzisti counselling line)**
  - Free and confidential telephone counselling by psychologists and counsellors.
  - This is a counselling line, not an emergency line; suicide and abuse cases are routed to 123.
  - **Hours conflict between sources:** 8:00–24:00, 8:00–20:00, 8:00–12:00 (UNHCR), and 24/7 during national crises.
  - The app should say "check hours" or omit them.
- **4030, Ministry of Health line (سامانه ۴۰۳۰)**
  - Free psychological counselling. Press the "8" option for a psychologist.
  - Started during COVID; a Zoomit article dated Esfand 1404 (about Feb–Mar 2026) says it is supervised by the Ministry of Health and runs around the clock. [search]
- **115 (ambulance/medical emergency)** and **110 (police)** for immediate danger.
- **Iran Crisis Line "طعم گیلاس"** is an NGO text chat.
- **Rah-Ava**, Roozbeh Hospital, Tehran (021-54467000).
- Both are [search: TherapyRoute only]; do not include them until verified.
- **School counsellor (مشاور مدرسه):** Iranian schools under the Ministry of Education have counsellor posts [recall, unverified: coverage is uneven, especially in primary schools]. Phrase it as "if your child's school has a counsellor (مشاور مدرسه), they can be a good first contact".
- **Health centres (مراکز خدمات جامع سلامت):** these reportedly have mental health experts (کارشناس سلامت روان) as part of primary care [search: COVID-era news; recall, unverified]. They are a reasonable referral path alongside paediatricians and child psychiatrists (روان‌پزشک کودک و نوجوان) and child psychologists (روان‌شناس کودک).
- **Before release, a human should call or check behzisti.ir and the Ministry of Health pages** to confirm 123, 1480 and 4030.

### 4.2 What parents can do
- **Notice change, not single bad days.** One grumpy week after a move or exam is normal. Ongoing change for two weeks or more, across home and school, is worth a conversation.
- **Talk and listen.** "I've noticed you seem [tired/upset] lately. I'm not cross — I'd like to understand."
- **Keep the basics steady:** sleep, food, movement, time outdoors, time with you.
- **Don't remove all challenges.** For worries, use gentle step-by-step facing with support, not avoidance (NHS; Cool Little Kids).
- **Involve the school.** Teachers often see a different side of the child.
- **Look after yourself.** A parent's own mental health matters, and IRCAP found parental illness linked to child disorder. Point to PlayNest's burnout and postnatal depression guides.

### 4.3 Evidence strength
- Symptom lists (NHS/AACAP/CMI): **expert consensus**, consistent across bodies.
- Asking about suicide does not increase risk: **moderate–strong** (multiple reviews and meta-analyses, mostly adolescents and adults).
- Preadolescent ideation prevalence: **moderate** (one large cohort plus meta-analytic estimates; definitions vary).

## 5. Body image and food talk in families (5–12)

### 5.1 Key findings

**Project EAT: Berge et al. (2018), *Pediatrics* 141(4):e20172955** [opened: PMC5869334]
- 556 people surveyed as teenagers (1998–99, mean age 15) and again as parents (2015–16, mean age 31).
- Those whose parents encouraged them to diet as teens had higher adjusted prevalence 15 years later of:
  - overweight: PR 1.25 (95% CI 1.10–1.41)
  - obesity: PR 1.37 (1.06–1.76)
  - dieting: PR 1.20 (1.04–1.37)
  - binge eating: PR 1.72 (1.08–2.75)
  - more unhealthy weight-control behaviours
  - lower body satisfaction.
- **Intergenerational pattern.** As parents, they were more likely to:
  - encourage their own child to diet: PR 1.52 (1.12–2.05)
  - talk to the child about the child's weight: PR 1.49 (1.15–1.93)
  - complain about their own weight: PR 1.46.
- **Limitations:**
  - observational
  - self-reported BMI
  - single-item exposure
  - one region
  - 66% retention.
- Note that dieting talk did *not* prevent overweight; it went with more of it.

**Berge et al. (2013), *JAMA Pediatrics*, cross-sectional** [search]
- About 2,800 adolescents and 3,700 parents.
- Weight-focused parent conversations were associated with more dieting, unhealthy weight control and binge eating.
- Conversations focused only on healthy eating were associated with less, including among adolescents with higher weight.

**AAP clinical report (Golden, Schneider, Wood 2016), "Preventing Obesity and Eating Disorders in Adolescents", *Pediatrics* 138(3):e20161649** [search: Stanford and AJMC summaries; the AAP site returned 403]
- **Avoid:**
  - encouraging dieting
  - "weight talk" about the parent's own body or the child's
  - weight teasing.
- **Promote:**
  - regular family meals
  - healthy body image
  - activity for fitness, not weight.
- Lead author: mothers' talk about their own bodies can inadvertently promote dissatisfaction in children.
- Written for adolescents, but the principles are widely applied to younger children [recall].

**Body dissatisfaction starts young** [search]
- **Lowes & Tiggemann (2003), UK, 135 children aged 5–8:** girls aged 6–8 chose a thinner ideal figure than their current figure; 5-year-old girls and boys did not. The desire for thinness emerges in girls around 6.
- Other small studies report up to about 40–50% of girls aged 6–7 wanting to be thinner. Samples are small and use figure-rating scales, so do not quote them.
- Boys more often want to be *larger* or more muscular.

**Iranian context**
- **Garousi (2014), *International Journal of Preventive Medicine* 5(12):1587–93** [opened: PMC4336990]
  - 500 primary pupils in Kerman (242 girls, 248 boys), aged 6–14, mean 9.7.
  - 27.4% were underweight and 13.3% obese by BMI.
  - Girls were less satisfied with their bodies than boys.
  - Dissatisfaction was related to BMI in both sexes.
  - Thinner girls showed more anti-fat attitudes.
  - It was unrelated to parental education.
- **Eshraghian et al. (2018), Tehran** [search]
  - About 72% of children with obesity and 52% of non-obese children misperceived their body size.
  - The authors comment that many Iranian mothers lacked a good understanding of healthy child weight.
- **CASPIAN-IV national school survey (13,486 students, mean age about 12.5)** [search] examined body image and weight-change diets and called for prevention of chronic dieting.
- **I found no Iranian study** specifically on relatives' comments about children's bodies at family gatherings.
  - That such comments are common is plausible and culturally familiar (e.g., "چقدر لاغر شده!" or "تپل شده"), but it is unstudied as far as I could find.
  - It also matters that in Iran *underweight* is common (27% in Kerman), so "eat more" pressure and "too thin" comments may be as common as "too fat" ones.
  - Frame the guide for comments in *both* directions.

**Early eating-disorder warning signs** [opened: NHS eating disorders overview, reviewed 23 Jan 2024]
- Spending a lot of time worrying about weight and shape.
- Avoiding social eating.
- Eating very little or having strict food rules.
- Vomiting or laxatives.
- Mood changes.
- Feeling cold, tired or dizzy.
- Fainting.
- Weight very high or low for age, or delayed puberty.
- Expect denial or secrecy.
- **Advice:** share your concern and see the GP together.
- **ARFID** (avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder) can start in younger children and is different from ordinary picky eating: fear of choking or vomiting, very few foods, weight or growth affected [recall, unverified].

### 5.2 What parents can do
- **Talk about what bodies *do*,** not how they look: "strong legs for climbing".
- **Don't comment on anyone's weight** (yours, theirs, relatives'). Don't call foods "fattening" or "bad". Prefer "everyday foods" and "sometimes foods".
- **Share family meals** with a relaxed atmosphere. The parent decides *what/when/where*; the child decides *how much* (Satter's division of responsibility [recall, unverified as an evidence base; widely used by dietitians]).
- **Avoid pressure** to finish the plate, and avoid food as reward or punishment.
- **Prepare scripts for relatives** (see App takeaways). Back your child up afterwards.
- **If you're worried about weight in either direction,** see the doctor. Don't put the child on a diet yourself.

### 5.3 Evidence strength
- Parent weight talk and encouragement to diet are associated with worse outcomes: **moderate** (consistent longitudinal and cross-sectional data, observational).
- Family meals are protective: **moderate** (observational) [recall, unverified for specific effect sizes].
- Body dissatisfaction emerges at about 6 in girls: **moderate** (several small studies).
- Iranian child body-image data: **weak** (few, regional studies).

## 6. Boosters and maintenance: designing "check-ins after a goal"

### 6.1 Key findings

**Parenting programme effects usually hold, on average, but vary**
- **van Aar, Leijten, Orobio de Castro & Overbeek (2017), *Clinical Psychology Review* 51:153–163.** [search: abstract]
  - 40 RCTs with follow-up of up to 3 years.
  - The average change in disruptive-behaviour effects from post-intervention to follow-up was d = 0.01 (95% CI −0.05 to 0.07). Gains were *sustained* on average: no fade-out and no sleeper effect.
  - Heterogeneity was substantial. Some programmes faded or grew, and no moderator explained why.
- **Incredible Years follow-up (2021):** parent-reported gains were sustained at 2.5 years; teacher- and child-reported gains were not. [search]

**Which components matter: Kaminski et al. (2008), *J Abnormal Child Psychology*** [search: abstract]
- 77 evaluations of programmes for children aged 0–7; mean ES about 0.34 for child behaviour.
- Larger effects were linked to:
  - increasing positive parent–child interaction
  - emotional communication
  - time-out and consistency
  - parents **practising with their own child during sessions**.
- Teaching problem-solving and adding extra services were linked to smaller effects.
- Design rule: check-ins should prompt *practice*, not just re-reading.

**Booster sessions: mixed evidence**
- **Gearing et al. (2013), *Depression and Anxiety*:** youth CBT for anxiety and mood, 53 studies, 1,937 youth. [search: abstract]
  - Studies with boosters showed larger effects, but only marginally: pre-post B = 0.13, p < .10.
  - Pre to follow-up r = .64 (k = 10) with boosters, versus r = .48 (k = 20) without.
  - This is a between-study comparison and confounded.
- **A later meta-analysis of 106 youth CBTs from 76 RCTs** (*J Clin Child Adolesc Psychol*, 2018, doi 10.1080/15374416.2018.1443459) found boosters, goal-setting and relapse prevention were **not** significantly related to effect sizes. Parental involvement was. [search]
- **Kolko & Lindhiem (2014), introduction to a *J Abnormal Child Psychology* special series on booster sessions:** [opened: PMC4011174]
  - Four randomised studies of maintenance and boosters, three with randomised booster arms:
    - yearly Family Check-Up
    - PCIT monthly phone maintenance
    - Coping Power boosters
    - a Kolko booster trial
  - The series exists because the evidence was thin.
- **Eyberg, Boggs & Jaccard (2014), "Does maintenance treatment matter?"** [search: abstract]
  - 61 children who completed PCIT were randomised to monthly therapist phone check-ins or assessment-only for 2 years.
  - Maintenance-treatment families showed little change from post-treatment, as hoped.
  - But assessment-only families also largely maintained gains. The authors suggest that the follow-up assessments themselves may have reinforced maintenance.
  - **This is directly useful.** Even a light-touch "how's it going?" check-in may be part of what helps.
- **Tolan (2014) commentary:** maintenance and boosters should be planned as core intervention elements, not afterthoughts. [search]

**Habit formation: Lally et al. (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology*** [opened: BPS Research Digest; search for details]
- 96 adults chose one daily health behaviour (e.g., fruit with lunch, a run before dinner), each tied to a daily cue; 82 completed.
- Automaticity rose steeply at first, then plateaued.
- The commonly cited 66 days is a modelled figure from a subset of participants. Sources differ on whether it is a median or a mean. The range was 18 days to a predicted 254 days.
- About half the participants had not reached a high automaticity score by day 84.
- **Missing a single day did not materially affect habit formation.** Many missed repetitions lowered the plateau.
- Exercise habits took about 1.5 times longer than eating or drinking habits.
- **Design implications:**
  - "21 days to a habit" is a myth.
  - Parenting habits are complex behaviours and likely slower.
  - Missed days should be explicitly normalised.

### 6.2 Design rules for "check-in after finishing a goal" (evidence-informed, not evidence-proven)
1. **Timing.** Schedule light check-ins at about **1 week, 1 month and 3 months** after a goal is completed. Optionally add a 6-month one. Rationale:
   - habit automaticity takes weeks to months (Lally)
   - booster trials typically used monthly to yearly contact
   - assessment itself may sustain gains (Eyberg 2014).
   These intervals are a reasoned choice, not tested values.
2. **Few, short and skippable.** Use at most 3 questions, a one-tap answer, and an always-visible "not now".
3. **Ask about practice, not compliance.** For example:
   - "In the last week, how often did you use [skill]?" (most days / sometimes / not lately)
   - "What's been helping?"
   - "Want a 2-minute refresher?"
4. **Treat a lapse as normal information.** A "not lately" answer triggers a warm message and one practice tip, never a streak-reset or red warning. Lally showed single misses don't undo habits.
5. **Offer, don't push, a mini-booster.** Offer one key skill from the guide, framed as practice with your own child. This follows Kaminski's finding that in-session practice with the child is the active ingredient.
6. **Celebrate what has stuck.** Reflect gains back: "You told us bedtimes were calmer — that's a real change." No "you're slipping" language.
7. **Let parents end the check-ins.** Include a "this goal is done for us" option. The app must not create dependence.
8. **Escalation path.** If a check-in answer suggests things are getting much worse (e.g., more aggression, or the child seems very low), show the relevant "when to talk to a professional" card rather than another tip.

### 6.3 Evidence strength
- Parenting programme effects sustained on average: **moderate–strong** (meta-analysis, heterogeneous).
- Booster sessions add benefit: **weak / contested**.
- Practice with one's own child improves effects: **moderate** (component meta-analysis, non-randomised comparisons).
- Habit formation timeline and missed-day tolerance: **weak–moderate** (one small study, much over-quoted).

## Contested / overclaims to avoid

**Temperament and sensitivity**
- **"Difficult child."** It is a historical NYLS research category. Never use it as a label in the app.
- **"Highly sensitive child" as a type or diagnosis.** Sensitivity is a continuous, partly heritable trait that overlaps substantially with neuroticism. There is no clinical category.
- **"Orchid vs dandelion" children.** This is a metaphor; the cut-offs are "mere approximations".
- **"Temperament is fixed" or "shy babies become anxious adults".** Stability is only moderate, and most high-reactive infants were not inhibited by 4.5.
- **"Differential susceptibility proves sensitive kids thrive more with good parenting."** Support is partial and from modest effects.

**Development and expectations**
- **"Toddlers who hit or lie are developing badly."** Both are developmentally normal at 2–4.
- **"A child should be able to share by 2."** Sharing develops around 3–4 (ZTT).
- **"Tantrums mean bad parenting."** About 84% of preschoolers have them.
- **"Tantrums are always normal."** Daily, out-of-the-blue or exhausting tantrums are worth asking about (Wakschlag).
- **"X minutes of attention per year of age."** This folk formula has no source I could find. Do not use it.

**PCEs and ACEs**
- **"PCEs cancel out ACEs" or "6 PCEs protect your child from depression."** The data are associations, from retrospective adult self-report.
- **Any "ACE score" for a child or parent, or predictions from it.** Anda et al. 2020 explicitly caution against this.

**Mental health and safety**
- **"Asking about suicide puts the idea in their head."** The evidence says no (Dazzi 2014; 2018 meta-analyses).
- **"Young children can't be depressed or suicidal."** They can, although it is uncommon and often looks like irritability or physical complaints.

**Body image and food**
- **"Telling a child they're getting chubby motivates healthy eating."** Weight talk and diet encouragement go with *more* dieting, binge eating and later overweight (Project EAT).

**Habits and boosters**
- **"21 days to form a habit"** and **"66 days to form a habit"** as fixed numbers.
- **"Booster sessions are proven to maintain gains."** They are mixed, and the light-touch follow-up itself may be what helps.

## App takeaways

### A. Proposed guides

**Guide 1 — "Every child is wired a little differently" (temperament and fit)**
- Ages 0–12.
- **Sections:**
  1. What temperament is: three everyday dimensions: how *active and outgoing*, how *strongly they feel*, how easily they *focus and stop*.
  2. Goodness of fit: changing the situation, not the child.
  3. Children who take time to warm up.
  4. Children with big energy and big reactions.
  5. Children who notice everything (sensory sensitivity).
  6. Your temperament too.
  7. When to ask a professional.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Temperament is your child's natural style — partly inherited, partly shaped by experience. It's not good or bad, and it can soften and change as children grow."
  - "Instead of 'she's shy', try 'she likes to watch first'. Children tend to live up to the words we use about them."
  - "Children who feel things strongly often respond strongly to warmth and calm too. Research suggests your steady support may matter even more for them."
  - "Some children notice sounds, textures and moods more than others. That's a normal difference, not a problem to fix. Planning quiet time after busy events can help."
  - "Doing too much for a cautious child can keep fears going. Doing too little support can overwhelm them. The middle path: small steps, prepared in advance, with you nearby."

**Guide 2 — "Helping a cautious child be brave" (shy / slow-to-warm-up)**
- Ages 2–10.
- **Sections:**
  1. Cautious is normal.
  2. Gentle steps: a "brave ladder" with 4–6 small steps.
  3. What to say before, during, after.
  4. What to avoid: speaking for them every time, forcing them, labelling them in front of them.
  5. When shyness becomes worry that stops life.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Let's try one small step today. I'll be right here."
  - Afterwards: "You felt nervous and you did it anyway — that's what brave means."
  - To relatives: "She likes to warm up first. Give her a few minutes and she'll come to you."

**Guide 3 — "What to expect at this age" (realistic expectations)**
- Ages 1–12, with the per-goal tables from §2.2 surfaced inside each goal.
- **Sections:**
  1. Why expectations matter (the expectation gap).
  2. Self-control by age.
  3. Normal things that look like "bad behaviour": hitting at 2, lying at 3–4, tantrums, fears.
  4. "Worth asking about" signs.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Many parents expect self-control earlier than children's brains can manage. Most children can't reliably stop themselves from doing something forbidden until around 3½–4, and sharing develops around 3–4. If your toddler can't do it yet, it's not your failure or theirs."
  - "Early fibs around age 3–4 are a sign of a growing mind, not a bad character. Calm responses teach honesty better than punishment."
  - "Children grow at different speeds. These are typical ranges, not tests. If something worries you, or your child loses a skill they had, talk to your doctor or health centre."

**Guide 4 — "Small things that protect children for life" (positive childhood experiences)**
- Ages 0–12.
- **Sections:**
  1. What research found.
  2. The seven everyday experiences, translated.
  3. Family traditions and the extended family as strengths.
  4. Never too late.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "A large survey of adults found that those who remembered more of these experiences in childhood — feeling able to talk about feelings with family, feeling safe and protected at home, having other caring adults, belonging at school and in community traditions — reported better mental health as adults. This shows a link, not a guarantee, but these are things families can build every day."
  - "You don't need to do everything. Every caring adult, shared tradition and listening moment adds up."
  - **Do not include:** any scoring quiz, ACE checklist or prediction.

**Guide 5 — "Is my child OK? Worries, low mood and when to get help" (5–12)**
- **Sections:**
  1. Ups and downs are normal.
  2. Signs worth noticing: change from usual self, lasting about 2+ weeks, affecting home or school.
  3. How worry and low mood can look in children (irritability, tummy aches, sleep changes, avoiding school).
  4. How to start a conversation.
  5. Asking about safety.
  6. Where to get help in Iran and elsewhere.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Every child has sad, worried or cranky days. What's worth noticing is a change from your child's usual self that lasts more than a couple of weeks, or that gets in the way of school, friends or family life."
  - "In children, low mood often looks like irritability, tiredness, tummy aches or headaches, losing interest in favourite things, or saying 'I'm bad at everything'."
  - "If you're worried, it's okay — and safe — to ask directly. Research shows asking about thoughts of suicide does not put the idea in a child's head. You could say: 'Sometimes when kids feel really bad, they think about not wanting to be alive. Have you ever felt like that?'"
  - "If your child says yes: stay calm, thank them for telling you, listen, and say: 'I'm so glad you told me. We'll get help together.' Keep medicines and dangerous items out of reach, and contact a professional the same day."

**Guide 6 — "Food talk, body talk" (5–12)**
- **Sections:**
  1. Why words about weight matter.
  2. Talking about bodies by what they can do.
  3. Calm family meals.
  4. When relatives comment.
  5. Signs to check with a doctor.
- **Safe wording:**
  - "Research following children into adulthood found that being encouraged to diet as a teenager was linked to more dieting, binge eating and lower body satisfaction later — not to healthier weight. Talking about healthy habits for the whole family works better than talking about weight."

  - "If you're worried about your child's weight, too high or too low, talk to your doctor rather than starting a diet at home."

### B. Red flags: always send parents to a professional
**Urgent: same day, or emergency services**
- The child talks about wanting to die, killing themselves, or not wanting to be alive. This applies even if they are young, and even if it might be "attention".
- Self-harm.
- Signs of abuse.
- Fainting.
- Rapid weight loss.
- Refusing all food or drink.

**Soon: book with a doctor or psychologist**
- Low mood or irritability most days for 2+ weeks.
- Loss of interest in everything.
- Persistent school refusal.
- Daily or out-of-the-blue or exhausting tantrums after age 4.
- Aggression that is severe or not easing by 4–5.
- Worry that stops ordinary life.
- Any loss of skills.
- No words by about 18 months, or no pointing by about 15–18 months.
- New bedwetting after 6+ months dry.
- Persistent preoccupation with weight or shape, strict food rules, or very few accepted foods with weight concerns.
- Sudden thirst, frequent urination and weight loss. This is a medical emergency sign for diabetes [recall, unverified wording; standard paediatric advice].
- Fears or sleep problems with breathing pauses or loud snoring.

- at most 3 questions, one tap each
