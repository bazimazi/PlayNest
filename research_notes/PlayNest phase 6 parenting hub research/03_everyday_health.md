## 1. Fever

### 1.1 What counts as a fever

| Body | Threshold as stated | Status |
|---|---|---|
| NHS (UK) | "A high temperature is 38C or more." | [opened] nhs.uk/conditions/fever-in-children, reviewed 3 Jan 2024 |
| AAP / HealthyChildren (US) | Normal is about 37°C (98.6°F), plus or minus about 0.6°C. A **rectal** reading over 100.4°F (38.0°C) is a fever. No thresholds for other sites on this page. | [opened] "Fever Without Fear", updated 23 Sep 2025 |
| Canadian Paediatric Society (CPS) | Normal ranges by site. Anything above the range is a fever. Rectal 36.6–37.9°C. Oral 35.5–37.5°C. Armpit 36.5–37.5°C. Ear 35.8–37.9°C. | [opened] Caring for Kids, "Fever and temperature taking", updated Jun 2022 |

App takeaway: use **38°C** as the plain-language threshold. Add a note that normal readings vary a little by measuring site.

### 1.2 How to measure, by age (bodies disagree)

- **NHS:** use a digital thermometer in the armpit with the arm held gently against the body. Re-check after a few minutes if the child has just come out of a bath or from under blankets. [opened]
- **NICE NG143:** under 4 weeks, an electronic thermometer in the armpit. From 4 weeks to 5 years, an electronic or chemical-dot thermometer in the armpit, or an infrared ear thermometer. Do not use forehead chemical strips. Oral and rectal routes are not routine. [recall, unverified: NICE returned 403]
- **AAP:** a rectal thermometer is the most accurate for babies. Touching the forehead is unreliable. [opened]
- **CPS:** birth to 2 years, rectal is most accurate, armpit as an alternative. 2–5 years, rectal is most accurate, ear or armpit as alternatives. Over 5 years, oral is most accurate, ear or armpit as alternatives. If an armpit or ear reading is normal but the child seems warm and unwell, check rectally. [opened]
- **Disagreement to show in the app:** the UK uses armpit readings under 5, while North America prefers rectal readings for babies and toddlers. Suggested wording: "Use a digital thermometer as the instructions say. Armpit readings are common and acceptable. Some doctors prefer other methods for young babies. Don't rely on touch."

### 1.3 Fever is a sign, not an illness

- CPS: fever medicines bring the temperature down for a while but **don't treat the cause**. It is normal for the fever to come back after a dose. [opened]
- NHS: most fevers settle within **1 to 4 days**. [opened]
- AAP: the comfort of the child matters more than the number. Call the doctor if the child still seems sick after the fever has come down. [opened]
- NICE: fever medicines should not be used **only to bring the temperature down** in a child who is otherwise well. They should not be used **to prevent febrile seizures**, because they don't. [search: secondary summaries of NG143]

### 1.4 Comfort care

| Point | NHS | AAP | CPS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fluids | Offer plenty of fluids. Keep breastfeeding. | Offer plenty of fluids. | Offer plenty of fluids. |
| Clothing | **Don't undress** the child or sponge them down. **Don't over-dress or over-wrap.** | **One light layer**, plus a blanket if they are shivering. | Take off extra blankets and layers, but **don't strip the child completely**, because shivering can push the temperature back up. |
| Cooling | No sponging. | Alcohol rubs, ice packs and sponging are **no longer recommended**. | (Not stated) |
| Night | Check on the child regularly, including during the night. | — | — |
| Medicines | Paracetamol or ibuprofen if the child is distressed. Check the pack. Ask a pharmacist. | Paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen. Under 2 years, check with the doctor or pharmacist. | Paracetamol or ibuprofen, dosed by weight as on the pack. Ibuprofen only if the child is drinking reasonably well. Talk to a doctor before giving ibuprofen to a baby under 6 months. |
| Both medicines together | **Don't alternate** paracetamol and ibuprofen unless a health professional advises it. | Not covered on this page. | Both can be given, but the two medicines have **different dosing intervals**, so keep careful track. |
| Aspirin | **Never under 16.** | Not on the fever page. | Never for fever, because of the risk of **Reye's syndrome** (liver and brain damage). |

- **NICE NG143:** avoid tepid sponging, under-dressing and over-wrapping. Don't give paracetamol and ibuprofen **at the same time**. Consider **alternating** them only if the child's distress persists or comes back before the next dose is due. [search: BMJ summary and MDDUS summary of NG143]
- **Disagreement:** CPS allows both medicines to be used together if they are tracked carefully. NICE and NHS discourage combining or alternating them unless a professional advises it. **PlayNest wording:** "Don't switch back and forth between two fever medicines unless a doctor or pharmacist has told you to."
- NHS age limits, in general terms and with no doses: some fever medicines are **not suitable for very young babies, very small babies, or children with certain conditions** (NHS names chickenpox, dehydration and asthma for ibuprofen). Tell parents to check the pack and ask a pharmacist. Don't reproduce the specific cut-offs as instructions.

### 1.5 When to get help (exact thresholds as each source states them)

**NHS: call 111 (urgent, but not an emergency) if the child** [opened]
- is **under 3 months** with a temperature of **38°C or higher**
- is **3–6 months** with a temperature of **39°C or higher**
- has other signs of illness, such as a rash
- has had a high temperature for **5 days or more**
- is refusing food, or isn't their usual self and you're worried
- shows signs of dehydration

**NHS: call 999 (emergency) if the child** [opened]
- has a stiff neck, or a **rash that doesn't fade when a glass is pressed against it**
- is bothered by light
- has a fit (seizure) for the first time
- has unusually cold hands and feet
- has pale, blotchy, blue or grey skin, lips or tongue
- is **hard to wake**, or is very agitated or confused
- is **finding it hard to breathe**, or is breathing very fast
- isn't responding normally or isn't interested in feeding or usual activities

**NICE traffic-light system (NG143, table 2):** signs are sorted into green (low risk), amber (intermediate) and red (high risk). Any red sign means high risk. [search: NICE QS64 statement 1 snippet]
- From memory, still unverified: **red** includes age under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more, pale, mottled, ashen or blue colour, no response to social cues, not waking or not staying awake, a weak, high-pitched or continuous cry, grunting, a fast breathing rate, chest indrawing, reduced skin turgor, a non-blanching rash, a bulging fontanelle, a stiff neck and seizures. **Amber** includes age 3–6 months with a temperature of 39°C or more, fever for 5 days or more, rigors, dry mucous membranes, poor feeding and reduced urine. Note that NICE places 3–6 months with 39°C or more in **amber**, not red. The NHS 111 advice matches this level of urgency. [recall, unverified]

**AAP: call the doctor right away if** [opened]
- the child is **under 3 months** with a temperature of **100.4°F (38.0°C) or higher**
- the fever **repeatedly rises above 104°F (40°C)**, at any age
- the child looks very ill, is unusually drowsy or very fussy, has a stiff neck, a severe headache, sore throat or ear pain, an unexplained rash, repeated vomiting or diarrhoea, signs of dehydration, or a seizure
- the child has been in a very hot place, or has an immune problem, sickle cell disease or cancer, or takes steroids

**AAP: call during office hours if**
- a child **under 2** has had a fever for **more than 24 hours**
- a child **aged 2 or over** has had a fever for **more than 3 days (72 hours)**
- the child still seems sick after the fever comes down, or is getting worse

**CPS** [opened]
- Any baby **under 6 months** with a fever should see a doctor. Under 3 months, the visit is **urgent**.
- Older children who are drinking and otherwise seem well can be cared for at home. See a doctor if the fever lasts **more than 48–72 hours**.
- Contact a provider if the child is very cranky, unusually sleepy or unresponsive, keeps wheezing or coughing, or has a rash with the fever.

**Disagreement on duration:** NHS says 5 days (and the NHS cold page says a fever for more than 3 days needs a GP). AAP says 24 hours for children under 2 and 3 days for older children. CPS says 48–72 hours. **PlayNest recommendation:** use the more cautious rule: "If a fever lasts more than 2–3 days, or you're unsure, check with a doctor. Get urgent help sooner for babies under 6 months." Show the age table and say that guidance differs between countries.

**Red flags for the app, in plain language and agreed by all sources:** any fever in a baby under 3 months. A rash that doesn't fade under a glass. Floppy, hard to wake or unusually drowsy. Breathing hard or fast, grunting, or ribs pulling in. Blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin or lips. Signs of dehydration. A seizure. A stiff neck or dislike of light. A fever that keeps going (see the duration note above).

## 2. Common illnesses and sick days

### 2.1 Colds
- **AAP:** children have about **8–10 colds in their first two years**. Children in childcare or with school-age brothers and sisters may have more. A typical cold improves gradually over about **7–10 days**. [opened] HealthyChildren "Children and Colds", updated 27 Jan 2025
- **NHS:** adults usually feel better within **1–2 weeks**. In children, symptoms can last **longer**, and children stay infectious for longer. [opened] reviewed 22 Mar 2024
- **Antibiotics don't help colds.** Colds are caused by viruses, and antibiotics only work on bacteria. Both NHS and AAP say this. [opened]
- **NHS home care:** rest and fluids. **No honey under 12 months.** No hot drinks for small children. **Never let a child breathe steam from a bowl of hot water**, because of the risk of scalds. **Decongestants are not for children under 6.** Vitamin C, echinacea and garlic have little evidence behind them. [opened]
- **When to see a GP for a cold (NHS):** a fever for more than 3 days, symptoms getting worse, no improvement after **10 days**, a cough lasting more than **3 weeks**, shortness of breath or chest pain, a long-term condition or weak immune system, or **any worry about a child**. [opened]
- **AAP:** call at the **first sign of illness in a baby 3 months or younger**. Over 3 months, call for flaring nostrils, the skin between the ribs pulling in, fast or hard breathing, blue lips or nails, a runny nose lasting more than 10–14 days, a daytime cough lasting more than 10 days, ear pain, a temperature **over 102°F (38.9°C)**, unusual sleepiness, or a child who isn't getting better each day or isn't well within a week. [opened]

### 2.2 Coughs
- Covered only through the cold pages above. Key points: a cough after a cold can last weeks, more than 3 weeks needs a GP (NHS), and difficult or fast breathing is urgent. A dedicated NHS cough page was not opened.

### 2.3 Tummy bugs and dehydration
- **NHS:** diarrhoea usually stops within **5–7 days** and vomiting within **1–2 days**. Give small, frequent sips of fluid. **Keep breastfeeding or bottle-feeding.** **Don't make formula weaker.** Avoid fruit juice and fizzy drinks. **Don't give anti-diarrhoea medicine to children under 12.** A pharmacist can advise on oral rehydration sachets. [opened] reviewed 21 Dec 2023
- **Dehydration signs (NHS):** under 5, **fewer wet nappies**. Over 5, **dark, strong-smelling pee, or peeing less often**. [opened]
- **Call 111 (NHS) if:** you're worried about a baby under 12 months, the child stops breastfeeding or bottle-feeding, a child under 5 has fewer wet nappies, dehydration signs continue despite rehydration sachets, the child can't keep fluids down, there is blood in the diarrhoea, or **diarrhoea lasts more than 7 days or vomiting more than 2 days**. [opened]
- **999 / A&E (NHS):** **green or yellow-green vomit** in children, vomiting blood or vomit that looks like coffee grounds, possible poisoning, a stiff neck with sensitivity to light, a sudden severe headache or tummy pain, blue, grey or blotchy skin, severe breathing difficulty, or confusion or unresponsiveness. [opened]
- **WHO:** treat diarrhoea with **oral rehydration solution (ORS)**, which is clean water, sugar and salt. In settings where WHO protocols apply, a **10–14-day course of zinc** shortens the illness. Keep feeding, including breast milk. WHO dehydration classes: **severe** is two or more of lethargy or unconsciousness, sunken eyes, drinking poorly or not at all, and a skin pinch that goes back very slowly (2 seconds or more). **Some** is two or more of restlessness or irritability, sunken eyes, and drinking eagerly. Diarrhoea is the **third leading cause of death in children under 5**. [opened] WHO fact sheet, 7 Mar 2024
  - App note: zinc is a clinician-led treatment. Mention it as "your doctor may suggest zinc", never as a dose. ORS: "use ready-made sachets mixed exactly as the pack says. Don't make your own mix stronger." Iranian pharmacies sell ORS sachets widely ("پودر ORS"). Don't confuse ORS with IV drips (see §9).

### 2.4 Ear pain
- **NHS:** most earaches in children come from ear infections that **start to improve after a few days**. Get help if there is **no improvement within 2–3 days**. Home care: pain relief and a warm flannel. **Don't put anything in the ear.** In England, pharmacists can now treat middle-ear infections in children aged **1–17**. [opened] reviewed 27 Oct 2025
- **Urgent (GP or 111):** earache lasting more than 2–3 days, a very high temperature or feeling generally unwell, **swelling around the ear**, **fluid coming from the ear**, a change in hearing, something stuck in the ear, or a child **under 2 with pain in both ears**. [opened]
- The NHS page does not discuss antibiotics. Antibiotic stewardship for ear infections should be sourced separately before copy says "antibiotics often aren't needed".

### 2.5 Staying home from school or nursery (NHS, based on UKHSA)
[opened] nhs.uk "Is my child too ill for school?", reviewed 5 Apr 2024
- **High temperature:** stay home until it has gone.
- **Cough or cold:** go to school if mild (runny nose, sore throat) and there is no fever.
- **Diarrhoea or vomiting:** stay home until **48 hours** after the last episode.
- **Chickenpox:** stay home until all spots have **crusted over**, usually about 5 days.
- **Conjunctivitis, head lice, threadworms, hand foot and mouth disease:** no need to stay home if the child is well.
- **Impetigo:** stay home until the sores have crusted and healed, or for 48 hours after starting antibiotics.
- **Scarlet fever:** go back **24 hours after starting antibiotics**.
- **Measles:** call the GP first. Stay home for at least **4 days from when the rash appears**.
- **Ear infection or sore throat:** stay home only with a high temperature or severe pain.
- App note: say that schools in Iran and elsewhere may have their own rules.

## 3. Teeth

### 3.1 When teeth come
- **NHS:** most babies start teething at around **6 months**, but the range runs from before 4 months to after 12 months. A few are born with teeth. Typical order: central incisors about 6 months, lateral incisors about 8 months, first molars about 12 months, canines about 18 months, second molars about 24 months. Bottom teeth usually come before top teeth. Most children have **all their milk teeth by 2–3 years**. [opened] reviewed 14 May 2026
- **FDA, citing AAP:** teething usually starts at **4–7 months**. [opened]

### 3.2 Teething myths and unsafe remedies
- **Fever:** NHS lists only a **slightly raised temperature, below 38°C**, as a possible teething sign. It also says there is **no evidence that teething causes diarrhoea**. FDA (citing AAP) lists a "low-grade fever". **App line:** "Teething doesn't cause a real fever (38°C or more). If your baby has one, look for another cause." [opened]
- **FDA:** don't use **benzocaine** products for teething, because of the risk of **methemoglobinemia**, which reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen and can be fatal. Don't use **prescription viscous lidocaine** in babies or young children, because of the risk of seizures, brain injury and death. Teething gels, creams and homeopathic tablets give **little or no benefit**. **Teething jewellery such as amber necklaces** has caused deaths and injuries from **strangulation and choking**. Safer options: rub the gums with a clean finger, or offer a firm rubber teether that is not filled with liquid and **not frozen**, with supervision. [opened] FDA consumer update, current as of 26 Jun 2024
- **MHRA (UK):** in 2009, oral gels containing **salicylate** (for example the original Bonjela) were declared **not for use under 16**, because of a theoretical risk of Reye's syndrome. In 2018 the MHRA found a **lack of evidence of benefit for lidocaine teething gels** and made them pharmacy-only. [search: gov.uk Drug Safety Update snippet and secondary reports]
- **Regulators differ:** the UK still allows some lidocaine teething gels to be sold through pharmacies. The FDA advises against all of them. **PlayNest wording:** "Gels and necklaces aren't recommended. Try a clean finger or a cool, firm teether."

### 3.3 Fluoride toothpaste by age (bodies differ on strength vs amount)
| Age | NHS (strength and amount) [opened, reviewed 30 Jun 2025] | ADA / AAPD (amount) [search] |
|---|---|---|
| First tooth to under 3 | Fluoride toothpaste of **at least 1,000 ppm**, a **smear**. Higher-strength family toothpaste only on a dentist's advice. | A **smear or rice-grain** amount |
| 3–6 | **1,000–1,500 ppm**, **pea-sized** | **Pea-sized** |
| 7 and over | **1,350–1,500 ppm** | — |

- **NHS:** brush **twice a day for about 2 minutes**, including **last thing at night**. **Spit, don't rinse**, because rinsing washes the fluoride away. A parent brushes for children under 3 and supervises from 3 to 6. Watch over children aged 7 and up as well. [opened]
- Iran note: toothpaste labels in Iran may not state the ppm. Tell parents to look for "fluoride" or "فلوراید" on the label and to ask a dentist.

### 3.4 First dentist visit
- **NHS:** when the first milk teeth appear, or **before 12 months**. NHS dental care for children is free in the UK. [opened]
- **AAPD/AAP:** by the **first birthday**, or within 6 months of the first tooth. [recall, unverified, consistent with NHS]

### 3.5 Bottles at bedtime, sugary drinks and snacks
- I did not open a source for this in this pass. Standard NHS, AAP and AAPD advice is: no bottle of milk or juice in bed, move from a bottle to a cup at around 12 months, give only milk and water between meals, and **how often sugar is eaten matters more than how much** (keep sweet things to mealtimes). [recall, unverified: needs sourcing, for example NHS "Children's teeth" sugar section or the UKHSA "Delivering better oral health" toolkit]

## 4. Vaccines

- **Why they matter (WHO):** I did not open a WHO immunization page this pass. Use the WHO "Vaccines and immunization" fact sheet when writing copy. [gap]
- **Follow your country's schedule:** Iran has a national immunization programme and schedule run by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education. Point parents to their local health centre (مرکز بهداشت / خانه بهداشت). No details are needed. [recall]
- **Mild side effects** such as soreness, redness, mild fever and fussiness are common and short-lived. [recall, standard WHO/NHS messaging: source before shipping]
- **Making injections less upsetting:**
  - **2010 HELPinKids guideline** (Taddio et al., CMAJ 182(18):E843). The panel included the **Canadian Paediatric Society**. Recommendations: **breastfeeding during the injection** for children up to 2 (graded strong despite very low-certainty evidence), sweet-tasting solutions for babies, topical anaesthetic creams, distraction, slow deep breathing for older children, and holding the child. **Avoid** laying the child flat and saying "**it won't hurt**". [search: CMAJ abstract and snippets]
  - **2015 HELPinKids&Adults update** (CMAJ 187(13):975). I could not open it (403). From memory: no aspiration, the most painful vaccine given last, infants held upright or skin-to-skin, and parents staying calm and avoiding repeated reassurance. [recall, unverified]
  - **2026 update** (Taddio et al., "Reducing distress during vaccine injections: 2026 clinical practice guideline update", **CMAJ, 21 Sep 2026, DOI 10.1503/cmaj.260123**). It has 16 recommendations built around **CARD (Comfort–Ask–Relax–Distract)**. For babies up to 12 months: **breastfeeding, bottle-feeding, sweet-tasting liquids or a dummy**. For all ages: **distraction** (toys and singing for babies, music, games or screens for older children) and **topical anaesthetic** creams. It also recommends education for parents and fast injection technique. [opened: news-medical.net report on the guideline, 21 Sep 2026. The CMAJ full text was not opened.]
- **Misinformation:** no source opened. Suggested approach: acknowledge the worry, give the parent one or two clear facts, point them to their doctor or health centre, and don't argue. [gap]

## 5. Movement and sleep

### 5.1 WHO 2019: under-5s (physical activity, sedentary behaviour, sleep)
[opened] WHO news release, 24 Apr 2019
| Age | Activity | Restrained (pram, high chair, carrier) | Screen | Sleep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 1 | Active several times a day. If not yet mobile, **at least 30 min of tummy time** spread across the day | No more than **1 hour at a time** | **Not recommended** | **14–17 h** (0–3 months), **12–16 h** (4–11 months), naps included |
| 1–2 years | **At least 180 min** of any intensity across the day | No more than 1 h at a time | **None for 1-year-olds**. **No more than 1 h** for 2-year-olds (less is better) | **11–14 h** including naps, regular times |
| 3–4 years | **At least 180 min**, of which **at least 60 min moderate-to-vigorous** | No more than 1 h at a time | **No more than 1 h** | **10–13 h** (may include a nap), regular times |

The WHO 2019 guidelines also encourage reading and storytelling with a carer when a child is sitting still. [recall]

### 5.2 WHO 2020: ages 5–17
- At least an **average of 60 minutes a day** of moderate-to-vigorous activity across the week, **mostly aerobic**. **Vigorous** activity and activities that **strengthen muscle and bone** on **at least 3 days a week**. **Limit sedentary time, especially recreational screen time.** "Any physical activity is better than none." [search: several secondary summaries, including Okely et al. (PMC9219310). The WHO fact sheet I opened (4 Nov 2026) confirms only "any amount is better than none".]
- **WHO fact sheet** [opened, 4 Nov 2026]: **86% of adolescents aged 11–17** don't meet the recommended activity levels. One in four reported no active days in the past week. More girls than boys reported no active days (28% vs 22%).

### 5.3 Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines (CSEP)
- The CSEP home page frames the guidelines as "Move, Sleep, Sit" for 0–4 and "**Sweat, Step, Sleep, Sit**" for 5–17. [opened, no numbers given]
- From memory, for 5–17 (2016): **60 min or more of moderate-to-vigorous activity a day**, vigorous activity and muscle/bone strengthening on 3 days a week, **several hours of light activity**, **9–11 h of sleep (ages 5–13)** and **8–10 h (ages 14–17)**, **no more than 2 h of recreational screen time a day**, and limited long periods of sitting. The 0–4 guidelines (2017) closely match WHO 2019, which drew on them. [recall, unverified: the children-youth page failed to load]

## 6. Toilet training and bedwetting

### 6.1 Readiness and age
- **AAP:** children generally gain control of bladder and bowel **between 18 and 24 months**. The average age to start toilet training in the US is **2 to 3 years**. **Most children are trained by about 4.** Readiness signs: staying **dry for at least 2 hours** or after naps, showing cues before peeing or pooing (grunting, going still, squatting), following simple instructions, walking to the bathroom and helping undress, disliking wet nappies, and asking for the toilet or "big-kid" underwear. [opened] HealthyChildren "The Right Age to Potty Train", updated 24 May 2022 (from AAP *Toilet Training*, 2022)
- **AAP approach:** child-led. Use positive words for the body and for toilet functions, let the child take ownership, build toilet trips into the routine, and **praise specific efforts rather than giving treats**. **Avoid punishment**, pleading and power struggles. Postpone training during big upheavals such as a move, a new baby, illness or bereavement. [opened]
- **Daytime before night-time:** the AAP page covers daytime and nap dryness only. Night-time dryness comes later and is a separate developmental step (see 6.3). The NHS potty-training page URL returned 404 this session. [gap]
- **Child-led vs structured approaches (e.g. "3-day" methods):** not researched in depth this pass. The AAP hub lists an article titled "Toilet Training: Which Method is Best?". Systematic reviews generally find little high-quality comparative evidence. [gap]

### 6.2 Constipation as a hidden cause
- **NHS bedwetting page:** bedwetting can be linked to **constipation** (and to diabetes). [opened]
- Constipation as a common hidden cause of daytime accidents and toilet refusal is standard paediatric teaching (NICE CG99), but I did not source it this pass. [recall, unverified]

### 6.3 Bedwetting
- **NHS:** bedwetting is **common** and often **runs in families**. **Many children under 5 wet the bed**, and most grow out of it. Help: give **enough drinks during the day (don't cut fluids)**, take the child to the toilet regularly and **just before bed**, make the toilet easy to reach at night, use waterproof covers, and reward dry nights. **Avoid:** **punishing the child**, caffeinated drinks such as cola, tea and coffee, and regularly waking the child to pee. **See a GP if** home measures don't work, or if bedwetting **starts again after more than 6 months of being dry**. Treatments include an alarm or medicine to reduce night-time urine. [opened] reviewed 11 Apr 2023 (the next review was due Apr 2026 and may have happened)
- **NICE CG111:** from memory, NICE says not to exclude younger children (under 5) from management just because of age. It treats a bedwetting alarm as first-line for motivated families and desmopressin as an alternative. It asks clinicians to look for constipation, daytime symptoms and new-onset wetting. [recall, unverified: NICE returned 403]
- **PlayNest age wording:** "Wet nights are common until about 5, and still fairly common up to 7. It isn't anyone's fault. Talk to a doctor if your child is 5 or older and it bothers them or you, if they were dry for 6 months and started wetting again, or if they have daytime accidents, pain, constipation, or are very thirsty."

## 7. Hygiene and the growing body

- **Handwashing:** no source opened this pass. Standard CDC/WHO points: wash before eating and preparing food, after the toilet or a nappy change, after coughing, sneezing or nose-blowing, and after touching animals. Use soap and water for about **20 seconds** (singing "Happy Birthday" twice is a common timer). [recall: source before shipping]
- **Eyesight and outdoor time:** a 2026 meta-analysis of 31 studies (about 380,000 children) linked **more than 2 hours a day outdoors** with **26% lower odds of myopia** (OR 0.74), with the benefit levelling off above that. In a Guangzhou randomised trial, adding **40 minutes** of outdoor class time a day cut 3-year myopia incidence from about **39.5% to 30.4%**. Outdoor time helps prevent myopia, but the evidence that it slows myopia that has **already started** is weaker. **I found no WHO or AAO 2-hour guideline.** The 2-hour figure comes from research and optometry bodies. The **20-20-20 rule** and screen distance were not supported by any source I found, and evidence linking near work to myopia is weaker than evidence for outdoor time. [search: Optometry Times (Apr 2026), Optometry Australia, Review of Optometry]
- **Hearing, posture and backpacks:** not researched this pass. Treat backpack weight rules (for example "10–15% of body weight") as **low evidence**. [gap]
- **Sun:** already covered elsewhere and skipped.

## 8. Medicines safety basics (no dosing)

No primary source opened this pass. Standard points shared by NHS, AAP and CDC "Up and Away" (**source before shipping**):
- Use **the syringe or spoon that comes with the medicine**, not a kitchen spoon.
- **Check the active ingredient** on every product. Many cold and flu mixtures already contain paracetamol, which risks a double dose. The NHS cold page warns that **decongestants are not for children under 6** [opened].
- Store medicines **up high, out of sight, and locked** if possible, including vitamins and iron.
- **Never call medicine "sweets"** or candy.
- Keep the original packaging and leaflet. Follow the age and weight limits on the pack. Ask a pharmacist when unsure.
- **No aspirin under 16** (NHS [opened], CPS [opened]).
- **No anti-diarrhoea medicine under 12** (NHS [opened]).
- **No honey under 12 months** (NHS cold page [opened]).

## 9. Iran-specific context

What the Iranian literature shows (local studies, not national data):
- **Tabriz, 300 parents of children admitted to hospital:** "fever phobia" was common and knowledge was low. Only **53%** of mothers knew the fever threshold. Only **29%** used a thermometer. **25.7% had given antibiotics for a child's fever.** [search: tbzmed DSpace abstract snippet. The page refused connection.]
- **Tehran, parents of primary-school children:** about **11.1%** did not see a doctor for a seasonal cold, and **around half of those self-medicated**. Self-medication was linked to the mother's education level. [search: Persian-language abstract via DOAJ, J. Pharmacoeconomics & Pharm. Mgmt, 2018]
- **Bandar Abbas (children aged 1–8), Gorgan (270 children at a paediatric clinic), Jahrom (mothers of children under 7), Ardakan/Yazd villages (mothers of children under 5):** local studies on parental self-medication, many covering antibiotics. Parental education, insurance status, number of children, the mother working outside the home and a past hospital admission were associated with self-medication or with better knowledge. The prevalence figures were not visible in the snippets. [search]
- **Sabzevar, Hamadan, Kerman:** fever-management knowledge among mothers was judged poor. In Kerman, simulation-based teaching improved knowledge and practice. [search]

Practices to address gently in PlayNest copy. The ones marked **unverified** are widely reported anecdotally but were not sourced this pass and should be confirmed with Iranian clinicians or reviewers:
- **Over-wrapping a feverish child** "to sweat the fever out". Covered by the NHS advice not to over-wrap and the AAP one-light-layer advice. **unverified for Iran**
- **Cooling with cold water, ice or alcohol rubs (پاشویه with cold water or alcohol).** Contradicted by AAP, NICE and NHS. Note that lukewarm پاشویه is a common traditional practice, while AAP and NICE advise against sponging. **unverified for Iran**
- **Asking for antibiotics for colds and fevers, or using leftover antibiotics.** Supported by the studies above. Message: "Most childhood fevers and colds are viral. Antibiotics don't help and can cause harm. Only use them when a doctor prescribes them, and finish the course as directed."
- **Requests for an IV drip ("سرم") or injections ("آمپول") for a quick recovery.** Message: oral rehydration (ORS) is the first-line treatment for mild to moderate dehydration (WHO). IV fluids are a medical decision. **unverified prevalence**
- **Over-the-counter access** to many medicines in Iranian pharmacies makes the "check the active ingredient" message especially important. **unverified**
- **Persian red-flag wording:** «تب بالای ۳۸ درجه در نوزاد زیر ۳ ماه = فوراً به پزشک», «لکه‌هایی که با فشار لیوان کم‌رنگ نمی‌شوند», «بی‌حالی و سخت بیدار شدن», «تنفس تند یا سخت», «علائم کم‌آبی: پوشک خشک، گریه بدون اشک». ## 10. Where bodies disagree (summary for writers)

1. **Measuring site:** UK uses the armpit under 5. AAP and CPS prefer rectal for babies.
2. **Fever duration before seeing a doctor:** NHS says 5 days (3 days on its cold page). AAP says more than 24 h under age 2 and more than 3 days from age 2. CPS says 48–72 h.
3. **Infant thresholds:** NHS uses 3–6 months with 39°C or more (NICE amber). CPS says any fever under 6 months should see a doctor.
4. **Two fever medicines:** CPS says they can be used together if carefully tracked. NICE and NHS say don't alternate unless advised and never give both at once.
5. **Undressing:** NHS says don't undress. CPS says remove extra layers but don't strip. AAP says one light layer.
6. **Teething gels:** the UK allows some lidocaine gels through pharmacies. FDA advises against all benzocaine and lidocaine gels.
7. **Toothpaste:** NHS specifies fluoride strength (ppm) by age. ADA and AAPD specify amount (smear or rice grain, then pea-sized).
8. **WHO 2020 strengthening:** most summaries say 3 days a week, but one table said 2. Check the WHO PDF.
