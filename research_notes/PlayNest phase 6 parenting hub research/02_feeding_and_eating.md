## 1. Milk feeding basics

### Responsive feeding (hunger and fullness cues)
- WHO lists responsive feeding (recognising and answering hunger and fullness signs) as a core practice from birth through the complementary-feeding period. [Opened: WHO fact sheet, *Infant and young child feeding*, updated Aug 2026]
- WHO's 2023 complementary-feeding guideline keeps responsive feeding as a stand-alone recommendation: respond to cues to avoid both under- and overfeeding and to support development. [Secondary: ENN *Field Exchange* summary of the 2023 guideline]
- App language: early hunger cues (rooting, hands to mouth, lip smacking, stirring), crying as a *late* cue; fullness cues (turning away, closing mouth, relaxed hands, falling asleep, pushing bottle away). Same cues apply to bottle-fed babies; do not push a baby to finish a bottle. [Memory, verify – standard content across NHS/UNICEF Baby Friendly]

### How long to breastfeed — where bodies agree and differ
| Body | Exclusive | Continued |
|---|---|---|
| WHO (fact sheet 2026; 2023 guideline) | 6 months; start within first hour | to 2 years or beyond |
| AAP (policy statement, *Pediatrics* July 2022, Meek & Noble) | about 6 months | alongside solids for 2 years or beyond, as long as parent and child wish (was "at least 1 year" in 2012) |
| CPS / Caring for Kids (page last updated Jan 2020) | breast milk alone meets needs for 6 months | can continue "well into the toddler years" (no fixed age on the parent page) |
| NHS | around 6 months | (no fixed end; as long as you both want) |
- The AAP 2022 statement also stressed **non-judgemental support** and structural barriers (paid leave, pumping space). Good model for PlayNest tone. [Opened: HealthyChildren news item, 27 June 2022; HealthyChildren "Where We Stand: Breastfeeding", updated 2026]
- Tone note for the app: present breast milk, formula and combination feeding as all valid ways to feed a baby; avoid "best/failure" language. Lactation support should be offered, not pushed.

### Formula safety basics
- NHS making-up steps: fresh tap water boiled and cooled for no more than 30 minutes (so it is still ≥70 °C, which kills bacteria that can be in powder); water in the bottle first, then powder; use only the scoop from that tin, loosely filled and levelled; **never add extra powder or extra water, never add sugar or cereal**; make one feed at a time and discard leftovers; cool under running water, test on the wrist; never microwave; sterilise bottles and teats. [Opened: NHS *How to make up baby formula*, reviewed Aug 2023]
- CPS: if not breastfeeding, use store-bought iron-fortified cow-milk-based formula to 9–12 months; **homemade formulas and plant "milks" are not substitutes**. [Opened: Caring for Kids, *Feeding your baby in the first year*]
- Why "never dilute or concentrate": over-diluted feeds can cause poor growth and dangerous low sodium (water intoxication); over-concentrated feeds stress kidneys and cause dehydration. [Memory, verify – standard paediatric teaching]
- Made-up formula left at room temperature: commonly "use within 2 hours, discard leftovers after a feed" (WHO/FAO 2007 preparation guidance). [Memory, verify — the NHS page I opened only says discard leftovers]

### Is my baby getting enough? (first weeks)
From an NHS trust (Cambridge University Hospitals, Rosie Hospital) infant-feeding page [Opened]:
- Day 1–2: 1–2+ wet nappies (orange/red "urate" stains can be normal at first), black-green meconium.
- Day 3–4: 3+ wet nappies, stools turning brown/green/yellow.
- Day 5–6: 5+ heavy wet nappies, 2+ yellow stools.
- Day 7–28: 6+ heavy wet nappies, 2+ yellow stools a day (about the size of a £2 coin or bigger).
- Breastfeeding usually ≥8 feeds in 24 h, audible/visible swallowing, baby comes off by themselves.
- Signs to get help: fewer than 8 feeds/24 h, very sleepy or hard to wake, constant feeding and never settling, jaundice, fewer wet/dirty nappies than expected, urates continuing after the first few days.
- Several NHS services add: weight loss of ≥10% of birth weight is a concern; most babies regain birth weight by about 2 weeks. [Secondary: NHS trust search snippets]
- App "call now" list (general, for an offline app): no wet nappy in 6+ hours in a young baby, dark urine, sunken soft spot, very sleepy/floppy, refuses several feeds, green (bile) vomit, any fever under 3 months. [Memory, verify against NHS 111 / AAP "when to call"]

## 2. Starting solids

### When — "around 6 months, not before 4 months"
- WHO: introduce safe complementary foods **at 6 months**; earlier or later can harm growth. [Opened: WHO fact sheet]
- NHS: around 6 months. Signs of readiness: (1) sits up and holds head steady, (2) coordinates eyes, hands and mouth to pick up food and bring it to the mouth, (3) swallows food rather than pushing it back out. **Not** signs of readiness: chewing fists, waking at night, wanting more milk; solids do not make babies sleep longer. [Opened: NHS *Baby's first solid foods*, reviewed Feb 2026]
- ASCIA (Australia): around 6 months when ready, **not before 4 months**. [Opened: ASCIA *How to introduce solid foods to babies*, Jan 2026]
- CPS: about 6 months; readiness = sits without support, good head control, picks up food, keeps it in mouth, shows interest, turns away when full. **Waiting well past 6 months raises the risk of iron deficiency.** [Opened: Caring for Kids]
- Why not before 4 months (17 weeks): gut/kidney immaturity, tongue-thrust reflex, solids displace milk, higher infection risk. [Memory, verify]
- **Disagreement to flag:** allergy bodies (BSACI, ASCIA, AAP) allow solids/allergens from 4 months for some babies; WHO and NHS say "around 6 months". Iran: end of 6 months, or 4–6 months only on health-worker advice for growth faltering (see §8).

### First foods and iron
- Iron stores from birth run low around 6 months, so first foods should include iron-rich foods. CPS: iron-rich foods **at least twice a day** (meat, poultry, cooked whole egg, fish, tofu, well-cooked legumes, iron-fortified infant cereal). [Opened: CPS]
- WHO 2023 guideline: animal-source foods (meat, fish, eggs) **daily**; fruit and vegetables daily; pulses, nuts and seeds frequently, especially where animal foods are scarce; prefer nutrient-dense foods over starchy staples. Modelling showed iron, zinc and B12 needs of 6–8-month-olds were hard to meet without animal foods. [Secondary: ENN summary]
- NHS starts with single vegetables/fruit (including less-sweet vegetables) and adds iron-rich foods "from 7–9 months" in its stage guide — a softer emphasis on iron than CPS/WHO. [Opened: NHS]
- Feeding frequency (WHO): 2–3 meals/day at 6–8 months, 3–4 at 9–23 months, plus 1–2 snacks as needed. NHS: about 3 meals + ~4 milk feeds by 7–9 months, 3 meals + ~3 milk feeds by 10–12 months, 3 meals + 2 snacks from 12 months. [Opened: WHO fact sheet; NHS]

### Textures and finger foods
- Progress from smooth/mashed to lumpy, then chopped family foods; keep offering lumps even if a baby prefers smooth. Finger foods from the start are fine (finger-length, soft enough to squash between your fingers). [Opened: NHS; CPS gives 6–9 m spoon + finger food, 9–12 m pincer grasp and trying a spoon, 12–18 m spoon and cup]
- Delaying lumpy textures past ~9–10 months is associated with later feeding difficulties (ALSPAC cohort, Coulthard 2009). [Memory, verify]

### Spoon-feeding vs baby-led weaning (BLW)
- NHS: no right or wrong; many families mix both; self-feeding does **not** carry a higher choking risk than spoon-feeding. [Opened: NHS]
- **BLISS RCT** (Dunedin, NZ; 206 families; Taylor et al., *JAMA Pediatrics* 2017): a *modified* baby-led approach (with guidance to offer iron-rich and energy-rich foods and to avoid high-choke-risk foods) gave **no difference in BMI z-score** at 12 or 24 months (overweight at 24 m 10.3% vs 6.4%, not significant, wide CI). BLISS children were rated **less food-fussy** and enjoyed food more at 12 months, but also had lower satiety responsiveness. [Secondary: Healio report of the trial]
- BLISS choking paper (Fangupo et al., *Pediatrics* 2016): choking rates similar between groups; BLISS babies gagged more at 6–7 months. But **many babies in both groups were offered high-choking-risk foods**, and most choking events happened during self-feeding with foods of the wrong shape/texture. [Secondary]
- Evidence strength: one moderate-size RCT of a *modified* BLW protocol; findings may not apply to unmodified BLW. Concerns about iron intake with unmodified BLW are why BLISS added iron guidance. App message: either method is fine; what matters is iron-rich foods, safe shapes/textures, sitting upright, and an adult watching.

### Gagging vs choking; food preparation by age
- Gagging is a normal, noisy protective reflex (watery eyes, tongue pushes food forward, retching). Choking is silent or near-silent, child cannot cough/cry/breathe → needs immediate first aid. Always stay with a baby who is eating. [Opened: NHS]
- High-risk foods/shapes (combined NHS, CPS, Iran MoH textbook):
  - Whole nuts and peanuts: NHS no whole nuts under **5**; AAP no whole peanuts under **4**; offer nuts ground, or nut butter thinly spread (never a spoonful alone). [Opened: NHS foods-to-avoid; HealthyChildren 2019]
  - Grapes, cherry tomatoes, cherries, berries of similar size: cut into quarters / lengthwise (NHS says quarters; CPS says slice round foods lengthwise). [Opened]
  - Hard raw vegetables/fruit (raw carrot, apple chunks, celery): grate, cook soft or slice thin. [Opened: CPS, NHS]
  - Popcorn, hard sweets/boiled candy, gum, gummies/jelly sweets, raw jelly cubes, marshmallows, hot dogs/sausages (cut lengthwise then small), fish with bones, raisins and dried fruit, whole corn kernels. [Opened: CPS, NHS, Iran textbook — which lists gum, آب‌نبات, large apple pieces, nuts, corn, raisins, raw carrot, celery, grapes, cherries and sour cherries (آلبالو)]
  - Child must sit upright and not walk, run, lie down or ride in a car while eating.

### Foods and drinks to avoid or limit before 1 year (and beyond)
- **Honey: none before 12 months** (infant botulism) — NHS, CPS, Iran MoH all agree. [Opened]
- **Salt**: no salt added to baby food or cooking water, avoid stock cubes and gravy (NHS); WHO: no added salt or sugar in complementary foods. [Opened: NHS; WHO *Healthy diet* fact sheet Jan 2026]
- **Sugar**: NHS advises children aged 4 and under avoid added sugar and sweeteners (incl. honey, syrups, juice, smoothies; whole fruit fine). WHO 2023: no sugar-sweetened drinks (strong rec., low-certainty evidence), no non-sugar sweeteners (strong rec., very-low-certainty), limit 100% juice (conditional). [Opened: NHS; Secondary: guideline excerpts]
- **Cow's milk as a drink** — a real disagreement:
  - NHS: not as main drink before 12 months; fine in cooking/mixed in food from ~6 months; from 12 months whole or semi-skimmed as main drink (skimmed/1% not before 5). [Opened]
  - CPS: whole (3.25%) cow's milk may start **9–12 months** if breast milk isn't available; after 12 months cap at **750 mL/day** (more crowds out food and causes iron-deficiency anaemia). [Opened]
  - WHO 2023: for non-breastfed infants 6–11 months, **either formula or animal milk** is acceptable (conditional, low certainty); for 12–23 months, animal milk, and follow-on/"growing-up" formulas are **not** recommended. [Secondary: ENN + guideline excerpts] — WHO's position reflects global affordability; ESPGHAN's response says animal milk should not be the *main* drink before 12 months.
  - Iran MoH: drinking cow's milk is **prohibited under 1 year**, but small recommended amounts may be used to cook فرنی / حریره بادام. [Opened: Iran MoH textbook, see §8]
  - AAP: whole milk from 12 months, roughly 16–24 oz (480–720 mL)/day. [Memory, verify]
- **Water**: NHS — sips from an open/free-flow cup with meals from about 6 months; tap water need not be boiled after 6 months (UK). Under 6 months, exclusively breastfed babies need no water; formula-fed babies may have a little cooled boiled water in hot weather. Iran MoH textbook: offer **boiled, cooled water** once complementary feeding starts. CPS: occasional water in an open cup. → App should say "use safe drinking water; boil and cool it if your local advice says so". [Opened]
- **Juice**: NHS — not needed under 12 months; if used, 1 part juice to 10 parts water, mealtimes only; from 5 years max ~150 mL/day with meals. CPS — not needed; if given, 100% juice max 125 mL/day. AAP 2017 — no juice before 12 months; 1–3 y max 4 oz (≈120 mL). Iran textbook (citing AAP): no fruit juice under 1 year, use fruit purée instead. [Opened: NHS, CPS, Iran textbook; Memory, verify: AAP 2017 numbers]
- **Rice drinks**: not for under-5s (arsenic) — NHS. Iran textbook recommends cooking rice in plenty of water (about 4 cups per cup of rice) and draining to remove much of the arsenic. [Opened]
- **Plant milks**: NHS — unsweetened calcium-fortified soya/oat/almond from age 1 (not rice). CPS — not a substitute for formula in infancy. [Opened]
- **Tea and coffee / caffeine**: NHS — not suitable for babies or young children. WHO 2023 places unhealthy drinks under "avoid". Tea's tannins reduce absorption of non-haem iron, which matters where iron deficiency is common. Iran MoH textbook says tea and herbal infusions block iron absorption and should be avoided as far as possible, **not even used to soften food**; if a mother insists, give only weak tea at least 2 hours before or after meals; avoid sweetened tea. [Opened: NHS drinks page (reviewed 2022); Iran textbook]
- Fizzy drinks, squash, "baby"/herbal drinks, slush ice drinks (glycerol, ≤4–7 y), energy drinks: avoid. [Opened: NHS drinks + foods-to-avoid pages; CPS]
- Other NHS no-gos: raw/lightly cooked shellfish; shark, swordfish, marlin (mercury, under 16); unpasteurised and mould-ripened soft cheeses; eggs fully cooked unless British Lion-stamped (UK-specific — **for Iran, advise fully cooked eggs**). [Opened]
- Bottles: offer a cup from ~6 months, discourage bottles from 1 year; nothing but milk or water in bottles (tooth decay). [Opened: NHS]

## 3. Early introduction of allergenic foods

### The evidence
- **LEAP** (Du Toit et al., *NEJM* 2015): ~640 high-risk infants (severe eczema and/or egg allergy), randomised at 4–11 months to eat peanut regularly vs avoid until 5 years. Peanut allergy at 5 years was dramatically lower with early eating (roughly 13.7% vs 1.9% overall; ~80% relative reduction). LEAP-On showed the protection persisted after a year of stopping. [Secondary for design (BSACI page); Memory, verify for figures]
- **EAT** (Perkin et al., *NEJM* 2016): ~1,300 exclusively breastfed 3-month-olds from the general population, six allergens introduced early vs standard ~6 months. Intention-to-treat result **not statistically significant**, largely because adherence was poor; per-protocol analysis showed less allergy (esp. peanut, egg). [Secondary for design (BSACI); Memory, verify for figures]
- Evidence strength: strong RCT evidence for **peanut in high-risk infants**; moderate/supportive for egg; weaker for other allergens. No evidence that delaying allergens past 4–6 months prevents allergy (AAP 2019 clinical report, Greer/Sicherer/Burks). Maternal avoidance in pregnancy/breastfeeding is not supported; hydrolysed formula does not prevent allergy. [Opened: HealthyChildren 2019 news item on the AAP clinical report]

### What each body says
- **NIAID Addendum (US, Jan 2017)** — three risk tiers: (1) severe eczema and/or egg allergy → peanut as early as **4–6 months**, after considering specific-IgE/skin-prick testing and possibly supervised first feed; (2) mild–moderate eczema → around **6 months** at home; (3) no eczema/food allergy → age-appropriate, with other foods. Maintenance in trials ≈ 6–7 g peanut protein per week over ~3 feeds. [Secondary + HealthyChildren 2019; NIAID site returned 405; Memory, verify for the 6–7 g figure]
- **AAP (2019)**: mirrors NIAID; also "no reason to delay" egg, fish etc. [Opened: HealthyChildren]
- **AAAAI/ACAAI/CSACI consensus (2021, JACI In Practice)**: introduce peanut and egg around 6 months but not before 4 months; feed a diverse diet; and (from my recollection) **routine screening tests before introduction are not recommended**, which loosens the 2017 NIAID testing advice. [Secondary for existence/authors; Memory, verify for the screening point] — **a genuine area of drift between 2017 and 2021 US guidance.**
- **ASCIA (Australia, updated 2026)**: start solids ~6 months, not before 4; introduce **all common allergens by 12 months** (cow's milk foods, egg, peanut, tree nuts, wheat, soy, sesame, fish, shellfish); egg and peanut soon after solids start; well-cooked egg, smooth peanut butter/paste, start with ~¼ teaspoon in a familiar food; one new allergen at a time; once tolerated, **keep each in the diet at least once a week**. Applies to babies with eczema too — keep eczema well controlled. A few hives around the mouth only, with no other symptoms, is usually not allergy; facial swelling, widespread hives, vomiting, noisy breathing, pallor/floppiness = stop and get medical help (emergency if severe). Don't rub food on skin. [Opened]
- **BSACI / BDA FASG (UK, guidance updated 2023)**: higher risk = eczema (esp. moderate–severe or early-onset in first 3 months) or an existing food allergy; family history alone is **not** "higher risk". For higher-risk babies, solids including egg then peanut may start **from 4 months**, once the baby is already managing other solids. Testing before introduction must be weighed against the harm of delay; anaphylaxis at first feeds is rare (~1–2 per 1,000 even in high-risk babies). Parent leaflet: talk to a health professional first if severe eczema. [Opened: BSACI page; Secondary for leaflet wording]
- **Iran MoH textbook (2021)**: summarises the AAP shift away from delaying allergens; advises small amounts of allergenic foods (except cow's milk) given at home, increasing gradually if no problem. [Opened; the exact two preconditions in the Persian text were not fully extracted]
- **Disagreements to state plainly**: testing for severe-eczema babies (NIAID 2017 "consider testing" vs ASCIA/BSACI/2021 consensus "don't delay for testing"); starting at 4 vs ~6 months; how often to keep allergens in (ASCIA ≥ weekly; LEAP-style ≈ 3 times weekly for peanut).
- **App message**: introduce common allergens (egg, peanut first) once solids are going, in the first year, in safe forms; one new one at a time; keep them in regularly; if your baby has **severe eczema or an existing food allergy, talk to a doctor or health centre first**; know reaction signs.

## 5. Responsive feeding for toddlers and older children

### Satter's Division of Responsibility (sDOR)
- Parent decides **what, when and where** food is offered; child decides **whether and how much** to eat (for babies: parent decides what, child decides how much and how fast). [Secondary: multiple educational texts; ellynsatterinstitute.org not opened]
- Evidence base — be honest: sDOR itself has **not** been tested as a named intervention in large RCTs. Support is **indirect**: (a) observational and experimental studies showing **pressure to eat** is linked to more food avoidance/pickiness and **restriction** to more interest in the restricted food and eating without hunger; (b) responsive-parenting RCTs that include structure + child autonomy (e.g., NOURISH in Australia, INSIGHT in the US) found modest benefits on eating behaviours/weight; (c) a validated adherence measure (Lohse & Mitchell, *J Nutr Educ Behav* 2021) now exists, so better studies are possible. It is widely adopted by dietitians and paediatric feeding clinicians and fits WHO's responsive-feeding principle. [Memory, verify — I could not open the Lohse/Mitchell paper]
- Iran's MoH textbook has a section on "not using force or compulsion in feeding": forcing food and using foods such as chocolate as a **reward** disrupt the child's self-regulation and can lead to under- or over-weight; when a child refuses food, look for the reason (tiredness, low activity) rather than force-feeding. [Opened] — nice local anchor for the Persian copy.

### Picky eating and food neophobia
- Fussiness peaks roughly 2–6 years and is a normal developmental stage for most children; growth slows after infancy so appetite drops. [Secondary]
- **Repeated exposure** works: in infant studies a disliked vegetable became accepted after about 8 tastes; commonly quoted "8–15 exposures" is a reasonable range but the exact number is not fixed, and a small taste counts. Repeated exposure alone may not be enough for highly neophobic or taste-sensitive children. [Secondary; the "15" number comes from popular sources — use "often 8–15 tries, sometimes more"]
- Practical: serve a small portion of the new food alongside a "safe" food the child already eats; eat it yourself; no pressure, no praise-for-swallowing contests; let the child touch, smell, lick; involve them in shopping/cooking.
- Avoid: pressure ("two more bites"), bribes ("eat your vegetables and you get dessert" — makes dessert more valued, vegetables less), "clean plate" rules, using food as reward/comfort, labelling the child "picky".

### Red flags → see a doctor (possible feeding disorder / ARFID)
- Weight loss, falling off growth curve, or not gaining.
- Gagging, coughing or vomiting with all or most textures; can't move beyond purées past ~10–12 months; choking or breathing problems with eating; pain with eating.
- Very short list of accepted foods (often quoted "fewer than ~20"), whole food groups dropped, list shrinking over time.
- Strong distress, panic or meltdowns at meals; family life organised around the child's eating.
- Dependence on supplements/milk for most energy; signs of nutrient deficiency.
- ARFID (DSM-5): restriction driven by sensory sensitivity, fear of aversive consequences (choking/vomiting) or low interest, causing weight/nutrition problems or psychosocial impairment — not about body image. More common alongside autism, ADHD and anxiety. [Secondary; DSM criteria from Memory, verify]

## 6. Family meals

- **Hammons & Fiese, *Pediatrics* 2011** meta-analysis (17 studies, ~182,800 children/adolescents): eating ≥3 family meals a week was associated with lower odds of overweight (~12%), of unhealthy eating (~20%) and of disordered eating (~35%), and higher odds of healthy eating (~24%). [Secondary for design and the 24%; Memory, verify for the other percentages — PMC blocked]
- Evidence strength: **correlational**. Families who eat together differ in many ways (income, structure, parental warmth). Some longitudinal studies show associations shrink after adjusting for family factors. Present as "linked with", not "causes".
- AAP 2016 (Golden, Schneider, Wood — *Preventing Obesity and Eating Disorders in Adolescents*): family meals are protective; they don't have to be every night. [Secondary]
- Screens off at meals: TV during meals is linked with poorer diet quality and more eating without attention to fullness (observational). Low-cost, sensible recommendation. [Memory, verify]
- Children helping cook: small trials/observational studies show children who help prepare food are more willing to taste it, especially vegetables. Weak but consistent evidence. Age-appropriate tasks: washing vegetables (2–3 y), tearing lettuce, stirring, measuring (4–5 y), cutting soft foods with a child-safe knife (6+), simple recipes (8+). [Memory, verify]

## 7. Healthy eating 2–12

### Plate models
- **UK Eatwell Guide** (2016; for over-2s, fully from age 5): about a third fruit and vegetables, a third starchy carbs (wholegrain), plus protein, dairy/alternatives, small amount of unsaturated oils; 6–8 drinks/day, mostly water. [Memory, verify]
- **US MyPlate** (2011; Dietary Guidelines 2020–25): half the plate fruit and vegetables, grains (half whole), protein, dairy on the side; DGA says **no added sugar under 2**, <10% of energy from added sugar from 2 years. [Memory, verify]
- **Canada's Food Guide** (2019): half plate vegetables and fruit, a quarter protein foods (more plant-based), a quarter whole grains; **water as the drink of choice**; for children, emphasises cooking together and eating together. [Memory, verify]
- All three converge on: half plate vegetables/fruit, whole grains, varied protein, water as the main drink, limit ultra-processed/sugary foods. Use a generic "half veg & fruit, quarter grains, quarter protein, plus milk/yogurt" visual in the app (culturally neutral; fits Iranian rice/bread-based meals).

### Quantities and portions
- WHO fruit and vegetables: at least **250 g/day for 2–5 y**, **350 g/day for 6–9 y**, 400 g/day from 10 y. [Opened: WHO *Healthy diet* fact sheet, Jan 2026]
- Child-sized portions: a common rule of thumb is a portion ≈ size of the child's own palm (protein) or fist (carbs/fruit/veg); start small and allow seconds. Evidence: practical heuristic, not a formal guideline value. [Memory, verify — British Nutrition Foundation / Infant & Toddler Forum material]
- Toddlers do well with 3 meals + 2–3 planned snacks at set times; grazing all day blunts appetite. [Opened: NHS — 3 meals + 2 snacks from 12 months]

### Sugar, salt and drinks
- **WHO free sugars**: below 10% of energy for adults and children, with further benefit below 5% (~roughly 6 teaspoons for an adult; less for children). Strong recommendation for <10%, conditional for <5% (WHO 2015 guideline). Free sugars include honey, syrups and fruit juice, not sugar in whole fruit or milk. [Opened: WHO *Healthy diet* fact sheet; grading from Memory, verify]
- **Non-sugar sweeteners**: WHO doesn't recommend them as a replacement. [Opened]
- **Salt**: adult max 5 g/day; children's maximum scaled down by energy needs. UK figures (from memory): 1–3 y 2 g, 4–6 y 3 g, 7–10 y 5 g. [Opened: WHO; Memory, verify UK numbers]
- Drinks: water and plain milk; juice small and with meals if at all; no fizzy drinks, energy drinks, tea/coffee for young children. [Opened: NHS, CPS]
- Sweets and treats: neither banned nor used as reward. Offer them sometimes as part of a meal/snack, in a normal portion, without fuss; strict restriction increases desire for the forbidden food (restriction research, Fisher & Birch). [Memory, verify]

### Body talk and weight
- **AAP 2016** (Golden et al.): obesity and eating disorders share risk factors — **dieting, weight talk, weight teasing, body dissatisfaction**. Advice: discourage dieting and skipping meals; talk about healthy eating and activity, not weight; parents shouldn't talk about their own weight/dieting in front of children; promote positive body image; ask about bullying; encourage family meals. [Secondary: AJMC, News-Medical, Stony Brook copy of AAP News]
- App message: never comment on a child's (or anyone's) body size or shape; praise what bodies can do; no "good/bad food" moral labels; any concern about weight → talk to the health centre privately.

### Vegetarian (and vegan) children
- Well-planned vegetarian diets can meet children's needs; key nutrients: **iron** (pair legumes/greens with vitamin C), **zinc**, **vitamin B12** (supplement for vegans), **iodine** (iodised salt in tiny amounts / dairy), **calcium**, **vitamin D**, **omega-3**, and enough **energy** (bulky foods can fill small stomachs). Vegan diets for infants/toddlers: seek dietitian advice; WHO 2023 notes needs of 6–23-month-olds are hard to meet without animal-source foods. [Secondary: ENN for WHO point; Memory, verify for nutrient list (NHS/BDA)]
- Iran note: iodised salt is the main iodine source; Iran textbook says children under 1 get iodine from breast milk, so **breastfeeding mothers should use iodised refined salt**. [Opened]

### What Iran's MoH guidance says
- Exclusive breastfeeding to end of month 6 (180 days); breast milk supplies about half of needs at 6–12 months and a third at 12–24 months; complementary feeding runs from end of 6 months to 24 months. Start between 4–6 months **only** on health-worker advice when growth is faltering.
- **First food: rice-flour فرنی** (rice flour with breast milk or pasteurised cow's milk), first consistency like ordinary yogurt, gradually thicker. Then meat purée, then vegetable purées, soft ripe fruits (apple, pear, banana, cantaloupe, peach, mango) and **fully cooked whole egg (white and yolk)**; later mixed purées, soups, حلیم and آش; lentils and mung beans soaked and water discarded; pasteurised yogurt and low-salt pasteurised cheese.
  - Note: older Iranian pamphlets (and many grandparents) say "yolk only, no egg white before 1 year" — the 2021 MoH textbook now advises whole cooked egg, consistent with international allergen advice.
  - **Discrepancy to handle:** the textbook's فرنی recipe still includes "a little sugar per recipe", while the same book says not to add sugar to yogurt, soup, fruit or cooked vegetables, and WHO/NHS say no added sugar. Recommendation for PlayNest: suggest فرنی **without** added sugar (sweetness from breast milk/fruit), and say plainly that international advice is no added sugar under 2.
- 3–5 days between new foods (to spot reactions). [International bodies are less strict; ASCIA/CPS just say one new allergen at a time.]
- Finger foods from about 8–10 months (cooked vegetables, soft ripe fruit pieces, small pieces of meat).
- **No salt or spices** added to infant food; no honey under 1 year; no raw/undercooked egg.
- Cow's milk **as a drink is prohibited under 1 year** (low iron, high protein/sodium/potassium → kidney load, iron deficiency); allowed in measured amounts to make فرنی / حریره بادام.
- Fruit juice: avoid under 1 year (cites AAP); purées instead.
- **Tea and herbal infusions**: inhibit iron absorption, avoid as far as possible, don't use tea to soften food; if insisted on, only weak tea ≥2 hours away from meals; avoid **sweet tea** and industrial juices.
- Boiled, cooled water offered freely once solids start.
- Choking list includes gum, آب‌نبات (hard candy), large apple pieces, nuts (use powdered nuts instead), corn, raisins, raw carrot, celery, grapes, cherries, آلبالو.
- No force-feeding; no sweets/chocolate as reward.
- Supplements: A+D drops from 3–5 days to 24 months; iron from end of 6 months to 24 months (details in §4).
- Rice: cook in a large volume of water and drain, to cut arsenic.

### Common practices to be aware of (evidence of how often they happen)
- **Pre-lacteal and early fluids**: Iranian studies report sugar water (آب قند), نبات water, herbal infusions (e.g., ترنجبین / camel-thorn manna, خاکشیر / flixweed — often given for jaundice or "cleansing") and tea given in the first days/months. A Mashhad case-control study linked first-week sugar water, camel thorn and flixweed with breastfeeding problems. A Kerman 24-hour-recall study found ~37% of mothers gave fluids and ~61% formula in the first month. Reviews describe early complementary feeding in Iran/MENA as often involving sugar water and tea. [Secondary: search snippets of PMC4443393 and Iranian journals — not opened in full]
- Why it matters: extra fluids fill a small stomach and reduce milk intake, may reduce milk supply, add infection risk if water isn't safe, and herbal products can be contaminated; they do not treat newborn jaundice (jaundice needs medical assessment). Sugar water/نبات adds sugar with no benefit; tea reduces iron absorption.
- **Early cow's milk**: I found no recent Iranian data quantifying it; anecdotally common (diluted cow's milk in فرنی or as a drink). MoH stance is clear (no drinking cow's milk under 1).
- **فرنی as the classic first food**: fine and fits MoH guidance; add iron-rich foods (meat, egg, lentils) early because rice flour is low in iron (iron drops cover part of the gap in Iran's programme).
- **Sweet tea for toddlers** and tea with breakfast for school-age children is culturally normal; app can suggest milk/water with meals and, if tea is given to older children, weak, unsweetened, and away from meals.

## Disagreements to surface in the app copy

1. Allergen/solids start: 4 months for high-risk babies (BSACI, NIAID, ASCIA allow "not before 4 months") vs "around 6 months" (WHO, NHS, Iran MoH).
2. Testing before peanut in severe-eczema babies: NIAID 2017 "consider testing" vs BSACI/ASCIA/2021 consensus "don't delay for tests".
3. Cow's milk as a drink: WHO allows animal milk 6–11 months for non-breastfed; CPS 9–12 months; NHS, AAP, Iran: 12 months.
4. Vitamin D: UK supplements breastfed babies from birth (8.5–10 µg) and A, C, D to 5 y; US/Canada 400 IU for breastfed babies; Australia not routine; Iran A+D to 2 y for **all** infants.
5. Iron: Iran routine drops 6–24 m; US AAP drops from 4 m for exclusively breastfed (contested); UK/Canada/Australia rely on iron-rich foods.
6. Water: UK says unboiled tap water fine after 6 m; Iran MoH says boiled-cooled — local water safety decides.
7. Whole nuts: NHS under 5, AAP under 4.
8. Sugar in فرنی: Iran textbook recipe permits a little; WHO/NHS: no added sugar.
9. Gap between new foods: Iran 3–5 days; others one-at-a-time without a fixed wait.
