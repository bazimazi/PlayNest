### 1.3 Topic areas most hubs cover that an app like PlayNest might miss

PlayNest already covers play, stories, songs, guides, hard moments, first aid, crying, crisis lines, CDC milestones, routines and a journal. Measured against the matrix, these are the likely gaps. Check each one against the current content before writing anything new.

1. **Feeding & nutrition across ages**: weaning, picky eating, family meals, "no battles at the table". Every major hub has this.
2. **Sleep as a full topic by age**: not only the bedtime hard moment, but sleep needs, night waking, early rising and school-age sleep.
3. **Health basics beyond first aid**: when to see a doctor, fever, common illnesses, **teeth/oral health**, plus **vaccination and check-up reminders** (Bebbo, AAP, UNICEF and NHS all have these; Bebbo builds them in as offline trackers).
4. **Safety & injury prevention by age**: home, water, road/car seat, burns, poisoning, and safe sleep for babies.
5. **Screens, media & online safety**: a family media plan and online safety for ages 6–12. This is the No. 1 parent worry in Mott 2023 and a top stressor in Iran (see §2).
6. **School-age learning**: school readiness, homework, reading, and talking with teachers.
7. **Child mental health signs**: anxiety, worry, low mood, bullying, and when and where to get help (Pew and Mott top concerns).
8. **Body, puberty & body safety (ages 8–12)**: early puberty, body autonomy/consent, safe and unsafe touch.
9. **Family life & transitions**: a new sibling, separation/divorce, grief, moving, blended families, grandparents in the home. In the Iran data, "family living in the household" stressed 40% of parents.
10. **Co-parenting and fathers**: in ZERO TO THREE's survey, 40% of dads said their partner interferes with their involvement.
11. **Parent's own wellbeing** as a section, not only crisis lines: stress, anger and yelling, sleep deprivation, couple relationship. This is RCN's "Grown-ups" model.
12. **Developmental differences**: what to do if a milestone check raises a concern, plus autism and ADHD basics.
13. **Big conversations and parenting in hard times**: war or scary news, disasters, displacement, financial stress. UNICEF treats this as core, and it is especially relevant for Persian-speaking families.
14. **"What to expect at this age" expectation-setting**: self-control, sharing, emotions. Parents explicitly ask for this (see §2), and it should be woven into every goal.

## 2. What parents most often worry about or ask for, by age

### ZERO TO THREE, *Tuning In* national parent survey (fielded Oct 2015, published 2016; n = 2,200 US parents of children aged 0–5) [opened PDF]
- Parents want to learn: **87% work hard to be a better parent**, **83% think good parenting can be learned**, **69% would use more positive strategies if they knew them**, and 54% wish they had more information.
- **Expectation gap**: 43% think children can share and take turns before age 2, and 71% before age 3; the skill actually develops at 3–4. 36% think under-2s have the impulse control to resist something forbidden, and 56% think under-3s do; most children master this around 3.5–4.
- **What they want to learn about**: **47% how and when self-control develops**, and **42% what skills to expect at different ages**.
- **Discipline dilemma**: 57% struggle to find the most effective discipline. **42% wish they didn't yell as quickly, and 35% wish they didn't lose their temper so fast.** 30% spank even though they don't feel okay about it, and 77% of frequent spankers don't rate it as among the most effective methods.
- **"Missing first year"**: parents underestimate how early babies feel emotions and how early reading and talking matter. For example, 45% think reading only starts to help at 2+; the benefit actually starts around 6 months.
- **Trust gap**: 58% find it hard to know whom to trust. 63% distrust advice from people who don't know their child.
- **Judgement**: **90% of mothers and 85% of fathers feel judged**, and about 45% feel judged nearly all the time.
- **Support gap**: 48% don't get the support they need when stressed. Preferred delivery channels were email 39%, apps 34%, text 18%.
- **Dads**: 40% of dads (vs 17% of moms) say their co-parent interferes with their involvement.

### C.S. Mott Children's Hospital National Poll, "Top 10 child health concerns" (Aug 2023; fielded Feb 2023; n = 2,099 parents of children 0–18) [opened PDF]
Concerns rated a "big problem": device/screen overuse 67%, social media 66%, internet safety 62%, depression/suicide 57%, bullying 53%, stress/anxiety 52%, unhealthy diet 52%, healthcare costs 50%, school violence 49%, smoking/vaping 48%. Just outside the top 10: obesity 48%, and **parental stress 35%**. Lower-income parents rated more issues as big problems. Device and social-media worries were higher in middle- and high-income homes. A 2025 edition reportedly has social media and screen time tied at 75% [search].

### Pew Research Center, *Parenting in America Today* (Jan 2023; fielded 20 Sep – 2 Oct 2022; n = 3,757 US parents of under-18s) [opened]
- The top worry is children's **anxiety/depression** (about 4 in 10 extremely or very worried), followed by **bullying (35%)**. Both rank above physical dangers, drugs, etc.
- Parenting is **tiring** all or most of the time for 57% of parents whose youngest is under 5, 39% for ages 5–12, and 24% for teens. It is **stressful** for 35% of parents of under-5s.
- 62% say parenting is harder than expected. 64% rate themselves excellent or very good.

### Iran / Persian-language data
- **Co-SPACE Iran, Report 01** (Rajabi, 10 Jul 2020; n ≈ 1,100 self-selected parents of 4–18-year-olds, 74% mothers, collected during COVID) [opened PDF]. Main stressors: child's wellbeing 70%, child's future 66%, **child's screen time 62%**, living conditions 59%, **child's behaviour 53%**, child's education 50%, family members in the household 40%. 72% wanted advice. Wanted help with: **behaviour 60%**, **emotions 49%**, education demands 46%, family relationships 37%. Preferred formats: online written material 38%, TV 37%, online video 36%, online support from a professional 34%.
- A qualitative study of Tehran preschoolers' screen time (2023) points to family factors: parental health literacy, monitoring and home atmosphere [search]. A 2025 Iranian Journal of Psychiatry digital-parenting package covered online safety, supervision, screen balance and family communication [search]. A survey of 605 Iranian parents of 6–12-year-olds reported more shouting during COVID [search].

### Synthesis by age
- **0–2**: sleep, feeding, crying, "is this normal?", how early talking and reading matter, and parent exhaustion (57% of parents with an under-5 are tired most of the time).
- **2–5**: tantrums and self-control, **realistic expectations** (sharing, impulse control), discipline without yelling, routines, toilet training, early literacy.
- **6–12**: **screens, social media and online safety**, school and homework, **anxiety/worry**, **bullying**, friendships, diet, puberty onset.
- **All ages**: the parent's own temper and stress, feeling judged, co-parent disagreement, and trust in information sources.

## 3. How structured, sequenced programmes deliver skills, and what drives behaviour change

### 3.1 Ready4K / Tips-by-Text (ParentPowered)
- **York, Loeb & Doss (2019)**, *Journal of Human Resources* 54(3): 537–566 (NBER WP 20659, 2014) [opened abstract]. An 8-month text programme for parents of preschoolers. Parent involvement at home and school rose by 0.15–0.29 SD, and children's early-literacy gains were about 0.11 SD. The authors argue that long, information-heavy programmes overwhelm parents and that the binding constraint is **behavioural barriers**, not knowledge. Hence "one step at a time".
- **Structure** (J-PAL summary of Cortes, Fricke, Loeb, Song & York 2023, *Economics of Education Review* 92: 102352; n = 4,149 Dallas families) [opened]. Three message types each week: a **FACT** (why a skill matters), a **TIP** (a concrete activity, mostly built into **existing routines** such as meals, bath time and travel), and a **GROWTH** message (encouragement and a next step). Skills **progress from simple to advanced** across the programme. Earlier trials found **three texts a week worked best**, and that tailoring to the child's level helped.
- **Timing matters**: weekend delivery (Fri/Sat/Sun) helped lower-income families and lower-achieving children more than weekday delivery, while weekday delivery suited higher-achieving children. Light-touch nudges only work when the parent has the bandwidth to act.
- **Vendor materials** [opened]: "strengths-based" messages written at a 3rd-grade reading level, sequenced, delivered "in small, easy steps" to give an "I can do it" feeling. The 2+ month learning-gain claim is the vendor's own.

### 3.2 Implementation intentions (if-then plans)
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006)**, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38: 69–119 [opened abstract]. 94 independent tests. If-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65) over and above simply intending. They help at four points: **getting started**, **shielding** a goal from distraction, **disengaging** from a failing approach, and **saving self-control** for later. They work by making the cue salient and the response automatic.
- **Caveat** [search]: a 2024 meta-analysis (Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer, 642 tests) gives bias-corrected effects of roughly d = 0.15–0.35. The effect is real but smaller than the 2006 headline.
- **Design implication**: every practice should have an explicit cue ("When I…/When my child…, I will…"), ideally anchored to a routine PlayNest already knows (bedtime, the drive to school, meals).

### 3.3 Habit formation
- **Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010)**, *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40: 998–1009 [search; UCL page blocked]. 96 volunteers repeated a self-chosen daily behaviour for 84 days. The median time to plateau automaticity was **~66 days**, with a range of **18 to ~254** days (some values were model projections). **Missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation**; only very inconsistent people failed. Simpler behaviours (a glass of water) automated faster than complex ones (50 sit-ups).
- **Design implication**: a 6-week goal will not make every practice automatic, and that is fine. Ask for *consistency in the same context*, not perfection. Tell parents explicitly that a missed day or week does not undo progress.

### 3.4 Streaks, gamification and guilt
- **Silverman & Barasch (2023)**, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6) [opened university summary]. Across seven studies, showing an *intact* streak increased repeat behaviour, and showing a *broken* streak reduced it, regardless of actual past behaviour. The demotivation was **worse when people blamed themselves** and smaller when the streak could be repaired. Streaks become a goal in themselves, so a break feels like failure.
- Parents already feel judged (85–90% in ZERO TO THREE) and overwhelmed. A self-blaming streak break is exactly the mechanism to avoid.
- **Design implication**: use **no streak counters, no "you missed X days", no points or leaderboards**. Show cumulative "things you tried" instead, which can only grow. If any continuity is shown, attribute gaps to life ("busy weeks happen"), not to the parent.

### 3.5 Dropout in digital parenting programmes
- Reviews consistently report **low adherence and high dropout** in online parenting programmes. In-person programme attrition reaches about 60% in some meta-analyses and rises with child age and family disadvantage. Engagement is inconsistently measured, and design features linked to engagement are poorly documented [search: JMIR 2024 e49431, PMC11255536, PMC9399849, Springer s10567-023-00457-0].
- **Self-directed Triple P** (book or online) and **online PCIT / Incredible Years** have RCT support, with typically 8–10 modules for Triple P Online [background, not verified this session]. Their brief variants, such as Primary Care Triple P in about 4 short sessions, show that focused, single-problem sequences can work [background].
- **Design implication**: assume most parents will do 2–3 steps, not 6. Front-load the highest-value practice (connection or one-on-one time) in week 1. Make every step independently useful, and make re-entry frictionless.

## 4. Evidence for parent goal-setting itself

- **Family Check-Up (Dishion, Shaw et al.)** [opened: Gardner et al. 2009, *J Consult Clin Psychol* 77(3): 543–553]. A brief, roughly 3-session model: home assessment → **feedback session delivered with motivational interviewing**, which explores readiness to change, affirms existing strengths and identifies what the family wants to work on → optional follow-up sessions chosen collaboratively. **Over two-thirds of families chose goals about parenting, child development and behaviour.** Main effects are modest (d ≈ 0.18–0.24 on problem behaviour, maternal depression and positive parenting) but much larger for less-educated mothers (d up to 1.17 on CBCL externalising). About 80% attended the feedback session at age 2. A training outline (National Register webinar) [search] stresses **writing goals in the parent's own words** and using video to highlight strengths before goals are set.
- **Triple P self-regulatory model (Sanders & Mazzucchelli 2013)**, *Clin Child Fam Psychol Rev* 16(1): 1–17 [opened abstract]. A parent's ability to regulate their own behaviour underlies consistent, warm, non-harsh parenting, and the intervention aims to build "personal control and mastery". Later papers [search] list the five components: **self-sufficiency, self-efficacy, self-management** (parents set their own goals, monitor and evaluate), **personal agency** (credit change to one's own actions, not luck) and **problem-solving**. In practice, Triple P asks parents to choose which child behaviours to target and to self-monitor [background].
- **Takeaway**: the evidence supports *parent-chosen, specific, self-monitored* goals, framed through strengths and motivational interviewing, much more than prescribed curricula. Goal-setting is an ingredient of effective programmes; no standalone trial isolates it.

## 5. Design rules for the "Parenting goals" feature

**Choosing a goal**
1. **One goal at a time** (allow a second later). Offer 8–10 goals that map to what parents ask about: calmer bedtimes; fewer power struggles; calmer me (less yelling), since 42% of parents want this; handling big feelings; siblings getting along; healthy eating without battles; screen balance; raising a reader; more connection/one-on-one time; school-morning routine.
2. **Let the parent say why in their own words** (Family Check-Up). Ask one optional free-text question: "What would be different if this went better?" Show it back on the goal card and in later reflections.
3. **Start from strengths**. Before step 1, ask "What's already working?" with tappable chips. This is the strength-affirming part of motivational interviewing.
4. **Set realistic expectations up front**. Each goal opens with a 2-line "what's typical at this age" panel, using the child's age from the profile. This targets the expectation gap.

**Sequence shape**
5. **5–6 steps, one practice per step, about one week per step**, so the whole goal takes 5–8 weeks. That matches brief evidence-based models (3–4 sessions for Family Check-Up and Primary Care Triple P) and Ready4K's one-skill-at-a-time sequencing. Steps go from easiest to harder. **Step 1 is always connection** (special time or praise), because it helps everything else and is the best single step if the parent drops out.
6. **Each step mirrors Ready4K's FACT → TIP → GROWTH**:
   - **Why** (1–2 sentences, the "fact"; Harvard/CDC style).
   - **This week's practice**: one tiny, concrete action of 2–10 minutes, **anchored to an existing routine** (bath, dinner, car ride, bedtime).
   - **If-then plan**: pre-filled and editable, e.g. "When I hear 'I don't want to!' at shoes-on time, I will offer two choices." Let the parent pick the cue from 2–3 options.
   - **A "what if it's hard" line**: a common obstacle and a fallback (a mental-contrasting element).
   - **Links** to 1–3 existing items (a hard-moment card, guide, play idea or story).
   - **Mid-week nudge and end-week reflection** (the "growth" message), with encouragement and a peek at next week.
7. **Let the parent choose when nudges arrive.** Offer a weekend option, since weekend timing helped busy and lower-income families in the Cortes et al. trial. Default to at most 2–3 local notifications per week, and make them easy to turn off.
8. **Steps unlock by choice, not by time.** At week's end, offer: "Next step", "Stay on this one another week", or "Swap for an easier version". Never auto-advance.

**Missed weeks, without guilt**
9. **No streaks, no day counters, no "missed" badges, no points.** Show a growing list of "things you tried" and a timeline of reflections. Silverman & Barasch show that broken streaks demotivate, especially when people blame themselves.
10. **The re-entry message is neutral and normalising.** For example: "Busy weeks happen. Pick up where you left off, or start this step again." Mention that habit research shows a missed day doesn't undo progress (Lally 2010). Never count the gap.
11. **Pausing is a first-class action** ("Pause this goal", e.g. illness, travel, a new baby). A paused goal stays visible and doesn't nag. Suggest a different goal or a "maintenance" check-in instead of failure framing.

**Measuring progress without scores**
12. **A before and after "snapshot" of 2–3 parent-rated items specific to the goal, using a 3–5 point word scale** (e.g. "Bedtime usually: calm / mixed / hard"; "How confident do I feel handling it?"). Take it at the start, at the mid-point and at the end, and show it as words or icons side by side, **never as a percentage or grade**. Self-efficacy is itself an outcome in Triple P.
13. **Weekly reflection = one tap plus optional note**: "Tried it a few times / Once / Not this week. That's OK." Then "One thing that went well". Notes go into the existing journal.
14. **Prompt personal agency**: "What did *you* do that helped?" Help parents credit change to their own actions, and include partners or co-parents in the framing.
15. **Close with a completion summary, not a score**: what you tried, the parent's own words from the start, the before/after snapshot, and "keep doing / next goal" suggestions.

**Safety and scope**
16. Every goal links to **when to get more help** (child mental-health signs, developmental concerns, parent crisis lines). If the snapshot stays "hard" after the sequence, show a gentle "This might need more support" card. This is not a diagnosis.
17. **Offline, private, and no account.** All goal data stays on the device. This matches Bebbo's offline model and the trust gap in parent surveys.
18. **Plain language** (about grade 3–6 reading level, like Ready4K), fully bilingual EN/FA, and culturally adapted. For Persian families, include extended-family-in-the-home, grandparent and emergency/hard-times contexts.
