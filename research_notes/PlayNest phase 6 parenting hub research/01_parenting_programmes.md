# 01 — Evidence-based parenting programmes and their core skills

Research notes for the PlayNest "Parenting skills" area (children 0–12, English + Persian).
Compiled 16 Nov 2026. All findings are paraphrased. Each claim is tied to a source that was opened in this round. Where a
source was blocked or only a search snippet was seen, that is flagged.

**Access notes.** PubMed blocked automated access (cookie wall), so abstracts were read through the Europe PMC REST API
(same records, same PMIDs). The AAP *Pediatrics* site returned HTTP 403, so the AAP 2018 statement is summarised from
its Europe PMC abstract only. The Incredible Years developer PDFs and the University of Iowa PCIT handout downloaded as
compressed PDFs that could not be read; the IY content below comes from the Blueprints registry page instead. The WHO
2023 parenting guideline landing page lists the documents but not the recommendation wording.

## 1. Which programmes have the strongest evidence, and what do they share?

### 1.1 Programme-by-programme snapshot

| Programme | Ages | Evidence status (honest) | Key source opened |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Triple P** (Positive Parenting Program, multi-level, Australia) | 0–16 | **Strong** but with developer-led bias concerns. Meta-analysis of 101 studies (16,099 families): child outcomes d≈0.47, parenting practices d≈0.58, long-term effects still significant. A 2026 Canadian online RCT found short-term gains that mostly faded after post-test. | Sanders et al. 2014, *Clin Psychol Rev*; Gonzalez et al. 2026, *JCPP* |
| **Incredible Years (IY)** (USA) | 0–12 (baby/toddler, preschool, school-age versions) | **Strong.** Independent IPD meta-analysis of 14 European trials (N=1,799): improved praise and reduced corporal punishment, threats and shouting. Reduced child conduct problems and ADHD symptoms. No harm. Did *not* improve tangible rewards, parental mental health or child emotional problems. Blueprints rates it only "Promising" (strict certification). | Leijten et al. 2018, *JCPP*; Blueprints registry |
| **PCIT** (USA) | ~2–7 | Strong clinical evidence base (widely accepted). In this round only the skill descriptions (PRIDE) were verified, not effect sizes. | PCIT International / pcit.org; Iowa handout (unreadable) |
| **PMTO / GenerationPMTO** (Oregon) | 3–18 | Widely regarded as well established. **Not verified this round:** the Europe PMC search for a PMTO meta-analysis returned nothing. Treat as "well regarded, not re-checked". | — |
| **WHO/UNICEF Parenting for Lifelong Health (PLH)** | Infants, toddlers 1–5, young children 2–9, teens 10–17 | **Moderate–strong for LMICs.** Open-access, non-commercial, scaled in 20+ LMICs. South Africa RCT (ages 2–9, n=296, 12 sessions): observed positive parenting up 39% at 4–5 months and 24% at 17 months; many child-behaviour measures null. | **Tuning in to Kids (TIK)** — emotion coaching (Australia) | 2–12 (mostly 3–6) | **Moderate.** Most trials are small, use wait-lists and parent report, and the developer co-authored 23 of 25 TIK studies. | **Care for Child Development (CCD)** (WHO/UNICEF Nurturing Care) | 0–5 | **Moderate, mixed.** Implemented in 54 LMICs. **Iranian RCTs exist** (Isfahan, n=181): a small cognitive benefit at 12 months and less anxiety/depression at 18 months. At 48 months only sleep problems still differed; the authors call long-term efficacy weak. | Bemanalizadeh et al. 2022; Talebi Anaraki et al. 2024, *BMC Pediatrics*; Ahun et al. 2023 |

### 1.2 Cross-programme meta-analyses: the "active ingredients"

- **Kaminski et al. 2008 (CDC; *J Abnorm Child Psychol*; 77 evaluations, ages 0–7).** After controlling for study
  design, four things predicted larger effects:
  1. Increasing positive parent–child interactions.
  2. Teaching emotional communication skills.
  3. Teaching time-out and consistency.
  4. Having parents **practise with their own child during sessions**.

  Programmes that taught problem solving, academic/cognitive promotion, or added extra services had *smaller* effects.
  This is still the clearest "what to include" finding.
- **Leijten et al. 2019 (*JAACAP*; 154 trials, 26 techniques coded).** Three techniques were tied to stronger effects:
  positive reinforcement, especially **praise**, and **natural/logical consequences**. Relationship-building and parental
  self-management helped in treatment samples but *not* in prevention (universal) samples. No technique predicted
  stronger *long-term* effects (42 trials). Effects scale with need: universal d≈0.21, treatment d≈0.69.
- **Leijten et al. 2018 (*PLoS ONE*; 19 studies of single discrete behaviours).**
  - **Time-out** robustly increased compliance (d 0.84–1.72).
  - **Briefly ignoring** non-compliance helped (d 0.36–1.77).
  - **Praise alone did not increase compliance.**
- **Laas Sigurðardóttir et al. 2025 (*JAACAP*; IPD, 14 European RCTs, 3,252 families, ages 1–13).** Programme effects on
  disruptive behaviour were carried mostly by **less harsh verbal discipline** and **better follow-through**. Praise was a
  *null* mediator. Families with the fewest *and* the most difficulties benefited least.
- **Leijten et al. 2026 (*JCPP*; 244 RCTs, 28,916 families, 36 countries).** Effects fell early in the literature, then
  stabilised. They are not improving. Newer trials use time-out less, but that does not explain the decline. The authors
  call for experimentation with content, delivery and personalisation.
- **Kjøbli et al. 2023 (*JCPP*; network meta-analysis, 69 trials).** Behaviour-focused programmes give only a small extra
  benefit for child *emotional* problems (d≈-0.14, fading). Combining behaviour management **with** relationship
  enhancement was most likely to be best.
- **Gardner, Montgomery & Knerr 2016 (*JCCAP*; 17 trials, 10 countries).** When Western programmes (IY, Triple P, PCIT,
  PMTO) were transported to new countries, randomised trials still showed SMD≈-0.71. Effects were **larger** in culturally
  more distant countries and in those with "survival" values. Heavy cultural adaptation did not seem necessary. This is
  directly relevant to Iran.
- **Backhaus et al. 2023 (*EClinicalMedicine*; 60 RCTs with violence outcomes, 22% LMIC).** Effects on physical and
  emotional violence were d=-0.46 right after the programme, -0.24 at 1–6 months and -0.18 at 7–24 months. They shrink
  but persist. Outcomes were mostly self-reported.
- **Backhaus, Blackwell & Gardner 2025 (*Child Abuse Negl*; 23 RCTs in humanitarian LMIC settings).** Violence d=-0.36,
  positive parenting d=0.48, parental mental health d=-0.34, child internalising d=-0.38. Externalising was not
  significant.
- **WHO Guidelines on parenting interventions (Feb 2023; handbook Jul 2024).** WHO recommends parenting interventions for
  ages 0–17 in all country income levels, to reduce maltreatment and harsh parenting and to improve the relationship and
  parent mental health. Recommendation wording and GRADE ratings sit in the PDF and web annex, which were not opened.

**Synthesis: the shared core, in roughly the order programmes teach it.**
1. Positive time together and attention (play, special time, describing, warmth).
2. Praise and rewards for wanted behaviour.
3. Clear instructions, rules and routines.
4. Mild, non-violent consequences for misbehaviour (ignoring minor misbehaviour, logical consequences, brief time-out).
5. Consistency and follow-through.
6. Emotion talk / coaching.
7. Parental calm and self-care.
8. Active practice between sessions.

The IY pyramid puts relationship and play at the broad base and discipline tools at the narrow top. The Blueprints page
confirms the content (child-directed play, praise, rewards, routines, limit-setting, logical consequences, coaching,
problem solving) and that the pyramid sets the course order, but it does not reproduce the figure. PLH's public summary
describes the same arc: "play and praise" first, then positive discipline.

## 2. Core techniques: what, how, ages, evidence, cautions

Evidence-strength key:
- **Strong** = supported by component meta-analyses or experimental studies.
- **Moderate** = consistently part of effective packages, with some direct support.
- **Weak** = mostly theory or clinical consensus.
- **Mixed** = conflicting findings.

### 2.1 Child-led special time (one-on-one time)
- **What:** A short, daily, predictable block where the child leads the play and the parent follows with full attention.
- **How:**
  1. Do it 5–10 minutes daily (PCIT uses 5 minutes; PLH says any amount, daily).
  2. Phone away; pick toys that suit creative play (blocks, drawing, dolls), not rule-based games.
  3. Let the child choose and lead.
  4. Use the PRIDE skills: **P**raise specific behaviour; **R**eflect (repeat or paraphrase) what the child says;
     **I**mitate their play; **D**escribe what they are doing like a sportscaster; **E**njoy with real warmth.
  5. Avoid questions, commands and criticism (these take the lead away).
  6. Ignore minor silliness. Stop the session calmly if the child becomes aggressive or destructive.
- **Ages:** About 2–8 is the PCIT target. Adapt for infants (follow their gaze and babble, as in VIPP-SD and Reach Up)
  and for 9–12s (child chooses the activity; more talk, less narration).
- **Evidence:** **Moderate–strong.** "Increasing positive interactions" was a top component in Kaminski 2008. Leijten
  2019 found relationship-building helps in treatment samples but not universal ones. Kjøbli 2023 found behaviour plus
  relationship work was best for emotional problems.
- **Cautions:** Describing every move can feel unnatural to older children. Keep it short and sustainable. Do not use
  special time as a reward to remove (it is unconditional).

### 2.2 Labelled (specific) praise
- **What:** Praise that names the exact behaviour ("Thank you for putting the cars in the box straight away").
- **How:**
  1. Catch the behaviour you want more of.
  2. Praise it within seconds, naming the action.
  3. Praise effort and process more than traits.
  4. Keep the ratio of positives clearly above corrections.
  5. Pair praise with a smile or touch.
- **Ages:** All. For infants, warm responsive talk; for older children, more understated and private.
- **Evidence:** **Mixed–moderate.** Leijten 2019 found programmes with praise had stronger effects. But Leijten 2018 found
  praise alone did not raise compliance, and the 2025 IPD found praise was not a mediator of reduced disruptive
  behaviour. Interpretation: praise is relationship "fuel" and a reinforcer for new habits, but it is not the lever that
  stops defiance.
- **Cautions:** Avoid sarcastic "praise with a sting" ("Good job, finally"). Avoid empty, constant "good job".
  Person-praise ("you're so clever") may undermine persistence (theory-based; not checked this round).

### 2.3 Attending / describing
- **What:** Narrating the child's activity without directing it.
- **How:** "You're stacking the red block on the blue one… now it's tall." No questions or instructions.
- **Ages:** 1–7 mostly. For infants, describing and naming objects supports language (the Reach Up/CCD style of
  responsive talk).
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** as part of PCIT/IY packages. Reach Up's developmental gains (language 0.38 SD) come partly
  from responsive talk and play. The technique was not isolated in a component analysis.
- **Cautions:** Same as special time. Drop it if the child finds it irritating.

### 2.4 Clear, calm instructions
- **What:** Short, specific, positively phrased directions given one at a time.
- **How:**
  1. Get close and get the child's attention (name, eye level).
  2. Say what *to do*, not what to stop ("Walk, please" instead of "Don't run").
  3. Give one step at a time, as a statement, not a question.
  4. Give warnings before transitions.
  5. Wait about 5 seconds.
  6. Praise compliance. If there is no compliance, follow through (see consequences).
- **Ages:** 2+.
- **Evidence:** **Moderate (indirect).** Clear instructions are in every effective package, and the WHO/PLH tips state
  that positive instructions plus praise raise compliance. The **follow-through** half is strongly supported: it is a key
  mediator in the 2025 IPD and "consistency" is in Kaminski. Instruction wording itself was not isolated in the opened
  meta-analyses.
- **Cautions:** Too many commands is a common problem. Do not give instructions you will not follow through on.

### 2.5 Routines and predictable rules
- **What:** Regular daily rhythms (sleep, meals, screens, homework) and a few clear household rules.
- **How:**
  1. Set 3–5 rules, positively worded.
  2. Use a visual routine chart for young children.
  3. Give a warning before changes.
  4. Involve older children in setting the rules.
- **Ages:** All (infant sleep and feeding rhythms through to school-age homework routines).
- **Evidence:** **Moderate–weak as an isolated component.** Routines are part of IY, PLH ("structure") and CCD (an Iranian
  quasi-experiment found mothers improved on "predictable routines" and responsiveness). There is no component-level test
  showing routines alone change behaviour.
- **Cautions:** Keep routines flexible. Cultural and household variation (extended family, shared rooms) is normal.

### 2.6 Planned ignoring of minor misbehaviour
- **What:** Deliberately withdrawing attention from harmless attention-seeking behaviour (whining, mild tantrums,
  silliness), then re-engaging as soon as it stops.
- **How:**
  1. Decide in advance which behaviours to ignore.
  2. Look away and stay neutral, with no talking.
  3. Expect a short "extinction burst" (it gets worse briefly).
  4. Attend and praise the moment the child is calm or behaves well.
- **Ages:** About 2–8.
- **Evidence:** **Moderate–strong** for short-term compliance (Leijten 2018: d 0.36–1.77). It is part of the IY pyramid
  and PCIT.
- **Cautions:** Never ignore danger, aggression, distress, fear or pain, or a child's genuine needs. Ignoring is of the
  behaviour, not the child. It is ineffective if the payoff is not adult attention.

### 2.7 Rewards, sticker charts and token systems
- **What:** Small, planned incentives for specific target behaviours, faded as the habit forms.
- **How:**
  1. Pick 1–3 positively stated targets ("Stays in bed after lights out").
  2. Make steps small enough that the child succeeds early.
  3. Reward immediately with a sticker plus praise.
  4. Trade tokens for cheap or activity rewards (time with a parent rather than toys).
  5. Never remove earned stickers as punishment.
  6. **Fade:** raise the criterion, move to intermittent rewards, then to praise alone, over weeks.
  7. Refresh or replace the chart when interest drops.
- **Ages:** About 3–10 (charts); points or contracts for 8–12.
- **Evidence:** **Moderate.** "Positive reinforcement" was one of the 3 techniques tied to stronger effects (Leijten
  2019), and tangible rewards were a unique mediator in the 2025 IPD. But the IY IPD found programmes did not change
  parents' use of tangible rewards. Fading guidance is clinical consensus, not trial-tested.
- **Cautions:** Rewards for things the child already enjoys could reduce intrinsic interest (the overjustification
  debate; not checked this round). Avoid bribes offered mid-tantrum. Avoid sibling comparison charts.

### 2.8 Logical and natural consequences
- **What:** A calm, brief, related, proportionate outcome for misbehaviour. A natural consequence is letting a safe
  outcome happen (no coat, feels cold). A logical consequence is related and imposed (toy thrown, toy put away for the
  afternoon).
- **How:**
  1. Warn first ("If…then").
  2. Follow through calmly and without lecturing.
  3. Keep it short (minutes to a day, not weeks).
  4. Give a fresh start afterwards.
- **Ages:** About 3–12. Natural consequences only where safe.
- **Evidence:** **Moderate–strong.** One of the 3 techniques tied to stronger programme effects (Leijten 2019).
  Follow-through is a key mediator (2025 IPD).
- **Cautions:** Consequences delivered in anger turn into punishment. Unrelated or long consequences lose effect.

### 2.9 Time-out (brief, calm, non-exclusionary where possible)
- **What:** A short removal from attention and reinforcement after a clear warning, used for aggression or persistent
  non-compliance.
- **How:**
  1. Give a warning.
  2. If the behaviour continues, use a calm short phrase and a boring, safe spot, roughly 1 minute per year of age
     (max about 5).
  3. The parent stays calm and nearby.
  4. Once the time is up and the child is quiet, re-engage warmly and give the original instruction again.
  5. Use it **only** inside a relationship rich in special time and praise.
- **Ages:** About 2–8.
- **Evidence:** **Strong for compliance** (Kaminski 2008 component; Leijten 2018 d 0.84–1.72). No harm when used
  appropriately in observational studies: a 2025 *Br J Psychiatry* survey found appropriate time-out was linked to *better*
  mental health and attachment, while inappropriate use was linked to worse outcomes. A 2020 Early Head Start
  longitudinal study found no differences. Dadds & Tully 2019 (*Am Psychol*) argue that attachment-harm claims do not hold
  for proper use.
- **Cautions:** This is the **most contested** item publicly, because "gentle parenting" and attachment advocates
  criticise it. Use is declining in newer trials (Leijten 2026). Harm signals come from **inappropriate** use: long,
  angry, frightening or isolating time-outs, locking a child in a room, or using it for distress or fear. Do not use it
  for infants or toddlers under 2, or for emotional upset as opposed to misbehaviour. A "time-in" or calm-down corner is
  a reasonable softer framing.

### 2.10 Emotion coaching
- **What:** Noticing a child's emotion, naming it, empathising, and helping them problem-solve, while still setting
  limits on behaviour.
- **How:**
  1. Notice low-intensity emotions early.
  2. See the emotion as a chance to connect.
  3. Name it ("You look disappointed").
  4. Validate the feeling.
  5. Set limits on actions if needed ("You can be angry; you can't hit").
  6. Once the child is calm, problem-solve together.
- **Ages:** 2–12 (most TIK trials are at ages 3–6; there is also an online TIK for ages 4–10).
- **Evidence:** **Moderate.**
  - Zahl-Olsen 2023 (33 studies): parent emotion-oriented parenting d≈0.41, child externalising d≈0.31, internalising
    d≈0.25. Effects are small.
  - England-Mason 2023 (15 RCTs, ages 1.5–7): child emotional competence g=0.44, behaviour g=0.34.
  - Caveats: developer-led trials, parent report and wait-list controls.
  - Kaminski 2008 also found "emotional communication" a strong component.
  - A 2026 factorial trial singled out *empathetic responding* as the most promising single component for child anxiety.
- **Cautions:** Cultural fit. A TIK pilot in **Iran**, Turkey, Germany and China found parents' beliefs about showing
  emotion and the emotion vocabulary available in the language shaped uptake. The fixes were in delivery (slower pace,
  more practice), not content. This is highly relevant for Persian copy: give parents a ready emotion-word list.

### 2.11 Parent stress management and self-care
- **What:** Brief strategies so the parent can stay calm in hard moments and look after their own wellbeing.
- **How:**
  1. Pause and breathe before responding (the PLH "keep calm" tip).
  2. Notice your own triggers.
  3. Plan for known flashpoints.
  4. Get support (partner, family, groups).
  5. Do daily small self-care.
  6. Seek help for depression or anxiety.
- **Ages:** Applies to all parents.
- **Evidence:** **Mixed.**
  - Self-management helps in treatment samples, not prevention (Leijten 2019).
  - In online programmes, the strongest clusters include parental self-care (Canário 2025).
  - Group programmes improved parent depression, individual ones did not (Mathijs 2024).
  - The IY IPD found no change in parental mental health; Reach Up found no change in maternal depression.
  - Reducing **overreactive and harsh** responses matters most: it is the strongest mediator (2025 IPD) and central in a
    digital programme network study (Baumel 2026).
- **Cautions:** An app is not treatment for postnatal depression. Signpost to professional help.

### 2.12 Repair after conflict
- **What:** After a parent loses their temper, reconnecting: acknowledging what happened, apologising for one's own
  part, and restating the limit and the love.
- **How:**
  1. Calm down first.
  2. Return to the child: "I shouted. That wasn't OK. I was frustrated, and it's my job to stay calm. I love you."
  3. Briefly revisit the issue.
  4. Do not over-explain or blame the child.
- **Ages:** All (simple words for under-5s).
- **Evidence:** **Weak as a taught component.** It is grounded in developmental theory and features in attachment-based
  programmes such as Circle of Security, whose trial evidence is null (2026 meta-analysis). No opened trial isolates
  repair. It is low-risk and plausibly reduces guilt and harsh-cycle escalation. Wang & Kenny 2014 show harsh discipline
  and child misbehaviour feed each other, which gives a rationale for breaking the cycle.
- **Cautions:** Repair does not cancel out frequent harshness (warmth did *not* buffer the harm of harsh verbal discipline
  in Wang & Kenny).

## 3. Physical punishment and harsh verbal discipline

- **WHO fact sheet "Corporal punishment and health"** (originally Nov 2021; the version opened is dated **11 May 2026**).
  - About **1.2 billion** children (0–18) experience corporal punishment at home each year, and about 17% experience
    severe forms in a given month.
  - Linked harms include injury, anxiety and depression, poorer cognitive and emotional development, school dropout, and
    more aggression and later violence.
  - WHO recommends law reform plus norm change, parent support (INSPIRE), and early intervention. It notes that bans
    alone do not always reduce prevalence.
- **AAP policy "Effective Discipline to Raise Healthy Children"** (Sege & Siegel, *Pediatrics* 2018; 142(6):e20183112).
  - Aversive strategies, including **all corporal punishment and yelling or shaming**, are minimally effective short
    term and not effective long term, and are linked to worse behavioural, cognitive and emotional outcomes.
  - Parents should avoid physical punishment and verbal abuse; paediatricians should teach age-appropriate positive
    discipline.
  - Full text was blocked (403), so the specific alternatives listed were not verified.
- **Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor 2016** (*J Fam Psychol*; 111 effect sizes, 160,927 children). 13 of 17 outcomes were
  significantly worse with spanking, none better. Effect sizes for spanking were similar to those for physical abuse and
  robust across study designs.
- **Heilmann et al. 2021** (*Lancet*; 69 prospective studies). Physical punishment predicts *increasing* behaviour
  problems over time and no positive outcomes. There are signs of a dose–response relationship. "Child effects" (children
  eliciting punishment) explain only part of the externalising link.
- **Full bans.** The End Corporal Punishment countdown page (undated, live in Nov 2026) lists **70 states** with full
  prohibition including the home. The latest additions in 2025 were Thailand, Czech Republic and Switzerland. A search
  snippet attributed "68 as of April 2025" to WHO (not opened).
  - In the **MENA region only Tunisia (2010) and Israel (2000)** have full bans.
  - **Iran has no full ban** and is listed among states where judicial whipping or flogging of juveniles remains lawful.
  - Content implication: frame for Persian-speaking parents with health and effectiveness evidence, not legal
    obligation.
- **Harsh verbal discipline — Wang & Kenny 2014** (*Child Dev*; 976 two-parent US families). Shouting, cursing and
  insults at age 13 predicted *increases* in conduct problems and depressive symptoms by 14. The effect ran both ways:
  misbehaviour also predicted more harsh verbal discipline. **Parental warmth did not buffer the harm.** This matches the
  2025 IPD finding that reducing harsh verbal discipline is a main route by which programmes work.

## 4. Parenting styles (Baumrind) — what holds up

- **Holds up:**
  - Pinquart & Kauser 2018 (*Cult Divers Ethnic Minor Psychol*; 428 studies) found that in **every world region**
    authoritative parenting (warm + firm, with explanations) was linked to at least one better child outcome, and
    authoritarian parenting to at least one worse outcome.
  - Kauser & Pinquart 2019 ran a **Pakistani** RCT (n=110) of an indigenous programme. It raised authoritative parenting
    and reduced child problems, partly through that change.
- **Overstated:**
  - Effects are **small**. In Pinquart & Lauk 2025 (184 studies on substance use) authoritative parenting correlated only
    r≈-0.12.
  - The style typology is correlational, and child temperament drives parenting too (see Wang & Kenny's bidirectional
    effects).
  - Style labels are broad. Programmes teach specific behaviours, which is more actionable than "be authoritative".
- **Cross-cultural caveats:**
  - Associations are weaker in more individualistic countries.
  - Among minority groups in Western countries, the authoritarian–achievement link is less negative for Hispanic
    families, and the authoritative–achievement link is weaker for Asian-heritage families (Pinquart & Kauser 2018).
  - Transported programmes work at least as well in culturally distant countries (Gardner 2016).
- **Iran / Middle East evidence:**
  - Mostly small Triple P RCTs: ADHD mothers, n=30, 2014; preschool aggression, n=52, 2025; intellectual disability
    samples. All are positive but tiny, single-site and often with weak reporting.
  - CCD RCTs in Isfahan: modest, short-lived effects.
  - TIK cultural-adaptation pilot including Iran.
  - Israel digital parent training (Baumel and colleagues).
  - Overall, Iranian evidence is supportive but low quality. International evidence plus Gardner 2016 are the better
    basis.

## 5. "Gentle parenting" and "positive parenting" — evidence vs marketing

- **"Positive parenting"** is not just a slogan. It is the name and umbrella of tested programmes (Triple P is literally
  the *Positive Parenting Program*), and in WHO/AAP usage it means the core set in §1.2, which **includes** limits,
  consequences and time-out.
- **"Gentle parenting"** has **no trials** of its own. The first systematic study (Pezalla & Davidson 2024, *PLoS ONE*;
  100 US parents of 2–7-year-olds) found:
  - It is defined by high affection and joint emotion regulation, overlapping with authoritative parenting and emotion
    coaching.
  - It varies in whether boundaries are actually enforced.
  - **More than a third of self-identified gentle parents reported uncertainty or burnout**, and self-critical parents
    felt less effective.
- **Verdict:** The warm, emotion-validating parts are evidence-aligned (emotion coaching, no hitting, no shouting). The
  parts that are marketing or not evidence-based are:
  - blanket rejection of time-out, ignoring, rewards and consequences, which conflicts with the component evidence in
    §1.2 and the 2025 time-out studies;
  - implied promises of "no tantrums";
  - standards of never losing your temper, which feed parental guilt.
- **App framing:** "Warm *and* firm". Teach repair rather than perfection.

## 6. Learning skills from an app or self-directed format

- **Thongseiratch, Leijten & Melendez-Torres 2020** (*Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry*; 12 studies, n=2,025). Online
  programmes reduced child behaviour problems g=-0.32, emotional problems g=-0.22, parental mental health g=-0.30.
  **Reminders prompting parents to work on the programme were the only component found sufficient.** Build reminders in.
- **Spencer, Topham & King 2020** (*J Fam Psychol*; 28 studies). Online programmes improved positive parenting and
  encouragement most, and reduced negative discipline, child behaviour problems and anxiety. **No differences** with
  vs without clinician support on six outcomes.
- **Baumel et al. 2016** (*J Child Adolesc Psychopharmacol*; 7 RCTs). Digital parent training ES=0.44 for child
  behaviour (0.61 in young children with clinical-range symptoms). Interactive programmes beat non-interactive ones for
  younger children.
- **Baumel et al. 2023** (*Internet Interv*; pilot RCT, n=88, unguided). Adding "therapeutic persuasiveness" features
  (reminders, feedback, progress) raised **completion from 28% to 69%** and child outcomes favoured the enhanced version
  (d≈0.43–0.54). Engagement is the main problem in self-guided apps.
- **Brandes et al. 2026.** When parents freely chose modules, 91.5% picked non-recommended ones without gaining benefit.
  Guided tailoring works better than full free choice.
- **Canário et al. 2025** (*Prev Sci*; network meta-analysis, 27 RCTs, N=5,312). Moderate effects. Strongest clusters were
  learning-theory (behavioural) content plus parent self-care and a "parent as coach" approach.
- **Gonzalez et al. 2026** (*JCPP*; n=502, online Triple P vs COS-P vs usual care). Triple P had short-term effects only;
  COS-P had almost none. Expect fading, so plan "booster" content.
- **Text-message programmes.** Ready4K / ParentPowered (York, Loeb & Doss 2019, *J Hum Resour*; abstract only) raised
  parental involvement by 0.15–0.29 SD and child early literacy by about 0.11 SD with weekly texts built around
  behavioural barriers. Small but cheap. ParentText (PLH chatbot over WhatsApp) has a large optimisation trial in South
  Africa; results were not found. **Vroom** was not checked this round (no evaluation opened).
- **Kaminski 2008** reminder: **practising with your own child** was among the strongest components, so an app should
  turn every skill into a tiny practice task ("Today: 5 minutes of special time; tap when done") rather than only text.
- **Mathijs et al. 2024:** group programmes improved parent depression and individual ones did not. A solo app may
  under-deliver on parent wellbeing, so signpost to groups and services.

---

## 7. Implications for PlayNest (draft)

1. **Order modules like the pyramid:**
   1. Special time + describing + labelled praise.
   2. Clear instructions + routines.
   3. Rewards/charts (with fading).
   4. Planned ignoring.
   5. Logical consequences + calm time-out (2–8 only; strict safety rules).
   6. Emotion coaching (can run in parallel from ages 2+).
   7. Parent calm/self-care and repair, woven throughout.
2. **Tag each technique by age band** (0–1, 1–3, 3–6, 6–9, 9–12) and hide time-out, ignoring and sticker charts for
   infants.
3. **Practice + reminders** are the evidence-backed app ingredients: daily micro-tasks, gentle reminders, progress
   feedback, guided rather than free module choice.
4. **Be explicit and evidence-led on no hitting / no shouting**, with repair guidance. Avoid legal framing for Iran.
5. **Present evidence levels honestly in-app** (e.g., "well-tested" / "promising" / "expert advice"). Do not present
   Circle of Security-style attachment claims or "gentle parenting" as evidence-based programmes.
6. **Persian emotion vocabulary list** for emotion coaching (TIK Iran adaptation finding).
7. **Mental-health signposting** for parent depression or anxiety. App effects on parent wellbeing are small.
