## Cross-cutting messages for the house style

1. **The relationship between the adults is part of the child's environment.** The child's sense of safety depends heavily on how the adults treat each other, not only on how they treat the child. Say this gently. It is a reason for hope, not for guilt.
2. **"Repair" is the unifying idea.** Couples who argue and then make up, parents who shout and then apologise, and parent–baby pairs who fall out of sync and then reconnect all show the same thing: repair is normal and protective. The goal is not to be perfect.
3. **Evidence strength varies a lot across this shelf.**
   - Strong: interparental conflict research, harsh verbal discipline associations, the post-baby dip in couple satisfaction, and the prevalence of paternal depression.
   - Moderate: couple and coparenting programmes (mostly developer-run trials), and family routines (largely correlational).
   - Thin: family meetings, "family rules" posters, and specific anger techniques for parents.
4. **Iran context.** Grandparents and the wider family are often co-caregivers. The content should treat them as allies whose help is valued, while helping parents agree a few boundaries kindly. It should never cast them as an obstacle.

## 1. Co-parenting as a team

### Framework
- **Feinberg's coparenting framework** [background: Feinberg 2003, *Parenting: Science & Practice*] has four domains:
  1. childrearing agreement
  2. division of labour (childcare and household)
  3. support versus undermining
  4. joint management of family interactions: shielding children from conflict, and not pulling a child into the parents' dispute ("triangulation")
- The Family Foundations paper [opened] defines **support** as affirming the other parent's competence and upholding their decisions. It defines **undermining** as criticism, disparagement and blame. It also adds a dimension of "parenting-based closeness": sharing the joys of raising the child together.

### Evidence: the Family Foundations RCTs
- **First trial: Feinberg & Kan 2008, *J Fam Psychol* 22(2):253–263** [opened, PMC3178882].
  - Design: 169 first-time expectant couples. Eight group classes (4 before the birth, 4 after). Outcomes measured at a baby age of about 6.5 months.
  - Results: more coparental support (effect sizes .35 for mothers and .54 for fathers); lower maternal depression (.56) and anxiety (.38); less dysfunctional father–child interaction (.70); some infant self-regulation benefits.
  - **No effect on undermining.**
  - Limitations: self-report only, a mostly middle-class educated sample, short-term follow-up, and developer-run.
- **Larger trial: 399 couples, Feinberg et al., *Prevention Science* ~2016** [search-abstract].
  - Intervention couples did better on more than two-thirds of measures, covering coparenting, parent mental health, parenting, child adjustment and family violence.
  - The effects on family violence were described as particularly large.
  - Higher-risk couples benefited more on some outcomes.
- Later follow-ups report child school and emotional adjustment benefits (*J Fam Psychol* 2014) [search-abstract].
- Blueprints for Healthy Youth Development lists Family Foundations as a programme [search-abstract].
- **Strength: moderate.** These are replicated RCTs, but most were run by the developers and samples are mostly Western and middle-class. Content can say "programmes that help parents work as a team have improved parents' mood and children's adjustment in trials."

### What parents can actually do
- **Agree on the few things that matter most.** Don't try to agree on everything. Pick 3–5 household basics, for example bedtime, screens, hitting is never OK, and how "no" is handled. Small differences in style (one parent sillier, one stricter) are normal and fine for children.
- **Back each other up in front of the child. Discuss disagreements privately and later.** A useful script: "Dad and I will talk about it and let you know." If a decision really is unsafe, step in calmly; otherwise wait.
- **Don't undermine.** No eye-rolling, no "Your mother is being silly," and no overruling in front of the child.
- **Don't recruit the child.** Avoid "Go tell your father…" and asking the child to take sides.
- **Notice and say the good.** For example: "You were really patient with her at bedtime." Feeling appreciated was a measured benefit in Bringing Baby Home.
- **When you disagree on discipline:**
  - Talk when calm, not mid-incident.
  - Each person names the value behind their view, e.g. "I want her to be respectful" or "I want her to feel heard."
  - Find the shared goal, then trial one agreed approach for 2 weeks and review it.
  - Agree a "default" rule: if in doubt, the parent who is present decides, and the other backs them up for now.
- **A non-negotiable line, whoever disagrees: no hitting and no humiliation.** This ties in with Section 5.

### Grandparents and extended family (very relevant to Iran)
- **Frame their help as a strength.** Persian articles [search-abstract: beytoote.com, jamejamonline.ir, khabarfoori.com] describe the grandparents' home as a safe place for the child when parents are away, and as a source of identity and belonging.
- **Common advice across Persian and English sources** [search-abstract: Greater Good/Berkeley "The trouble with grandparents"; Persian magazine pieces]:
  - Grandparents act as "consultants, not supervisors."
  - Parents can share up-to-date information kindly, e.g. "the paediatrician told us…", and accept well-meant advice graciously.
  - Talk about shared concerns rather than arguing over punishments.
  - Agree concrete, specific help.
- **Practical steps for parents:**
  - Choose the *few* boundaries that really matter. Safety examples: safe sleep, car seats, medicines and sweets limits, no smacking or frightening. Let the rest go: grandparents' homes can have slightly different rules, and children cope well with "at Grandma's it's different."
  - The adult child talks to their *own* parent. The mother talks to her parents and the father to his, which reduces in-law friction.
  - Use "we" and gratitude, e.g. "We're so grateful you look after her. The doctor asked us to… could you help us with that?"
  - Use outside authority to depersonalise the request (doctor, teacher, "new guidance").
  - Avoid correcting grandparents in front of the child. Same principle as backing up a co-parent.
- **Evidence:** the specific evidence on grandparent boundary-setting is **thin**. This is practical wisdom, so frame it that way.

## 2. Parents' conflict and children

### Emotional security theory (Cummings & Davies)
[search-abstract: Schermerhorn, Cummings & Davies 2005; Davies & Cummings 1994; Cummings & Davies 2010 book; *Development & Psychopathology*]
- Children monitor the parents' relationship for safety. Repeated **destructive** conflict undermines their sense of security, and that insecurity partly explains the link between conflict and children's anxiety and behaviour problems.
- Hostile conflict is a stronger and more consistent predictor of child difficulties than the mere absence of cooperation.
- **Destructive conflict** looks like: shouting, insults, threats, physical aggression, the silent treatment and stonewalling, conflict about the child, involving the child, and leaving things unresolved.
- **Constructive conflict** looks like: calm discussion, listening, problem-solving, some warmth or humour, and compromise or resolution [background: Goeke-Morey, Cummings et al. 2003 coding of children's responses].
- Children's emotional responses track resolution. In one study, children's happiness was associated with resolution, while fear was associated with escalating and unresolved conflicts [search-abstract].
- [background] Cummings' lab studies suggest that even partial resolution, or being *told* that an argument was resolved privately, reduces children's distress compared with unresolved conflict. **I could not verify this in a primary source this session.** Phrase it softly, e.g. "Research suggests children feel better when they see or hear that things were sorted out."

### UK evidence review
- **Harold, Sellers et al. 2016, "What works to enhance inter-parental relationships and improve outcomes for children"**, EIF for the DWP.
  - I [opened] the landing page; the key findings are [search-abstract] from the Sussex press release and the UKRI impact page.
  - Frequent, intense, poorly resolved conflict harms children's mental health and life chances. Children of every age notice it, from infancy onwards.
  - Hostile couple relationships spill over into harsher, less responsive parenting.
  - Parenting programmes that ignore severe ongoing conflict are unlikely to help children.
  - **Normal disagreement handled respectfully is not harmful and can model problem-solving.** This review led to the UK "Reducing Parental Conflict" programme.
- **Strength: strong** for associations, with reasonable evidence for causal pathways (including genetically informed designs in Harold's work).

### What parents can do
- **Disagreeing is normal.** It is *how* you disagree that matters to children.
- **Keep it respectful:** no insults, contempt or threats. Lower your voice. Take a break if heated ("Let's finish this tonight") and **actually come back to it**.
- **Keep the child out of it:** no arguing about the child in front of the child, no asking them to take sides, and no using them as a messenger.
- **Let them see the repair.** A visible hug, apology or "we sorted it out" teaches more than hiding every disagreement.
- **What to say to a child after an argument (sample scripts, age-adjusted):**
  - "You heard Mum and Dad arguing. That must have felt scary/upsetting."
  - "Grown-ups sometimes disagree. We've talked and we're OK now."
  - "It was not your fault. It's grown-up business, and it's our job to sort it out."
  - "Our family is safe, and we both love you."
  - Don't overshare details, and don't ask the child to judge who was right.

### When conflict becomes harmful: route, don't advise
- If there is fear, physical harm, threats, controlling behaviour, or the child is frightened of a parent, this is no longer "conflict". **Route to help.** Do not give detailed domestic violence guidance in the app.
- **Iran:** Social Emergency (اورژانس اجتماعی) **123**, and the Welfare Organization counselling line (صدای مشاور) **1480**. [background, must verify current numbers before shipping.] Police 110 for immediate danger.
- **Elsewhere:** local emergency number plus a national domestic abuse helpline. Keep the app content generic.
- Note: the Family Foundations trial found the programme reduced family violence [search-abstract]. The UK review states that when conflict is severe, parenting tips alone are not enough.

## 3. The couple after a baby

### Relationship satisfaction dip
- **Doss, Rhoades, Stanley & Markman 2009, *J Pers Soc Psychol* 96(3):601–619** [search-abstract; the PDF failed to load].
  - Sample: 218 couples followed over the first 8 years of marriage.
  - New parents showed a **sudden** post-birth decline in observed and self-reported relationship functioning. It was **small to medium** in size and tended to persist.
  - Mothers and fathers declined similarly.
  - Childless couples declined gradually, without the sudden drop.
  - The size of the decline varied by individual, marriage and pregnancy characteristics. Secondary summaries suggest larger drops for couples who had a baby very soon after marrying and for lower-income couples.
- **Caution:** popular coverage claimed "90% of couples" declined. Do not use this figure. The paper reports averages.
- **App framing:** "It's very common for couples to feel more strain after a baby. It's not a sign that something is wrong with you."

### Bringing Baby Home (Gottman)
- **Shapiro, Gottman & Fink 2019/2020, *Psychological Reports*** [opened, PMC8136247].
  - Design: RCT with 136 expectant couples (Seattle), comparing a weekend workshop with a control group.
  - Results at about 3 months: fathers reported a more equal division of parenting tasks, more satisfaction with the division of labour, and felt more appreciated.
  - **Mothers did not report differences.**
  - Limitations: small sample, attrition, a low-risk homogeneous sample, and the authors are the developers.
- Earlier claims (less competitive coparenting, less hostility, and lower maternal depression) come from **program-authored summaries** [search-abstract, Gottman.com]. Treat them as modest.
- **Strength: low–moderate.** OK to mention as "couple programmes like Family Foundations and Bringing Baby Home have shown benefits in some trials."

### Dividing night-time and household work
- In the BBH trial, a more equal division of parenting tasks correlated with satisfaction for both parents (r ≈ .31 for fathers, .38 for mothers) [opened].
- **Practical steps:**
  - Make the invisible work visible. Write down every task (night feeds, nappies, laundry, appointments, *remembering* things) and decide who owns which. Owning a task means remembering it too, not just doing it when asked.
  - **Night shifts:** protect at least one longer sleep block for each parent where feasible. With breastfeeding, the partner can do the nappy, settling, burping and morning shift.
  - Re-negotiate every few weeks. The baby changes, and so should the plan.
  - Use appreciation and small daily check-ins, e.g. "What was hardest today?" (10 minutes).
  - **Avoid "maternal gatekeeping"** (the BBH paper notes it can undermine father involvement): let the other parent do it their own way when it's safe.

### Fathers' involvement and paternal depression
- **Paulson & Bazemore 2010, *JAMA* 303(19):1961–1969** [search-abstract via RTI, ScienceDaily, MDedge].
  - Meta-analysis of 43 studies, about 28,000 fathers in 16 countries.
  - Pooled prenatal/postpartum depression in fathers was **10.4%** (95% CI 8.5–12.7%), with high heterogeneity.
  - Rates were highest at **3–6 months postpartum** (25.6% in that subgroup; treat with caution). The US figure was 14.1% versus 8.2% elsewhere.
  - Paternal depression is moderately correlated with maternal depression (r ≈ 0.31).
- **App framing:** "About 1 in 10 new dads experience depression, more when mum is also struggling."
  - Signs in fathers may look like irritability, withdrawal, working longer hours, or drinking more, as well as sadness.
  - Encourage seeing a GP or doctor.
  - Partners should check on each other.
- The BBH paper [opened] summarises evidence that positive father involvement is associated with better child cognitive, social and mental-health outcomes.
- **Iran:** fathers' limited participation was a theme in Iranian mothers' parenting challenges (Hajkhodadadi et al. 2020) [opened, abstract]. A Tehran study linked greater father involvement with lower parental exhaustion [search-abstract, ensani.ir].

## 4. Parental stress and burnout

### Parental burnout (Mikolajczak & Roskam)
- **Roskam, Aguiar, Akgun et al. 2021, "Parental Burnout Around the Globe: a 42-Country Study", *Affective Science*** [search-abstract: jyx.jyu.fi, Tilburg; the full-text PDF mirror failed].
  - Sample: 17,409 parents (71% mothers).
  - Prevalence varied dramatically across countries.
  - **Individualistic cultures had markedly higher prevalence and mean levels.** Individualism mattered more than economic inequality or family characteristics such as the number or age of children.
  - Press coverage: rich, individualistic Western countries were most affected.
  - The data are correlational. The authors disclose that they founded a training institute on parental burnout, which did not fund the study.
  - Western prevalence is often cited as roughly 5–8% of parents [background]. I could not check this figure or any Iran- or Turkey-specific data this session, so **do not give a number.**
- **Definition: four dimensions** [background, from the Roskam/Mikolajczak Parental Burnout Assessment]:
  1. overwhelming exhaustion from the parenting role
  2. emotional distancing from one's children ("running on autopilot")
  3. loss of pleasure and fulfilment in parenting (being "fed up")
  4. a contrast with one's former parental self ("I'm not the parent I used to be")
- **Why it matters** [background]: burnout is associated with escape and suicidal thoughts in parents, and with more child neglect and harsh or violent behaviour. Burnout is *specific to the parenting role*. It is different from ordinary tiredness and different from depression, although they overlap.
- **What helps** [search-abstract]:
  - Structured group programmes over 8 weeks. Two kinds were tested: mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, and "Parenting in Balance", a stress/resources rebalancing programme. In a 2025 RCT of 118 mothers (SWPS, Poland), both reduced burnout similarly, with gains lasting 6 months.
  - A 2024 Belgian comparison (Bayot et al., *Children*) found that mindfulness/compassion and Parenting in Balance helped equally. The improvement did **not** run through increases in mindfulness or self-compassion, which points to common factors (support, time for self, perspective).
  - A 2023 experiment found that increasing parents' **perceived coparenting support** lowered burnout. Partner support is a practical lever.
  - The core model is a **balance between stressors and resources**. Reduce stressors where you can: lower standards, drop perfectionism, ask for help. Add resources: sleep, support, time for self, a partner who shares the load.
  - Perfectionism is a risk factor. An Iranian study of 410 mothers (Jolfa, 2025) linked perfectionism with more parenting stress and psychological capital with less [search-abstract, ensani.ir].
- **Strength: moderate** for the concept and measurement; **low–moderate** for interventions (small trials).

### Self-compassion and mindful parenting
- **Self-compassion (Neff)** [background]: kindness to self, common humanity ("all parents struggle"), and mindfulness of one's feelings. One review of 13 self-compassion-focused parenting interventions reported **small** reductions in parent depression, anxiety and stress (g ≈ 0.36–0.43) [search-abstract; the review's authors are unconfirmed].
- **Mindful parenting:**
  - **Burgdorf et al. 2019 meta-analysis** [search-abstract]: 25 studies, few of them controlled. Small effects on parenting stress (g ≈ 0.34 within-group) and on child outcomes (g ≈ 0.27).
  - **Shorey & Ng 2021** found no significant overall effect on parenting stress in their pooled analysis.
  - **Verdict: modest and mixed. Say so.** Mindful parenting is reasonable to suggest as one tool, not a cure.
- **Practical "self-compassion moment" for the app:** a hand on the chest, then: "This is hard. Lots of parents feel this. May I be kind to myself right now." Then one tiny restorative act.

### "Good enough" parenting and repair
- **Winnicott's "good enough mother"** [background]: babies benefit from a parent who adapts well *most* of the time and gradually fails in manageable ways, not from perfection.
- **Tronick's mutual regulation and "mismatch and repair"** [background; the primary paper (Tronick & Gianino 1986, *Zero to Three*) was not reachable]:
  - Even in healthy parent–infant pairs, interactions are frequently out of sync, and these mismatches are normally repaired quickly.
  - The experience of repair is thought to help babies learn that disruptions can be fixed.
  - **Caution:** popular sources cite "only ~30% in sync" or "70% out of sync". The figures conflict and come from secondary sources [search-abstract], so **don't put a percentage in the app.**
  - The still-face paradigm [opened, Yoo & Reeb-Sutherland 2013] shows infants are distressed when a parent is unresponsive and partly recover when the parent re-engages. Infant temperament affects that recovery.
- **App framing:** "You don't have to get it right every time. What matters is coming back and reconnecting."

## 5. Parent anger

### Evidence on harsh verbal discipline
- **Wang & Kenny 2014, *Child Development* 85(3):908–923** [opened, PMC3875601; published online 2013].
  - Sample: 976 two-parent families in Pennsylvania, with adolescents measured at ages 13 and 14.
  - **Definition:** shouting/yelling/screaming, swearing, and humiliation (e.g. calling the child dumb or lazy).
  - **Prevalence:** about 45% of mothers and 42% of fathers reported using it in the past year. This was *not* a high-risk sample.
  - **Findings:** harsh verbal discipline at 13 predicted increases in conduct problems and depressive symptoms by 14.
  - **Parental warmth did not buffer the effect.** "But I'm loving the rest of the time" didn't cancel it out.
  - Effects ran both ways: teen misbehaviour also increased harsh verbal discipline, producing a cycle.
  - Limitations: observational (cross-lagged), self-report, two-parent families only, early adolescence only.
- **App framing:** "Shouting and name-calling are common, and they tend to make behaviour worse over time, not better. That's not a reason for guilt. It's a reason to have a plan for the hard moments."

### Practical in-the-moment strategies
The evidence here is thin for each specific technique. These are standard emotion-regulation advice from parenting bodies. I could not open Raising Children Network (403).
1. **Notice the early signs:** a hot face, a clenched jaw, a raised voice, "here we go again" thoughts. Name it to yourself: "I'm getting angry."
2. **Pause before acting.** Say "I need a minute" out loud. This also models the skill.
3. **Make the child safe, then step away.** A baby goes in the cot or a safe place. With an older child, say "I'm going to the kitchen to calm down, I'll be back." Never leave a young child somewhere unsafe. **Never shake a baby.**
4. **Breathe slowly** with a long out-breath. Splash cold water, count, or move your body.
5. **Lower your voice and get down to the child's level** when you return. Use fewer words.
6. **Deal with the behaviour later,** when calm. A consequence decided in anger is usually too big.
7. **Look at the patterns:** HALT (hungry, angry, lonely, tired), the times of day when things blow up, unrealistic expectations for the child's age, and your own stress or burnout (see Section 4).
8. **When to get help:** if anger feels out of control, you fear you might hurt your child, you are hitting, or anger is daily. Talk to a doctor, counsellor or parenting line. In Iran: 1480 [verify].

### Repair after you've lost it
- Go back when calm. Get down to their level.
- **Own it without excuses:** "I yelled and called you a name. That wasn't OK. I'm sorry. I was frustrated, but it's my job to handle my feelings."
- Don't blame the child for your outburst ("you made me…"). You can still address their behaviour separately and calmly.
- Reconnect with a hug, play or a story.
- This links to Tronick and Winnicott (Section 4): repair is normal and protective. **But repair does not cancel a pattern.** Wang & Kenny found warmth didn't buffer frequent harsh discipline. The aim is fewer outbursts, *and* repair when they happen.

## 6. Family rituals and routines

### Evidence
- **Fiese, Tomcho, Douglas, Josephs, Poltrock & Baker 2002, *J Fam Psychol* 16(4):381–390**, "A review of 50 years of research on naturally occurring family routines and rituals: Cause for celebration?" [search-abstract: PubMed 12561283, APA press release].
  - A qualitative review of 32 publications.
  - Predictable routines are associated with healthier, better-regulated infants and preschoolers, and with **stability during stress and transitions**.
  - **Routines** carry instrumental communication ("what needs doing"). **Rituals** carry symbolic meaning ("who we are").
  - Examples: dinnertime, bedtime and chores (routines); holidays, birthdays and religious occasions (rituals).
  - **Caveat from the authors themselves:** the studies could not establish how many routines are needed, and the evidence is largely correlational.
- **Strength: moderate, correlational.** A safe claim: "Families with regular routines and meaningful rituals tend to cope better with stress."

### Family meetings (Positive Discipline / Adlerian)
- [background] Positive Discipline (Nelsen) and the Adlerian/Dreikurs tradition recommend a regular weekly family meeting: compliments and appreciations, an agenda, solving one problem together, planning something fun, and keeping it short.
- **Evidence is thin.** I found no RCT of family meetings as a standalone practice. Positive Discipline as a whole has limited, mostly small or uncontrolled evaluations. **Say so plainly:** "There's little research on family meetings specifically, but they put into practice things that are well supported: warmth, involving children in decisions, and solving problems calmly."
- **Practical shape:**
  - 10–20 minutes, ages 4+.
  - Start with appreciations: everyone says one nice thing about someone.
  - Take one issue only, brainstorm solutions (children's ideas count), and try one for a week.
  - End with something fun, e.g. choosing Friday's dessert or outing.
  - Parents keep veto power over safety.

### One-to-one time with each child
- Supported indirectly. It is a core component of evidence-based parenting programmes, e.g. "child-directed play" and "special time" in PCIT and Incredible Years [background]. The AAP recommends daily special time for older siblings when a new baby arrives [opened].
- **Practical:** 10–15 minutes a day, or a weekly "date", with each child. The child leads, phones are away, and there is no teaching or correcting.

### Family traditions and "family rules" posters
- **Traditions** (weekly, e.g. a Friday family meal or picnic; annual, e.g. Nowruz preparations, Yalda night, Sizdah Bedar, Ramadan iftar, birthdays) give continuity and identity. These are good Iran-relevant examples.
- **Family rules posters:** there is no direct evidence. Clear, few, positively worded rules are consistent with evidence-based behaviour management [background]. **Advice:** 3–5 rules, worded as what to *do* ("We use gentle hands"), made together, with pictures for young children, and they apply to adults too.

## 7. Big family changes

### A new baby
From the AAP (HealthyChildren.org, "Preparing your family for a new baby", updated 10 Feb 2026) [opened]:
- **Telling by age:**
  - Toddlers: read books about babies and siblings.
  - Preschoolers: start talking early, e.g. when buying baby things; consider a sibling class.
  - School-age children: explain what will change.
- Under-5s feel more threatened. Over-5s may still resent the attention.
- **Expect some regression** (accidents, wanting a bottle). Respond with attention, praise grown-up behaviour, and don't shame them.
- Finish toilet training or the cot-to-bed move **before** the birth, or wait until the baby is settled.
- **Involve the older child:** shopping, preparing the room, cuddling beside you during feeds, holding the baby with help.
- **Daily special time** with the older child.
- Ask visitors to give the older child attention and perhaps a small gift.
- Cincinnati Children's and other sources [search-abstract] suggest avoiding other major changes for about 8–12 weeks after the birth.

### Moving house / new school / new city
[background, practical; the evidence is mostly correlational. Mobility risk is concentrated in frequent moves and moves combined with other adversity.]
- Tell children early enough to adjust: days for toddlers, a few weeks for older children.
- Keep routines (bedtime, meals) the same through the change. This draws on Fiese: routines stabilise families during transitions.
- Visit the new place or school beforehand if possible, or look at photos.
- Let them pack a "first night box" of familiar things.
- Expect a few weeks of clinginess, sleep changes or irritability.
- Help them keep in touch with old friends.
- For a new school, do short practice runs, get to know the teacher, and set up a playdate with a classmate.

### A parent working away
This is common in Iran and the Gulf region, e.g. a father working in another city or abroad.
- Use honest, concrete explanations and a visual calendar for return dates.
- Keep regular, predictable calls rather than random long ones.
- Keep a "together" ritual that continues at a distance: reading the same story, a shared photo of the day.
- The parent at home needs support (see burnout).
- Reunions can be bumpy. Children may be cool or angry at first, and that's normal.

### A parent's illness
Content already exists. Key points to keep: tell the truth at the child's level, and keep routines.

### Divorce
Content already exists. Skip deep coverage. Cross-link to the conflict section (keep children out of the middle), and note the Iranian coparenting-after-divorce intervention study [search-abstract, ouci.dntb.gov.ua].

## 8. Work and family

### Guilt about working, and child care quality
- **NICHD Study of Early Child Care and Youth Development (SECCYD).** The PDF booklet was unreadable this session.
  - [background] The NICHD 2006 summary booklet reports that **family and parenting characteristics were stronger and more consistent predictors** of child development than child care features.
  - **Higher-quality care** predicted somewhat better cognitive and language outcomes.
  - **More hours** in care predicted slightly more behaviour problems in early childhood. These effects were small.
- **Follow-up to age 15 (Vandell et al. 2010, *Child Development*)** [search-abstract, NIEER]:
  - Higher-quality early care predicted slightly higher academic achievement at 15.
  - More hours predicted slightly more risk-taking and impulsivity at 15.
  - **Effects were small and correlational.**
- **App framing (no guilt):**
  - "What happens at home, your warmth and responsiveness, matters most. When you choose child care, quality matters more than the type."
  - **Quality markers:** warm, responsive, stable caregivers; small groups and good adult-to-child ratios; trained staff; low staff turnover; safe, stimulating spaces.
  - Avoid claiming any type (grandparent, nursery, nanny) is best. Grandparent care is common in Iran, and its quality depends on the same markers: warmth, responsiveness, safety, language-rich interaction.

### Limited time
- Quality over quantity:
  - short daily one-to-one time (Section 6)
  - phone-free meals
  - bedtime reading or talk
  - involving children in chores together (talk while you work)
  - a weekly family ritual
- Lower the standards elsewhere (housework, perfect meals). This links to the burnout balance model.

### Phones and "technoference"
- **McDaniel & Radesky 2018, *Child Development* 89(1):100–109** [opened, PMC5681450].
  - Sample: 170 US two-parent families with children aged about 3. Mostly white, educated and married.
  - **48% of parents reported device interruptions 3+ times a day.** 40% of mothers and 32% of fathers rated their own phone use as problematic.
  - **Mother–child** technoference was associated with more child externalising and internalising behaviour (β ≈ .12–.20). **Father–child technoference was not.**
  - **Effects were small and cross-sectional:** direction unknown.
- **McDaniel & Radesky 2018, *Pediatric Research*** [search-abstract]: a longitudinal study of 183 couples found **bidirectional** effects. Child behaviour problems raised parenting stress, which raised parents' withdrawal into devices, which in turn predicted more behaviour problems.
- **App framing:** "Phones are part of life. It's not about zero use. Small changes help."
- **Practical:**
  - phone-free zones and times: meals, bedtime, the first 15 minutes after pickup
  - notifications off
  - phone out of reach during play
  - narrate when you must use it ("I'm checking the time for your class")
  - notice when you reach for the phone to escape stress, and try a different break

## 9. Iran / Persian-speaking context

### Family structure and pressures
Search-abstract unless marked.
- **Grandparents and extended family** are frequently involved in daily childcare. Persian parenting media treat their role positively (a safe place, identity), while acknowledging friction over differing methods.
  - Common advice in Persian articles: respect the elders' views, share current information gently, accept well-meant advice, and avoid contradicting in front of the child.
  - **No Iranian research on grandparent co-parenting was found this session. This is a gap.**
- **Iranian mothers' parenting challenges: Hajkhodadadi, Etemadi, Abedi & Jazayeri 2020, *J Relationships Research* 11:e16** [opened, abstract]. A qualitative study of 24 mothers found seven themes:
  1. diminished power and control over children
  2. feelings of inadequacy
  3. **fathers' limited participation**
  4. adapting to new situations
  5. changing family relationships
  6. unfulfilled expectations
  7. environmental threats
  - The authors describe these challenges as culture-bound and recommend parent training.
- **Coparenting and stress in Iran:**
  - A Kermanshah study of 300 parents (*J Family Relations Studies*, 2024) linked parenting stress with weaker coparenting, with family humour and leisure as buffers.
  - A Tehran study linked greater father involvement with less parental exhaustion; mothers reported more exhaustion.
  - The Jolfa 2025 study of 410 mothers linked perfectionism with more stress [ensani.ir].
  - A qualitative study of 13 couples in Tehran on lived experience of parenting disagreement [apsy.sbu.ac.ir] and a study of counsellors working with children of parents who disagree on childrearing [qccpc.atu.ac.ir] show the topic is live in Iranian research. **Mostly small and qualitative.**
- **Academic pressure and the Konkur (کنکور):**
  - The university entrance exam is described as the single biggest academic stressor for Iranian students. Preparation is a year-long effort with 10–12-hour days and heavy family spending on tutoring (the "Konkur economy").
  - Iranian studies report that exam-focused, controlling families with unrealistic expectations increase stress and conflict, while balanced families reduce it [jehp.mui.ac.ir and qccpc.atu.ac.ir, search-abstract].
  - The Konkur also exacerbates inequality [Times Higher Education, search-abstract].
  - In June 2026, students protested exam rule changes amid wartime uncertainty [IranWire, search-abstract].
  - **Relevance for 0–12:** the pressure starts early (tutoring, Tizhooshan/gifted-school entrance exams, grades). Content can gently address competitive comparison, keeping play time, and valuing effort over rank.
- **Gender roles in caregiving:** mothers carry most of the daily care, and fathers' limited involvement is a recognised stressor. Content encouraging father involvement and a fair division of tasks (Section 3) is directly relevant. **Frame it as invitation, not criticism.**
- **Collectivism and burnout:** the 42-country study found lower burnout in less individualistic cultures. Extended-family support may be a resource for Iranian parents. **Do not claim that Iranian parents have low burnout:** there are no data, and urban nuclear families, economic stress and perfectionism may change the picture.

### What Persian-speaking parents look for
These are search themes; I ran two Persian searches.
- «اختلاف والدین در تربیت فرزند» (parents disagreeing on upbringing): common magazine topic, framed as "the child gets confused and loses trust"; advice is "be united" («پدر و مادر با هم متحد باشید», jamejamonline).
- «دخالت پدربزرگ و مادربزرگ در تربیت» (grandparents' interference): very common; how to set limits without offending.
- Also likely high-interest:
  - فرزندپروری مثبت (positive parenting)
  - کنترل خشم والدین (parent anger)
  - تک‌فرزندی / آماده‌سازی برای فرزند دوم (only child / preparing for a second child)
  - استرس کنکور (Konkur stress)
  - استفاده از موبایل (phone use)
- **Terminology:** «هم‌والدگری» or «والدگری مشارکتی» is used for coparenting in Iranian academic literature. The everyday equivalent is «هماهنگی پدر و مادر در تربیت».
