# Shahnameh, mythic (Pishdadi) era to young Rostam: story candidates for PlayNest (round 8, note 01)

## Scope and method

- **Span:** from Kiumars through the Pishdadi kings (Hushang, Tahmures, Jamshid, Zahhak, Fereydun, Manuchehr, Nowzar, Zav) to young Rostam and his search for Kay Qobad. That covers the Ganjoor parts `qmars`, `hushang`, `tahmoores`, `jamshid`, `zahak`, `fereydoon`, `manoochehr`, `nozar`, `zutahmasb`, `garshasp` and `kqobad`.
- **How the text was read:** I downloaded every section of those parts through the Ganjoor API (`https://api.ganjoor.net/api/ganjoor/page?url=/ferdousi/shahname/<part>/shN`) and read the verses. Couplet numbers below are the order of the couplets inside each Ganjoor section as the API returns them [opened]. Ganjoor's Shahnameh is the Moscow-based text [recall, unverified].
- **Not on Ganjoor:** three episodes the owner listed are not in Ganjoor's text: Fereydun testing his sons as a dragon, the white elephant, and Mount Sepand. I read them in Jules Mohl's edition on Persian Wikisource instead (links in each section and in Sources) [opened]. An app ref for these needs a custom `Ref` (Wikisource), not the `ganjoor()` helper.
- **Excluded:** stories already in the app (Hushang/Sadeh, Jamshid/Nowruz, Kaveh, Zal and the Simorgh, Rakhsh, Kavus, Gordafarid, Arash). I checked them against the poem in the last section.
- **Tags:** [opened] I read it myself · [abstract] · [search] snippet only · [recall, unverified].
- **Ages** use the app's bands (2–4, 4–6, 6–8, 8–10, 10–12). "Open" means it can be told almost as written. "Care" means it needs softening and a parent note.

## 1. Zav and the rain of peace (زو و باران آشتی)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/zutahmasb/sh1 ("تاج‌گذاری زو پسر طهماسب، خشکسالی و پیمان صلح میان ایران و توران", 45 couplets) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. After the defeat by Afrasiab, Zal says the army needs a king (couplets 1–8). They choose Zav, son of Tahmasp, from Fereydun's line. He is eighty years old (9–17).
2. Zav keeps the soldiers from doing harm, and there is little killing (18–19).
3. Then a drought comes. No rain falls and bread costs silver (20–21). The Iranian and Turanian armies face each other for eight months without one real battle (22–23). Both are starving.
4. Both armies say "this misfortune from the sky comes from us" (25). They send an envoy to Zav: let us share the earth and say kind words to each other (26–28).
5. They agree to hold no old grudge, to divide the world fairly, and "not to remember what is past" (30–31). The borders are set (32–35).
6. Thunder fills the mountains, and the earth becomes "like a young bride", full of springs, gardens and running water (38–39). Feasts are held everywhere, and hearts are emptied of hatred and cursing (43). Five good years follow (44).

**Key couplets**
- «سخن رفتشان یک به‌یک هم‌زبان / که از ماست بر ما بد آسمان» (zutahmasb/sh1, 25)
- «بیا تا ببخشیم روی زمین / سراییم یک با دگر آفرین» (sh1, 28)
- «بر آن برنهادند هر دو سخن / که در دل ندارند کین کهن» (sh1, 30)
- «پر از غلغل و رعد شد کوهسار / زمین شد پر از رنگ و بوی و نگار» (sh1, 38)

**Needs care**
- Almost nothing. There is no on-stage death.
- Iran and Turan are enemies at the start, but the poem treats both armies the same: both are hungry, both admit fault, and both make peace. That makes it a useful counterweight to Iran–Turan othering elsewhere.
- Keep "Turan" as a neighbouring land, with no "Turks" label (the poem says «ترکان» once, in 35).
- The link between quarrelling and the drought is a moral, not a weather lesson. Tell it as the poem does ("they felt"), not as cause and effect.
- Zav's death in the last couplet (45) can be dropped.

**Age band:** 6–8 and 8–10. Ages 4–6 would also work with a very short version.

**Softening:** none really needed. End on the rain and the feasts.

**Parent note (one line):** "From the Shahnameh: two armies too hungry to fight make peace, and the poem says the rain came back; we end before old King Zav's death."

**Values:** making peace, admitting your own part in a quarrel, sharing, forgiving ("not remembering what is past"), the wisdom of the old.

**Questions to ask**
1. Why did nobody want to fight any more?
2. What did both armies promise each other?
3. Have you ever made up with someone? How did it feel afterwards?

**Children's adaptations:** I found none that focus on Zav [search: none found]. It seems to be overlooked, which is a chance for the app.

## 2. Zal answers the mobeds' riddles (معماهای موبدان)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh20 (27 couplets, the riddles) and https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh21 (76 couplets, the answers and the riding contest) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
King Manuchehr has his wise men (mobeds) test young Zal with six riddles (sh20, 5–27).

| Riddle | Couplets | Zal's answer | Couplets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Twelve tall cypresses, each with thirty branches | sh20, 6–7 | The twelve months of thirty days | sh21, 3–5 |
| Two fine horses, one black as pitch, one white as crystal, always racing and never catching each other | sh20, 8–10 | Night and day | sh21, 6–8 |
| Thirty riders pass the king; count them and one is missing, look again and there are thirty | sh20, 11–12 | The month that is sometimes one night short | sh21, 9–11 |
| Two cypresses with one bird that perches on each in turn; one side withers, one is green | sh20, 16–19 | The two halves of the year, and the sun | sh21, 12–16 |
| A city on a mountain, and people who leave it to build in a thorn-field until an earthquake comes | sh20, 20–25 | This passing world and the lasting one | sh21, 17–27 |
| A man with a sharp scythe mowing dry and green alike | sh20, 13–15 | Time, the reaper; "nobody was born from a mother except for death" | sh21, 28–33 |

The king is delighted and holds a feast (sh21, 34–37). The next day Zal shows his skill in the field: his arrow pierces an old tree, his javelin goes through three shields, and he lifts a rider from the saddle by his belt (47–70). He is given rich gifts (73–76).

**Key couplets**
- «که از ده و دو تای سرو سهی / که رُسته‌ست شاداب با فرهی» + «ازآن برزده هر یکی شاخ سی» (sh20, 6–7)
- «یکی زان به کردار دریای قار / یکی چون بلور سپید آبدار // بجنبید و هر دو شتابنده‌اند / همان یکدیگر را نیابنده‌اند» (sh20, 9–10)
- «سپید و سیاهست هر دو زمان / پس یکدگر تیز هر دو دوان» (sh21, 7)
- «دروگر زمانست و ما چون گیا / همانش نبیره همانش نیا» (sh21, 30). This is the death riddle; keep it for 10–12 only.

**Needs care**
- Riddles 5 and 6 are about death and the afterlife. Leave them out for under-10s.
- Context: in the poem the test comes before the king approves Zal's marriage to Rudabeh (sh19–sh22), and there is wine at the feast (sh21, 37–39). Neither is needed. Frame it as "the king wanted to know if the young man from the mountain was wise as well as strong".
- The field contest is mild.
- Zal's white hair is part of his story. Here he is honoured for his mind, which quietly repairs the abandonment theme.

**Age band**
- 6–8: riddles 1–3 (maybe 4), told as a game in which the child answers before Zal.
- 10–12: all six, with a gentle talk about time.

**Softening:** cut riddles 5 and 6 for younger children, drop the wine, and keep the archery as one short paragraph.

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh: King Manuchehr's wise men test Zal with riddles; we kept the four about the calendar and left two about life and death for older children."

**Values:** wisdom over strength, noticing the world (the calendar, the sky), a child who looks different being honoured for his mind.

**Questions to ask**
1. Can you answer the riddle of the two horses before Zal does?
2. Why do you think the king tested Zal with riddles and not only with a bow?
3. Make up a riddle of your own about something in the sky.

**Children's adaptations:** Kanoon's «نامهٔ نامور» series includes «زال و رودابه» by Mostafa Rahmandoust, labelled for teenagers [search]. Atoosa Salehi's «فرزند سیمرغ» (Ofogh, «قصه‌های شاهنامه» series, vol. 2) covers Zal's birth, love and marriage [search]. Whether either keeps the riddles is unconfirmed.

## 3. Tahmures, the div-binder who learned to write (تهمورث دیوبند)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/tahmoores/sh1 (46 couplets) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. Tahmures, Hushang's son, takes the throne and promises to "wash the world of wrongs" and bring every useful thing to light (1–7).
2. He shears sheep and lambs for wool, and people learn to spin and weave carpets (8–9). He feeds fast animals on grass, straw and barley (10).
3. From wild beasts he picks the caracal (سیاه‌گوش) and the cheetah (یوز) and tames them (11–12). He trains hawks and falcons (13–14). He brings hens and roosters to people (15–16).
4. He tells people to treat the animals warmly and call them only with soft voices (17), and to thank the Creator (18–19).
5. His pure minister Shahrasp guides him to goodness (20–26). Tahmures binds Ahriman by spells and rides him like a horse around the world (27–28). (This line is strange and scary; omit it.)
6. The divs rebel (29–34). Tahmures fights them. He binds two-thirds by spells and strikes down the rest with his mace (35–37).
7. The bound divs beg: "Don't kill us, and we will teach you a new art" (38–39). He spares them (40). They teach him writing, nearly thirty scripts: Rumi, Arabic, Persian, Sogdian, Chinese, Pahlavi (42–44).

**Key couplets**
- «بفرمودشان تا نوازند گرم / نخوانندشان جز به آواز نرم» (tahmoores/sh1, 17)
- «که ما را مکش تا یکی نو هنر / بیاموزی از ما که‌ت آید به بر» (sh1, 39)
- «نبشتن به خسرو بیاموختند / دلش را به دانش برافروختند» (sh1, 42)
- «نبشتن یکی نه، که نزدیک سی / چه رومی، چه تازی و چه پارسی» (sh1, 43)

**Needs care**
- One battle line: "struck the rest down with his mace" (37). Soften to "he bound them all".
- Ahriman ridden like a horse (27–28) may confuse or frighten young children; omit.
- Divs: the app's existing gloss ("strong, strange creatures of old tales") works.
- Religious content (prayer, the Creator) is light and matches existing stories.
- Othering is low. The scripts list many peoples' writing with respect.
- Mild care for "taming wild animals", which today could mean caging. Frame it as looking after animals gently, as the poem's "soft voices" line does.

**Age band:** 4–6 and 6–8.

**Softening:** the divs make trouble; Tahmures ties them up without hurting them; they promise to teach him something if he lets them go; he keeps his word and frees them.

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh: the old poem says the divs, beaten and tied up, bought their freedom by teaching King Tahmures to write; in the poem he strikes some of them down, and here he only ties them up."

**Values:** gentleness with animals, mercy to a beaten opponent, keeping your word, the worth of learning and writing, respect for other peoples' scripts.

**Questions to ask**
1. What did the divs teach Tahmures?
2. Why do you think he let them go?
3. If you could learn to write in another script, which would you choose?

**Children's adaptations:** «تهمورث دیوبند» and «تهمورث شاه دیوبند» (vol. 3 of «رمان‌های شاهنامه», Khaneh-ye Adabiyat) are listed on Iranketab [search]. «بنویس خورشید» (vol. 8 of «داستان‌های شاهنامه») has the Tahmures–div war in its summary [search]. Ages and treatment are unconfirmed.

## 4. How Rostam was born, and the silk doll (زادن رستم)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh25 (41 couplets), https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh26 (49 couplets) and https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh27 (63 couplets, Grandpa Sam's visit) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. Rudabeh is pregnant, and the baby is so heavy she feels "stuffed with stone or iron". She grows pale and weak (sh25, 1–8). One day she faints, and her mother Sindokht cries out (9–10).
2. Zal, in tears, remembers the Simorgh's feather and smiles. He burns a little of it, the sky darkens and the Simorgh comes (11–17).
3. The Simorgh says: why the tears? A lion-hearted child is coming (18–26). Fetch a skilled, clear-sighted man and a blade. Make the mother sleep with wine so she feels no pain, open her side, lift the child out, sew the wound, and heal it with a herb mixed with milk and musk and with the Simorgh's feather (27–34). Thank the Creator (35–37). She gives Zal a feather and flies off (38).
4. A skilled mobed does it all "without pain", and the child comes out unharmed (sh26, 1–3). The baby is huge and "lion-like" (4–5). The wound is sewn and healed (6). Rudabeh sleeps a day and a night, then wakes and speaks to Sindokht (7–8).
5. They show her the baby. She laughs and says «برستم», "I am free (of the pain)", and so he is named Rostam (11–12).
6. They sew a silk doll the size of the baby, stuffed with sable fur, with the sun and Venus drawn on its cheeks. They set it on a horse with a little mace and send it to Grandpa Sam (13–20). There are feasts from Zabol to Kabul (21–24). Sam's hair stands on end: "this silk looks just like me" (25–27). He sends back a happy letter (32–39).
7. Ten nurses feed baby Rostam. He eats like five men, and by eight he is as tall as a cypress (44–49).
8. In sh27 Grandpa Sam visits. Little Rostam rides out on an elephant with a little mace, kisses the throne and greets his grandfather: "my face is like yours" (sh27, 11–26).

**Key couplets**
- «همان پر سیمرغش آمد به یاد / بخندید و سیندخت را مژده داد» (sh25, 13)
- «بخندید از آن بچه سرو سُهی / بدید اندرو فر شاهنشهی // بِرَستم بگفتا غم آمد به سر / نهادند رُستَمَش نام پسر» (sh26, 11–12)
- «یکی کودکی دوختند از حریر / به بالای آن شیر ناخورده شیر» (sh26, 13)
- «به چهر تو ماند همی چهره‌ام» (sh27, 24, Rostam to Sam)

**Needs care**
- This is a birth, a mother in danger and an operation (caesarean). The poem is frank: "open her side", "bathe her flank in blood" (sh25, 29–30), with wine as the anaesthetic.
- For small children a mother in danger can be frightening. For a child born by caesarean, or whose mother had a hard birth, it can be affirming. In Persian the word «رستم‌زاد» is a folk term for caesarean birth [recall, unverified].
- Leave out "ate like five men" or keep it as a joke.
- Leave out the feasts with wine.
- Gender: Rudabeh is passive in this part. Give her the naming moment, as the poem does, since it is her word.

**Age band:** 4–6 (very soft) and 6–8.

**Softening:** "The baby was very big and needed help to be born. Zal burned the feather, and the Simorgh came and told them how a wise healer could help. Rudabeh slept deeply and felt no pain, and when she woke, there was her baby." Keep the healing herbs and feather. Cut the blood and the wine.

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh: Ferdowsi tells of the first caesarean birth in Persian poetry, guided by the Simorgh; we keep it gentle, with no blood or wine, and keep his mother naming him."

**Values:** help comes to those who ask; the Simorgh keeps her promise (this continues the app's existing Zal story); family joy; grandparents.

**Questions to ask**
1. What did Zal do when he was worried?
2. What does Rostam's name mean, and who chose it?
3. What do you know about the day you were born?

**Children's adaptations:** «نخستین نبرد رستم» (Peyk-e Adabiyat, vol. 5 of a Shahnameh series, "translated" by Shadi Beyzaei) is said to open with Rostam's birth and the burning of the Simorgh's feather [search]. I could not see how it handles the operation.

## 5. Rostam finds Kay Qobad on Mount Alborz (رستم و کیقباد)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/garshasp/sh3 (19 couplets, choosing Qobad), https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/garshasp/sh4 (7 couplets, Zal's order) and https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/garshasp/sh5 (88 couplets) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. Iran has no king, and Zal says "a body cannot be without a head". A mobed has pointed to Kay Qobad, of Fereydun's line, living on Mount Alborz (sh3, 14–19).
2. Zal tells young Rostam to ride there and be back in two weeks (sh4, 1–7).
3. On the way Rostam breaks through enemy scouts (sh5, 1–5). Near Alborz he finds a lovely place with trees, running water and a throne by the stream, where a moon-faced young man sits among champions (13–18).
4. They invite him to stop and feast. Rostam refuses: "I must go to Alborz on a great task. While Iran's throne has no king, feasting is not for me" (22–25). Then he asks if anyone knows where Qobad is (26). They promise to tell him if he sits with them (27–29).
5. The young man asks who wants Qobad (35). Rostam explains (36–42). The young man laughs: "I am Kay Qobad, of Fereydun's seed" (43–44). Rostam bows and gives Zal's message (45–52).
6. Qobad tells his dream: two white falcons flew from Iran and set a shining crown on his head. "Rostam is my white falcon" (57–62).
7. They ride back. Qolun's Turanian troops block the road, and Rostam unhorses Qolun (69–80). He brings Qobad to Zal by night (86), and Qobad is crowned (87–88).

**Key couplets**
- «سر تخت ایران ابی شهریار / مرا باده خوردن نیاید به کار» (garshasp/sh5, 25)
- «ز گفتار رستم دلیر جوان / بخندید و گفتش که ای پهلوان // ز تخم فریدون منم کیقباد» (sh5, 43–44)
- «که از سوی ایران دو باز سپید / یکی تاج رخشان به کردار شید // خرامان و نازان شدندی برم / نهادندی آن تاج را بر سرم» (sh5, 58–59)
- «تهمتن مرا شد چو باز سپید» (sh5, 62)

**Needs care**
- The Qolun fight ends graphically: Rostam lifts him on a spear "like a bird on a spit" (sh5, 78–79). Cut it.
- Wine: the "feast" is a wine party (21, 33–34, 54–55). Make it a feast with sherbet and fruit.
- Othering: Afrasiab warns that "Iranians are a tricky people" (sh5, 9). That cuts both ways and is not needed.
- "Turks" appears as an enemy label. Use "enemy scouts".

**Age band:** 6–8 and 8–10.

**Softening:** Rostam slips past the enemy scouts on Rakhsh, and the return ride needs no fight.

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh: young Rostam finds the hidden prince Kay Qobad; we left out a short fight on the road home and the poem's wine-cups."

**Values:** duty before fun, humility (the prince hides who he is and laughs), dreams and hope, a messenger's loyalty. It links to the app's Rakhsh story, since this comes straight after it.

**Questions to ask**
1. Why wouldn't Rostam sit down at the feast at first?
2. What did Kay Qobad dream, and who was the white falcon?
3. Have you ever had to finish a job before you could play?

**Children's adaptations:** none found [search: none found].

## 6. Fereydun becomes a dragon and names his sons (آزمودن فریدون پسران را)

**Source:** not in Ganjoor. I fetched all 20 sections of `fereydoon`, and the passage is absent [opened]. Ganjoor's fereydoon/sh3 ends with the sons leaving for Yemen. sh4 jumps to the weddings and the return journey, and sh5 is the division of the world, so the Yemen tests and the dragon test are missing. Text read in:
- Mohl edition: https://fa.wikisource.org/wiki/شاهنامه_(تصحیح_ژول_مل)/آزمودن_فریدون_پسران_خود_را (Mohl vol. 1, about vv. 238–282) [opened]
- Turner Macan edition: https://fa.wikisource.org/wiki/شاهنامه_(تصحیح_ترنر_ماکان)/بازگشتن_پسران_فریدون_از_یمن_و_آزمودن_فریدون_ایشان_را [opened]

The two differ slightly. In Macan the middle son also turns and runs after his boast, and the youngest draws his sword. For app refs, cite Wikisource (Mohl), with Ganjoor's https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/fereydoon/sh5 as the nearest Ganjoor section.

**What happens (Mohl)** [opened]
1. Hearing his three sons are coming home from Yemen, Fereydun wants to know their hearts. He goes to meet them in the shape of a fire-breathing dragon (vv. ~237–242).
2. The eldest says a wise man does not fight a dragon, and runs (244–245).
3. The middle son strings his bow: "if it's a fight, a lion or a rider, it's all the same to me" (246–247).
4. The youngest shouts: "Get out of our way! If you have heard the name of King Fereydun, don't try this with us. We are his sons." (248–252)
5. Fereydun vanishes, then comes back as their father with drums and elephants and greets them kindly (253–259).
6. He tells them the dragon was their father (264–265) and gives them names:
   - Salm, the eldest, "who sought safety (سلامت) from the dragon's jaws", with the comment that a man who doesn't fear elephants and lions is reckless, not brave (266–269);
   - Tur, the middle son, "the fierce", who showed sharpness from the start (270–272);
   - Iraj, the youngest, who chose the middle way "between earth and fire", hasty and patient at once: "brave, young and wise" (273–276).
7. He also names their Yemeni brides: Arzu, Mah-e Azadeh-khu and Sahi (277–280).

**Key couplets (Mohl, Wikisource)**
- «بیامد بسانِ یکی اژدها / کزو شیر گفتی نیابد رها» (~v. 240)
- «پسر گفت با اژدها روی جنگ / نه بیند خرد یافته مردِ هنگ» (~v. 245)
- «دلاور که نندیشد از پیل و شیر / تو دیوانه خوانش مخوانش دلیر» (~v. 269)
- «ز خاک و ز آتش میانه گزید / چنان کز رهِ هوشیاران سزید» (~v. 274)

**Needs care**
- The dragon is frightening only for a moment, and the twist ("it was Daddy!") is reassuring.
- The main care point is what follows in the poem: Fereydun divides the world, the elder brothers grow jealous, and they kill Iraj. Tell this as a standalone naming story and do not say "and they lived happily".
- Sibling comparison: a father openly ranks his sons. Note that no son "failed". Fereydun even praises Salm's caution ("a man who fears nothing is reckless") [opened]. This keeps it from being a "the youngest is best" story that may sting older siblings.
- Othering: Salm gets Rum and the West, and Tur gets Turan and "Turk and Chin" (Ganjoor fereydoon/sh5, 2–6). Stop before the division.

**Age band:** 6–8 and 8–10.

**Softening:** none needed beyond stopping before the division. Present all three answers as kinds of wisdom (caution, courage, standing firm while thinking).

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh (Mohl's edition; this passage is not in the Ganjoor text): Fereydun tests his sons in a dragon's shape and names them; in the poem the brothers later fall out, which we leave for older readers."

**Values:** different temperaments are each valuable; true courage is a balance; a father who tests his children and then welcomes them warmly.

**Questions to ask**
1. What did each brother do when he saw the dragon?
2. Fereydun said someone who is never afraid is not really brave. What do you think?
3. If a dragon blocked your path, what would you do?

**Children's adaptations:** «فریدون و پسرانش» is listed on Iranketab (book 6541) [search]. Atoosa Salehi has an «ایرج» volume, and Fidibo lists an audio episode «فرجام فریدون» [search]. I could not see their treatment of the dragon scene.

## 7. Faranak's secret gifts and the first Mehregan (فرانک و مهرگان)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/fereydoon/sh1 (48 couplets) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. Fereydun is crowned on the first day of the month of Mehr. People make a new festival, light fires and burn amber and saffron (3–8). "The worship of Mehregan is his faith", and Mehregan is his memorial (9–10).
2. His mother Faranak has not heard. When she learns her son is king, she washes, prays, and thanks God (14–19).
3. Then, for a whole week, she secretly gives to everyone in need. She tells no one and keeps their need private, until no one in the land is poor (20–22). In the second week she hosts the nobles (23–24).
4. Then she opens her hidden treasures (horses, jewels, armour) and sends them to her son with blessings, and he thanks her (25–32). The nobles come with gifts (33–42).
5. Fereydun travels the land, puts right whatever is unjust, and plants cypresses and roses in place of weeds (43–46).

**Key couplets**
- «به روز خجسته سر مهر‌ماه / به سر بر نهاد آن کیانی کلاه» (fereydoon/sh1, 3)
- «نهانش نوا کرد و کس را نگفت / همان راز او داشت اندر نهفت» (sh1, 21)
- «یکی هفته زین گونه بخشید چیز / چنان شد که درویش نشناخت نیز» (sh1, 22)
- «بیاراست گیتی بسان بهشت / به جای گیا سرو گلبن بکشت» (sh1, 46)

**Needs care**
- Low. Zahhak's fall is the background. Say only "the cruel king was gone".
- Wine (7) can be dropped.
- Mehregan is a Zoroastrian-rooted festival (as are Sadeh and Nowruz, already in the app); couplet 9 says "Mehregan worship is his faith". Tell it as a harvest and thanks festival.
- Gender: a strong, generous mother.

**Age band:** 4–6 and 6–8.

**Softening:** none needed.

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh: Ferdowsi links Mehregan to the day Fereydun was crowned, and tells how his mother Faranak gave secretly to the poor for a week."

**Values:** giving quietly without shaming anyone, gratitude, a mother's joy, making the land fair and green. It also gives the app a third festival story (Mehregan) beside Nowruz and Sadeh.

**Questions to ask**
1. Why did Faranak give in secret, without telling anyone?
2. What did Fereydun plant instead of weeds?
3. What could our family do on Mehregan to say thank you?

**Children's adaptations:** none found specific to Faranak or Mehregan [search: none found].

## 8. Fereydun, his mother Faranak and the cow Barmayeh (فریدون و گاو برمایه)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/zahak/sh4 (26 couplets), https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/zahak/sh5 (17) and https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/zahak/sh6 (34) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. In Zahhak's time Fereydun is born. He grows straight as a cypress and shines with royal glory (sh4, 1–6).
2. At the same time the wonderful cow Barmayeh is born, "like a male peacock, every hair a fresh colour". Wise men and star-readers had never seen such a cow (7–10).
3. Zahhak is searching the world. Fereydun's father Abtin is caught by Zahhak's men and killed (12–15).
4. Fereydun's wise mother Faranak runs, grieving, to the meadow of Barmayeh. She begs the keeper: guard this nursing baby, be a father to him, and raise him on this cow's milk (16–23). He agrees, and for three years the baby drinks Barmayeh's milk (24–26).
5. Word of the cow spreads. Faranak, guided by "a thought from God", takes the boy to Mount Alborz, to a holy man who promises to guard him "like a father" (sh5, 1–12).
6. Zahhak comes to the meadow, kills Barmayeh and all the animals, and burns Faranak's house (13–17).
7. At sixteen Fereydun comes down and asks his mother: "Who was my father? Who am I?" (sh6, 1–4). She tells him everything, including what Zahhak did to his father ("they emptied your father's head of brain to feed the dragon", 14), and how Zahhak killed "that kind, wordless nurse" (22).
8. Fereydun burns for revenge. His mother counsels patience: "don't look at the world with the eyes of youth" (24–34).

**Key couplets**
- «همان گاو کش نام برمایه بود / ز گاوان ورا برترین پایه بود // ز مادر جدا شد چو طاووس نر / بهر موی بر تازه رنگی دگر» (zahak/sh4, 7–8)
- «بدو گفت کاین کودک شیرخوار / ز من روزگاری بزنهار دار // پدروارش از مادر اندر پذیر / وزین گاو نغزش بپرور به شیر» (sh4, 21–22)
- «بگو مر مرا تا که بودم پدر؟ / کیم من؟ ز تخم کدامین گهر؟» (sh6, 3)
- «جز این است آیین پیوند و کین / جهان را به چشم جوانی مبین» (sh6, 32)

**Needs care (high)**
- The father's killing, with the brain-feeding detail (sh6, 14). Never include the detail.
- The cow's killing (sh5, 14), the burning of the house, and revenge.
- Separation from mother and fostering by two non-parents (the keeper and the holy man). This is very sensitive for adopted and fostered children, but it can also be affirming: the mother chooses the carers out of love, to keep the child safe, and the carers love him "like a father".
- Fereydun asking "who was my father, who am I?" mirrors questions adopted children ask. Handle it with warmth.
- Religious content is light.

**Age band:** 8–10. A 6–8 version is possible with heavier softening.

**Softening**
- For 6–8: "Fereydun's father had been taken away by the cruel king's soldiers." Faranak hides the baby with the kind keeper and the beautiful cow. When danger comes near, she takes him up the mountain to a wise old man. End with sixteen-year-old Fereydun asking "Who am I?" and his mother telling him he is the son of a good man and loved.
- Barmayeh's fate: "When the cruel king came, the meadow was empty of the boy" — but the poem's line that the cow was killed should not be falsely reversed. Either omit the cow's fate or (8–10) say plainly "the cruel king killed the gentle cow", and add that Fereydun later had a mace shaped like a cow's head. The poem says he drew a "bull/buffalo head" design (zahak/sh7, 81) but does not link it to Barmayeh; the link is a common reading [recall, unverified]. Present it as "some say".

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh: to keep her baby safe from the cruel king, Fereydun's mother Faranak left him in the care of a kind keeper and a wonderful cow, and later a wise man; in the poem his father is killed, which we only hint at."

**Values:** a mother's courage and good judgement, care given by people (and an animal) who are not your birth parents, knowing where you come from, patience over rash revenge (sh6, 29–34).

**Questions to ask**
1. Why did Faranak take baby Fereydun to the meadow, and later up the mountain?
2. Who looked after Fereydun while he grew up?
3. What did his mother mean by "don't look at the world with the eyes of youth"?

**Children's adaptations:** Atoosa Salehi's «ضحاک بنده ابلیس» (Ofogh, vol. 1 of the 12-volume «قصه‌های شاهنامه», for teenagers) retells Zahhak, Fereydun and Kaveh [search]. Mohammad-Reza Shams has a Zahhak book (Mehr News, 2015) [search]. Both target teens, which supports 8–12 here.

## 9. Kiumars, the first king, and the animals (کیومرث و جانوران)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/qmars/sh1 (51 couplets). Use couplets 1–18 only [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. The poet asks who first wore a crown. The old book says Kiumars (1–6).
2. When the sun entered Aries (spring), the world became young (7–8).
3. Kiumars made his home in the mountains and wore leopard skin with his people. New clothes and new food came from him (9–11). He ruled thirty years, shining like the sun (12–13).
4. Every wild beast and creature that saw him grew calm, came and bowed before his throne, and learned their ways from him (14–16).
5. He had a handsome, gifted son, Siamak, whom he loved so much that he wept at the thought of losing him (17–19).
6. Then Ahriman's son, a div, attacks. Siamak fights bare-chested and is torn by the div's claws (35–37).
7. Kiumars, the army, and even the beasts and birds mourn for a year. Then the angel Sorush tells him to stop grieving and fight (38–51).
8. In qmars/sh2, Hushang leads an army of beasts and peris and kills the black div. Kiumars dies.

**Key couplets**
- «چو آمد به برج حَمَل آفتاب / جهان گشت با فرَ و آیین و آب» (qmars/sh1, 7)
- «دد و دام و هر جانور کش بدید / ز گیتی به نزدیک او آرمید» (sh1, 14)
- «کیومرث شد بر جهان کدخدای / نخستین به کوه اندرون ساخت جای» (sh1, 9)

**Needs care**
- Couplets 1–18 are very gentle.
- From 21 on, the story turns to Siamak's violent death, deep grief, revenge and Kiumars' own death. Do not include these for 2–6.
- A 10–12 grief story ("the year the animals mourned with the king") is possible but heavy, and I would not propose it now.

**Age band:** 2–4 and 4–6, as a short picture-like story.

**Softening:** stop at the animals coming to Kiumars. Optionally add (couplets 17–18) that he had a son he loved very much. Stop there.

**Parent note:** "From the opening of the Shahnameh: the first king, Kiumars, lived in the mountains in leopard skins and the animals came to him; the poem goes on to a sad loss, which we leave for later."

**Values:** beginnings, spring, calm and kindness with animals, the bond between parent and child.

**Questions to ask**
1. Where did Kiumars live?
2. Which animals do you think came to him?
3. What would you teach the animals, and what could they teach you?

**Children's adaptations:** «شاهنامه، داستان کیومرث» is listed on Taaghche [search]. Treatment is unknown.

## 10. Manuchehr's birth: the grandfather who saw again (زاده شدن منوچهر)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/fereydoon/sh12 (40 couplets). For context, fereydoon/sh11 is where Fereydun goes blind from weeping [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. After Iraj's death, Fereydun has wept until "his face went dark and his eyes white" (sh11, 14).
2. In Iraj's household is Mah-Afarid, whom Iraj loved and who is carrying his child. She has a daughter who looks just like Iraj (sh12, 1–8). The daughter grows up and marries Pashang (9–10).
3. A son is born, and he is rushed to his great-grandfather. The mobed says: "be glad, look: it is Iraj" (11–13). Fereydun's lips fill with laughter, "as if Iraj were alive" (14).
4. He holds the baby and prays, and God gives him back his sight (15–17). He names the boy Manuchehr (19–20).
5. The child is raised so tenderly that "the wind was not allowed to pass over him" (21–23). He is taught all a king's arts (25) and given the throne, the mace and the armies (26–40). (From here the story turns toward revenge on Salm and Tur.)

**Key couplets**
- «جهان‌بخش را لب پر از خنده شد / تو گفتی مگر ایرجش زنده شد» (fereydoon/sh12, 14)
- «همان کز جهان آفرین کرد یاد / ببخشود و دیده بدو باز داد» (sh12, 17)
- «چنان پروریدش که باد هوا / برو بر گذشتی نبودی روا» (sh12, 21)

**Needs care**
- The background is a son's murder and a grandfather's grief and blindness.
- Mah-Afarid is a «کنیزک», a slave girl or servant (3). Say "a young woman Iraj loved".
- The sequel is a revenge war in which Manuchehr kills his great-uncles (sh17–sh20).
- Name meaning: the naming couplet (20) uses a wine-cup image; skip the image.

**Age band:** 8–10.

**Softening:** "Old King Fereydun had lost his youngest son, and he had cried so much that his eyes grew dim." The baby comes, he laughs, and he sees again. Stop at the loving upbringing.

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh: a grieving, blind old king laughs when his great-grandson is placed in his arms, and the poem says his sight came back; we stop before the story turns to revenge."

**Values:** new life after loss, grandparents, hope, family continuity.

**Questions to ask**
1. Why did the old king's eyes grow dim?
2. What happened when he held the baby?
3. Who in your family is so happy to see you that their face lights up?

**Children's adaptations:** none found [search: none found].

## 11. Sindokht rides to Sam (سیندخت)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh16 (39 couplets) and https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh17 (92 couplets) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. King Manuchehr, angry that Zal wants to marry into Zahhak's line, has ordered Sam to destroy Kabul (sh14 title).
2. Kabul's ruler Mehrab, terrified, threatens to kill his wife Sindokht and their daughter Rudabeh in public to calm the king (sh16, 1–6).
3. Sindokht thinks hard and finds a plan (7–8). "Even a long night ends; the day comes like a bright spring" (11–13). She will go to Sam herself with treasure (17–19). First she makes Mehrab promise not to harm Rudabeh while she is away (23–27).
4. She rides out "like a champion", wearing a Roman helmet, and announces herself only as an envoy from Kabul (sh17, 1–7). She lays rich gifts before Sam (8–11). Sam accepts them for Zal (16–17).
5. She pleads: "if anyone sinned, it was Mehrab; what have the innocent of Kabul done?" (27–29). She asks for his oath that neither she nor her loved ones will be harmed (37–40). Sam gives it (43–44).
6. Then she says who she is: Zahhak's kin, Mehrab's wife, Rudabeh's mother. "Kill me if you must, but do not burn the hearts of Kabul's innocent" (46–53).
7. Sam sees "a woman of wisdom and clear mind". He keeps his oath and agrees to the marriage (54–58).

**Key couplets**
- «اگر چند باشد شب دیریاز / برو تیرگی هم نماند دراز» (manoochehr/sh16, 12)
- «گنهکار گر بود مهراب بود / ... // سر بیگناهان کابل چه کرد / کجا اندر آورد باید بگرد» (sh17, 27–28)
- «دل بیگناهان کابل مسوز / کجا تیره روز اندر آید به روز» (sh17, 53)
- «سخنها چو بشنید ازو پهلوان / زنی دید با رای و روشن روان» (sh17, 54)

**Needs care**
- A husband threatening to kill his wife and daughter (sh16, 4) is domestic violence. Omit it entirely and make Mehrab "frightened and in despair".
- A city threatened with burning.
- The marriage and romance in the background.
- "Zahhak's line" is a kind of hereditary stigma. That is worth a quiet point: Sam judges Sindokht by her wisdom, not her ancestry.
- Wine-bearers (sh17, 20–22) can be omitted.

**Age band:** 10–12. Ages 9+ would work.

**Softening:** "The king of Iran was angry with Kabul because of a quarrel between two families. Sindokht's husband was frightened." Keep her ride, her gifts, her plea for the innocent, the oath and the reveal.

**Parent note:** "From the Shahnameh: Queen Sindokht of Kabul rides alone to the great champion Sam and talks him out of punishing a whole city; we left out her frightened husband's threats."

**Values:** diplomacy, courage without weapons, speaking up for the innocent, judging people by their character and not their family, a strong mother (it complements Gordafarid).

**Questions to ask**
1. Why did Sindokht not say her name at first?
2. What did she mean by "what have the innocent people of Kabul done?"
3. When have words solved a problem better than shouting?

**Children's adaptations:** Rahmandoust's «زال و رودابه» (Kanoon, teen) and Salehi's «فرزند سیمرغ» (teen) cover this arc [search]. Whether Sindokht's mission is kept is unconfirmed.

## 12. Iraj's words of peace (ایرج)

**Source:** https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/fereydoon/sh7 (38 couplets, Iraj and Fereydun) and https://ganjoor.net/ferdousi/shahname/fereydoon/sh10 (43 couplets, the meeting and the killing) [opened].

**What happens in the poem** [opened]
1. Salm and Tur, angry that the youngest got Iran, threaten war. Fereydun tells Iraj to arm himself (sh7, 1–12).
2. Iraj answers that life passes like wind: why plant a tree whose fruit is hatred? He wants no crown, no throne and no army. He will go to his brothers alone and calm their hearts, "which is better than bringing war" (sh7, 14–29). Fereydun: "your brother seeks battle and you seek a feast. From the moon only light comes" (30–31). He fears for Iraj but lets him go (33–38).
3. At the brothers' camp Iraj runs to meet them with a heart full of love (sh10, 4). Tur demands to know why the youngest has the crown (6–10). Iraj replies: "I want neither Iran, nor the West, nor Chin. I give you the crown and the seal; hold no hatred against me" (11–20).
4. Tur, enraged, strikes him with a golden chair. Iraj begs for his life (21–29). Iraj is then killed (title of sh10; graphic detail follows).

**Key couplets**
- «دل کینه ورشان بدین آورم / سزاوارتر زانکه کین آورم» (fereydoon/sh7, 29)
- «برادر همی رزم جوید تو سور / ... ز مه روشنایی نیاید شگفت» (sh7, 30–31)
- «من ایران نخواهم نه خاور نه چین / نه شاهی نه گسترده روی زمین» (sh10, 13)
- «جز از کهتری نیست آیین من / مباد آز و گردن‌کشی دین من» (sh10, 20)

**Needs care (very high)**
- A brother murders a brother, after the victim begs for his life. The head is sent to the father (sh11). Grief, blindness and a generational revenge war follow.
- Othering: this is where the poem places the origin of the Iran–Turan feud, with Tur the founder of Turan and "سالار ترکان و چین" (sh5, 6). Turkic-heritage children, and Iran's Azeri and Turkmen citizens, could hear "Tur is the bad brother = Turks are bad". Avoid that framing.
- Sibling rivalry is real for the target age. A story where peacefulness gets you killed is a hard message.

**Age band:** 10–12 only, if at all.

**Softening:** a 10–12 version could centre on Iraj's speech to his father. Say honestly that "his brothers would not listen, and it ended in great sorrow". No violence on the page, no beheading, no revenge.

**Recommendation:** hold. If the owner wants Iraj, #6 (the naming) gives him his "middle way" moment without the murder.

**Parent note (if used):** "From the Shahnameh: Iraj chose peace over a crown; in the poem his jealous brothers kill him, and the story's grief runs through generations."

**Values:** choosing peace over power, humility, love between siblings.

**Questions to ask**
1. What did Iraj offer his brothers?
2. Why do you think they were jealous?
3. What can you do when someone in your family is angry with you?

**Children's adaptations:** Atoosa Salehi «ایرج» (Ofogh, teen) [search]; Fidibo audio «فرجام فریدون» [search]. Both are aimed at teens, which supports holding this for younger children.

## Not recommended (for the record)

### Zal and Rudabeh (manoochehr/sh4 to sh9)
[opened] Rudabeh offers her long hair as a rope. Zal refuses to hurt her and throws his lasso instead (sh9, 16–25), which is a lovely moment of respect. But the scene goes on with kisses, embraces and wine until dawn (sh9, 36, 48–49). The surrounding story has a father threatening violence, a king ordering Kabul burned, and Rudabeh's family descending from Zahhak.

For a cautious app, use Sindokht (#11) for this arc. The hair-and-lasso moment could appear inside a 10–12 Sindokht story as one line ("Zal would not climb her hair, for fear of hurting her").

### Child Rostam and the white elephant; Mount Sepand
[opened] These are not in Ganjoor's text. They are in Mohl's edition on Wikisource ("کشتن رستم پیل سپید را" and "رفتن رستم به کوه سپند", Mohl vol. 1, about vv. 1814–1935). Many scholars regard them as later additions [recall, unverified]; one search snippet notes such episodes appear in manuscript margins [search].

Content [opened]:
- **White elephant:** after a drinking party, Rostam goes to bed "his head full of wine". A shout comes that Zal's white elephant has broken loose. When the doorkeepers block him for his own safety, he punches one "so that his head became like a ball", breaks the chains on the door, and kills the elephant with one blow of his grandfather's mace. Zal laments the fine elephant.
- **Mount Sepand:** Zal sends him to avenge his great-grandfather Nariman on the fortress of Sepand. Rostam disguises himself as a salt-caravan driver, with his mace hidden in the salt sacks. The salt-starved fortress opens its gates gladly, children and all come to buy, and that night Rostam and his men kill everyone they find ("بکشتند مر هر کرا یافتند"). Then he finds a treasure house.

Problems: a drunk child, violence against a protective servant, killing a household animal, and the massacre of a welcoming town. Only the disguise (a hero hiding a mace in salt sacks) is child-usable, and without the ending it has no point.

Recommendation: do not adapt. The owner's examples "white elephant" and "salt-caravan disguise" are known from children's books [recall, unverified]. If the owner still wants one, a very loose 6–8 "Rostam and the runaway elephant", where his roar calms it, would be an invention and should not be credited to Ferdowsi.

## Problems in existing stories (checked against the poem)

Severity scale: **P1** = factual or safety error to fix now · **P2** = should fix (transparency or age band) · **P3** = polish.

**No P1 problems found.** All seven Shahnameh records I checked match the poem in plot and in the specific details they quote [opened].

### Zal and the Simorgh (`src/data/stories.ts`, id `zal-and-the-simorgh`; poem: manoochehr/sh2, sh3)

- **P2: the note does not disclose the main softening.** In the poem the Simorgh first carries the baby off as food for her hungry chicks. Only "God's mercy" makes them look on him with love instead (manoochehr/sh2, 35–46: «سوی بچگان برد تا بشکرند / بدان نالهٔ زار او ننگرند») [opened]. The app says she "lifted him up and carried him to her nest, and she fed him along with her own young". That is a fine softening, but the other Shahnameh notes disclose their changes and this one does not. Kanoon's own retelling (Rahmandoust) keeps the "food for the chicks" beat, so parents may meet it [search]. Suggested addition to the note: "In the poem the Simorgh first meant him for her chicks, until her heart softened; here she is kind from the start."
- **P2: age band 4–12 (minMonths 48) for a father abandoning his baby because he looks different.** The telling is careful and the note flags it. But the opening is the strongest abandonment beat in the app and is told to 4-year-olds. Consider either starting at 5 (60 months), or adding a line to the note for adoptive and foster families, such as "if your child was adopted, you may want to read it first; the story is about a child who is loved, and a parent who learned".
- **P3: refs.** Only Wikipedia refs are given; every other Shahnameh story cites Ganjoor. Add:
  - `ganjoor('شاهنامه، منوچهر: داستان زاده شدن زال', 'ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh2')`
  - `ganjoor('شاهنامه، منوچهر: بازگشت زال از پیش سیمرغ', 'ferdousi/shahname/manoochehr/sh3')`
- **P3: culture string and note wording differ from the others.** It says "Traditional, Iranian, from Ferdowsi's Shahnameh" and "From a great Iranian poem"; the others say "Persian, from Ferdowsi's Shahnameh" and name Ferdowsi.
- **P3: naming.** "They called him Zāl" at birth: the poem does not name him at birth. The Simorgh's speech calls him Dastan (sh2, 90), and "Zal-e Zar" is used later (sh3, 77). This is harmless.
- **P3: how Sam came to be sorry.** In the poem this happens through two dreams and a rebuke from the mobeds and a dream-rider (sh2, 52–72). The app's "every year he was sorrier" is a fair compression.
- **P3: King Manuchehr's welcome.** The app's "the king honoured" is right. Manuchehr even tells Sam "keep him safe, never hurt him for no reason" (sh3, 22–23), a line worth adding for the abandonment theme.

### Hushang and the First Fire (`hushang-and-the-fire`; hushang/sh2) [opened]
- Accurate: the stone hits a stone, the snake is "not killed" (8), "this light is divine" (11), a fire "like a mountain" (12), the feast named Sadeh (13).
- **P3:** the poem says Hushang "made this fire the qibla" (10) and "it must be worshipped, if you are wise" (11). The app's "honour it and take care of it" is a reasonable softening and needs no change.
- **P3:** the wine in 13 is silently dropped, as in the other stories.

### Jamshid and the First Nowruz (`jamshid-and-nowruz`; jamshid/sh1) [opened]
- Accurate: iron softened for armour (9–10), spinning and weaving (13–15), divs making bricks and bathhouses (34–37), jewels, perfumes and medicine (38–44), ships (45), the divs lifting the throne (49), jewels scattered on Jamshid and the day called the "new day" (52), and "the first of Farvardin" («هرمز فرودین», 53).
- **P3:** the poem says Jamshid made weapons and armour "first" (8). The app's "tools and armour" softens that. Fine.
- The four-class division (18–33) is omitted, rightly.

### How Rostam Found Rakhsh (`rostam-and-rakhsh`; garshasp/sh2) [opened]
- Accurate: pressing each horse's back (4–5), the grey mare (7), the rose-on-saffron coat (11), "three years ready for the saddle" (19), the mother fights "like a lion" (20), the lasso and the roar (21–23), the punch (24), which the note discloses, and "the price is the land of Iran" (31–32).
- **P3: possible enrichment.** The poem says that every night they burned espand (wild rue) for Rakhsh against the evil eye (37), a living Iranian custom that children recognise.
- **P3:** the app's "herds from every pasture in Zabulistan": the poem says the herds came from Zabulistan to Kabulistan (2). Trivial.

### Kaveh the Blacksmith (`kaveh-the-blacksmith`; zahak/sh7, sh12) [opened]
- Accurate in order and detail: the false testimony (14–17), Kaveh's cry at the gate (18–27), the son returned (29), the document torn and trampled (31–35), the apron on a spear (47–48), red, yellow and violet jewels added (57–59), the cow-headed mace (77–83), Sorush's "don't kill him, his time has not come" (sh12, 29), and Damavand (sh12, 61–63).
- **P3:** the app's "your soldiers have taken my son" softens «که مارانت را مغز فرزند من / همی داد باید» (27), and the note discloses this. Good.

### Kavus and the Flying Throne (`kavus-and-the-flying-throne`; hamavaran/sh8, sh9) [opened, spot-checked]
- Confirmed: the eagles fed on "chicken and lamb" (sh8, 26), spears with legs of lamb (29–30), four eagles (31), landing in the forest near Amol (42), "Rostam and Giv and Tus" find him (sh9, 2), forty days of penance (sh9, 24).
- The note correctly says Iblis sends the div. No issues.

### Gordafarid and the White Fortress (`gordafarid-and-the-white-fortress`; sohrab/sh7) [opened, spot-checked]
- Confirmed: Gazhdaham's daughter (1), hair hidden under the mail (6), Sohrab lifts her from the saddle (25), she cuts his spear in two (27), her hair comes loose when the helmet comes off (30–31), his lasso (35), "two armies are watching" (40), "let us settle this in secret" (43), "the army and fortress are yours" (45).
- **P3:** couplet 30 is ambiguous about who takes the helmet off. The app's neutral "her helmet came off" is the safe choice. No issues.
