# 06 — Iranian folk tales by region (round 8)

Research date: 2026-11-20. About 30 searches and fetches, mostly in Persian, plus some in Azerbaijani and Sorani Kurdish.

## Evidence tags

- **[opened]**: I read the page myself, and the claim is on it.
- **[abstract]**: I saw only a paper's abstract.
- **[search]**: the claim came only from search-result snippets or a search summary. I did not open the page.
- **[recall, unverified]**: from my own memory. It must be checked against a printed collection before anyone writes a story from it.

**Honesty note.** I read a complete documented plot for only two tales in this note: *Gonjeshk-e Ashi-Mashi* and *Mah Pishani*. For Jirtdan, Nokhodi, Davidam o Davidam, Kachal and the Div, and the Turkmen *Fox and the Jug*, the sources I opened show that the tale exists, name its hero and give its core idea. The detailed plot steps I give for them are from memory and are marked that way. Before writing, someone should check them against a collection (Anjavi Shirazi's *Qesse-ha-ye Irani*, Behrangi and Dehghani's *Afsane-ha-ye Azarbaijan*, Sobhi's collected tales, Diehji's Turkmen translations). Folk plots are free to use. The wording in all of those books is not. Retell every story in our own words.

## Scope

- **Covered:** folk tales from across Iran's peoples and regions, plus festival lore.
- **Left out:** tales already in the app (Rolling Pumpkin, Uncle Nowruz, Auntie Cockroach, Shangul and Mangul, the Bakhtiari fox who lost his tail, and the Kalila and Masnavi fables listed in `src/data/stories-persian.ts`), and modern authored books.
- **Regions with a usable candidate:** Persian (pan-Iranian, plus Kazerun in Fars and Kashan), Azerbaijani-Turkic, Turkmen, Gilaki and Hormozgani.
- **Gaps:** Kurdish, Baluchi, Khuzestani Arab, Qashqai and Zoroastrian/Yazdi. Searches turned up studies and collection titles but no child-suitable plot I could check. Leads are listed under "Gaps" below.

## 1. گنجشک آشی‌مشی — Ashi-Mashi the Sparrow (the sparrow and the thorn)

- **Region / people:** Kazerun, Fars; a Persian *matal* (rhyming chain tale) [opened]. Versions with the "thorn → bread → …" refrain are told across Iran [recall, unverified].
- **Where documented:**
  - Delgarm reprint, "گنجشک آشی مشی (متل کازرونی)", told by Hassan Hatami (به روایت حسن حاتمی), first printed in *Ketab-e Hafte* in 1340 (1961) [opened].
  - Type: ATU 1655 (*The Profitable Exchange*) or the related ATU 170 chain [recall, unverified]. Check Marzolph's *Typologie des persischen Volksmärchens*.
- **Plot, as documented** [opened]:
  1. A sparrow pecking grain in a field gets a thorn in its foot. It gives the thorn to a baker to keep while it goes to pray.
  2. The thorn falls into the oven and burns, so the sparrow takes the oven in exchange and leaves it with an old woman who is milking her cow.
  3. She hides the oven and says it broke, so the sparrow takes her cow.
  4. It leaves the cow at a wedding house. The guests kill and eat it, so the sparrow takes the bride and carries her to the local ruler (*hakem*), who keeps her.
  5. The sparrow sits on the ruler's roof and is warned off (rain, snow, the pool).
  6. Tired and cross, it goes home, beats a *naqqareh* drum, mocks the ruler as powerless, then flies up into the sky "like a star."
- **Variants:** The urban Tehran-style version is a trade-up rhyme that ends with the sparrow drumming and singing the whole chain of trades (تیغی دادم نون گرفتم …) [recall, unverified]. The thorn, the oven and the drum ending match the Kazerun text [opened].
- **Needs care:**
  - The bride is taken and handed to a ruler as payment. That is a marriage-as-property plot and must go.
  - The cow is killed and eaten. That is mild, but cut it for 2–4.
  - Mocking the ruler is fine. Keep it light, as "nobody can stop a happy sparrow."
- **Age band:** 2–4 (short chain of 4–5 trades) and 4–6 (full chain). It works for toddlers because of the repetition, the cumulative refrain and the small hero.
- **Softening that keeps it recognisable:**
  - Keep the thorn, the baker, the oven, the old woman and her cow, the wedding and the drum.
  - At the wedding, the family has already used the cow's milk for the wedding sweets, so they give the sparrow a little drum (*dombak*) instead.
  - Drop the bride and the ruler.
  - End as documented: the sparrow drums, sings the chain of everything it traded, and flies up into the sky.
- **Parent note:** An old rhyming tale from Kazerun. Let your child join the refrain and count the trades on their fingers.
- **Values:** making the best of a loss, cleverness, keeping promises (people kept things the sparrow trusted them with, or failed to).
- **Questions:**
  1. What did the sparrow start with, and what did it end with?
  2. Was it fair when the old woman hid the oven?
  3. If you had a thorn, what would you swap it for?

## 2. جیرتدان — Jirtdan (Cırtdan)

- **Region / people:** Azerbaijani-Turkic.
  - Wikipedia calls him "one of the heroes of Azerbaijani folk tales," famous because he defeated a div [opened].
  - Parents call small children "cırtdan" affectionately, and lullabies use the word [opened].
  - It has no single author: it is oral folklore, which Abdulla Shaig recorded and published [search].
  - Collections of Turkic folk tales from Tabriz and Ardabil exist (theses by Sarpkaya 2020 and Gülmez 2008, listed on the English "Azerbaijani fairy tales" page) [opened, bibliography only].
- **Where documented:**
  - https://az.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cırtdan [opened; no plot, unsourced]
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jirtdan (about the film): "a child much smaller than his peers relies on intelligence and cunning to defeat a div, a large, hairy, child-eating monster, and lead himself and his brothers to safety"; it notes a resemblance to Hansel and Gretel [opened].
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijani_fairy_tales: Jirtdan is "the most popular character in children's tales," known for "keenness of wit, courage and bravery" [opened].
  - Persian collection: Behrangi and Dehghani, *افسانه‌های آذربایجان* [opened via fidibo]. I did not check whether it contains Jirtdan.
  - Type: ATU 327B (*The Brothers and the Ogre*, Hop-o'-My-Thumb) [recall, unverified].
- **Plot.** Only the core is documented [opened]: a tiny boy leads a group of children (his brothers or village children) to safety from a child-eating div by wit. The detailed steps are [recall, unverified]:
  1. The children go out to gather firewood, are caught by nightfall and shelter in the div's house.
  2. The div plans to eat them in the night. Jirtdan stays awake and says he cannot sleep until he has water carried in a sieve, which sends the div off on an impossible errand.
  3. Jirtdan swaps the children's caps with the head-scarves of the div's own daughters, so the div harms his own children by mistake.
  4. The children flee and cross a river. Jirtdan tells the div to tie a millstone to his neck to cross, and the div drowns.
  5. Some versions have a later episode in which Jirtdan goes back for something.
- **Needs care:**
  - The div harming his own children (in the version I remember) must go.
  - The drowning trick is a deliberate killing.
  - The child-eating threat is the same level as the Rolling Pumpkin wolf, which is acceptable for 4+.
- **Age band:** 4–6 softened (danger of being eaten, all resolved by wit); 6–8 for the fuller night-time escape.
- **Softening:**
  - Keep the woodcutting trip, the div's house, the sleepless trick with the sieve and the escape over the river.
  - The cap swap only makes the div muddle up the beds and shout at an empty room.
  - At the river, Jirtdan's wording leaves the div stuck on the far bank, grumbling, and he never troubles the village again.
  - No one dies.
- **Parent note:** Jirtdan is the most-loved hero of Azerbaijani tales: the smallest child, saved by a quick mind rather than strength.
- **Values:** courage, quick thinking, looking after others, being small is not being weak.
- **Questions:**
  1. Why did the div believe Jirtdan?
  2. What did Jirtdan do that the bigger children did not?
  3. When have you been small but helpful?

## 3. نخودی — Nokhodi, the Chickpea Boy

- **Region / people:** pan-Iranian.
  - Zolfaghari studies 18 versions "told in different regions" [abstract].
  - A 2025 paper studies the water and fire myth in the Azerbaijani Nokhodi [search].
- **Where documented:**
  - Hasan Zolfaghari, "Classifying the folktale of Nokhodi," *Journal of Children's Literature Studies* 7(2), 2017, pp. 75–100 [abstract]. It describes Nokhodi as "one of Iran's well-known magic legends": a very small creature who, with the help of wild animals, gets back what is his from the king. All versions open with the hero's birth.
  - Zolfaghari's three groups [abstract]: «نخودی و حق او از شاه» (ATU 715), «نخود و دیو» (no international type), «نخود و دزدان» (ATU 700).
  - A comparison of the folk «نخودی و دیو» with Asadollah Shabani's rewrite for children [search]. Shabani's wording is authored, so do not use it.
  - Hamshahri reports a custom: after cooking *ash*, women made little Nokhodi dolls for children from a few of the chickpeas and some cloth [opened].
- **Plot.** The core is from the abstract: a tiny hero, helped by wild animals, wins his due from a king, and the story begins with his birth. Detailed steps are [recall, unverified]:
  1. A childless woman cooking *ash* wishes for a child, "even one as small as a chickpea," and a chickpea from the pot becomes a tiny boy.
  2. In the ATU 715 pattern, the king takes or owes him something. On the road Nokhodi gathers helpers (fox, wolf, river) who hide about him.
  3. The king tries to get rid of him: in the henhouse, the stable, the oven. Each time a helper comes out and saves him, until the king pays what he owes.
- **Variants:** In the robbers version (ATU 700) the tiny hero rides in an animal's ear or is stolen and escapes; one group involves a div [abstract].
- **Needs care:**
  - Recalled ATU 715 versions have the helpers eat the king's hens and horses.
  - Choking: never give real chickpeas to under-4s for the doll craft.
- **Age band:** 4–6. The tiny-hero fantasy, the repeated "come out, my fox!" pattern and fairness themes suit this age.
- **Softening:**
  - The fox shoos the hens out, the wolf scatters the horses, and the river puts out the oven fire.
  - The king laughs and pays.
  - Choose and cite one version from Anjavi or Sobhi before writing.
- **Parent note:** Grandmothers once made a little Nokhodi doll from the *ash* chickpeas. Draw one together instead (chickpeas are a choking risk for small children).
- **Values:** fairness, asking for what is yours, friendship, small but brave.
- **Questions:**
  1. Who helped Nokhodi, and how?
  2. Was the king fair at first?
  3. Who would be in your pocket to help you?

## 4. دویدم و دویدم — I Ran and I Ran

- **Region / people:** a pan-Iranian Persian children's matal.
- **Where documented:**
  - Hafez Hatami and Naghmeh Shokraneh, «اسطوره در متل دویدم و دویدم» (*Adab-Pazhuhi*) [search; PDF downloaded but unreadable as text].
  - A modern retelling by Mohammad Hadi Mohammadi is summarised as "a chain of giving that ends in prayer and healing" [search]. That retelling is authored, so do not use its wording.
- **Plot** [recall, unverified]:
  1. The opening: «دویدم و دویدم، سر کوهی رسیدم، دو تا خانمی دیدم».
  2. One woman gives the child bread, the other water. The child gives the water to the earth, the earth gives grass, the grass goes to a goat, the goat gives milk, and so on: each gift passed on brings back another.
  3. Endings vary by region.
- **Needs care:** nothing in the content. The care is about the text: I could not read a documented full text. Get it from a cited collection (Anjavi, Hedayat's *Neyrangestan*/*Owsaneh*, or Sobhi) or from the Hatami paper before writing.
- **Age band:** 2–4. It is short and rhythmic, about giving and receiving, with no villain.
- **Softening:** none needed. Keep the chain to 6–8 links for toddlers.
- **Parent note:** A rhyme Iranian grandparents chanted to toddlers. Say it with a running rhythm and let your child give each gift with their hands.
- **Values:** generosity, nature's cycle (earth, grass, goat, milk), gratitude.
- **Questions:**
  1. What did the earth give back?
  2. Who did the milk go to?
  3. What could you give someone today?

## 5. ماه‌پیشانی — Mah Pishani (Moon-Brow)

- **Region / people:** pan-Iranian. Persian Wikipedia: «در بیشتر نقاط ایران با دگرگونی‌های اندکی رواج دارد» [opened].
- **Where documented:**
  - https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/ماه_پیشونی [opened]. It cites:
    - Morteza Heydari (1394), «ریخت‌شناسی تطبیقی قصه‌های ماه‌پیشانی، یه‌شین و سیندرلا»
    - Bahar Mokhtarian, «گاو برمایه، گرز گاوسر و ماه‌پیشانی»
  - Type: ATU 510A combined with 480 (kind and unkind girls) [recall, unverified]. The page itself gives no ATU number [opened].
- **Plot, as documented** [opened]:
  1. A girl's schoolteacher (*Mollabaji*) tricks her into pushing her own mother into a vat of vinegar, then marries the father.
  2. The mother returns as a yellow cow. The stepmother sets the girl to herd it while spinning cotton.
  3. The cotton blows into a well where a div lives. On the cow's advice, the girl does the opposite of whatever the div asks and wins his favour.
  4. After she washes in a river, a moon grows on her forehead and a star on her chin. The stepsister angers the div and gets a snake on her forehead and a scorpion on her chin, which grow back when cut.
  5. Impossible tasks follow (lentils, a bowl of tears, solved with a rooster and salt).
  6. The girl attends a wedding and loses a shoe, which the prince's searchers use to find her.
  7. The stepmother feigns illness so the cow will be slaughtered. The girl buries the bones.
  8. The stepmother tries to spoil the match. On the wedding night the vizier's son flees the snake-and-scorpion stepsister.
  9. The king throws the stepmother and her daughter into a ditch, and the cow becomes the mother again.
- **Variants:** "rewritten several times" and made into plays [opened]. The page links it to the cow Barmayeh of the Fereydun myth and to Yeh-Shen [opened].
- **Needs care (heavy):**
  - The child's part in her mother's death.
  - Stepmother cruelty (a "wicked stepmother" stereotype that hurts blended families).
  - The animal slaughter.
  - The punishment in a ditch.
  - A marriage-as-reward ending.
- **Age band:** 8–10. It is long, has many episodes and needs some emotional maturity.
- **Softening that keeps it recognisable:**
  - An enchantment has turned the girl's mother into a yellow cow; no child is involved.
  - The new household is unkind, but call her "the woman of the house" and her daughter, rather than stressing "stepmother."
  - Keep the well, the div and the "do the opposite" test, the moon and star, the shoe, and the lentils solved with help.
  - The cow is hidden safely, not slaughtered.
  - Ending: the enchantment breaks, and the mother is human again.
  - The unkind pair are shamed and must undo their mischief, not killed.
  - The prince can stay, but the end is about the mother returning, not about a wedding.
- **Parent note:** Iran's own "Cinderella" story, told across the country for generations. In this telling, kindness and good sense win.
- **Values:** kindness to the unkind, courage, resourcefulness, a mother's love that outlasts hardship.
- **Questions:**
  1. Why did the div reward the girl and not her stepsister?
  2. What helped her finish the impossible tasks?
  3. How is this story like other "lost shoe" stories you know?
- **Also note:** some families have a stepmother. The app's parent note should say plainly that real stepmothers are not like the story.

## 6. کچل و دیو — Kachal and the Div

- **Region / people:** pan-Iranian; prominent in Azerbaijani tales.
  - Fidibo describes Kachal as "a poor young man who lives with his old mother, lazy at times but hard-working when needed, often clashing with a greedy vizier; the div, fox and wolf recur" [opened], citing Behrangi and Dehghani's *Afsane-ha-ye Azarbaijan* [opened].
- **Where documented:**
  - Fidibo [opened, general].
  - The Ali Hatami film *Hasan Kachal* (1970) is authored. Its Persian Wikipedia page describes only the film's plot (the apple trail laid by the mother, Chehel-Gis, the div's life-bottle) and does not separate it from folk tradition [opened]. Do not use the film's plot.
  - The Kachal-and-div strength bluff: I found no source this round [search found none].
- **Plot** [recall, unverified]:
  1. Kachal meets a div and bluffs him in a strength contest (he squeezes "water from a stone," really an egg or cheese).
  2. When the div plans to harm him at night, he puts a log or pillow in his bed and sleeps elsewhere.
  3. The div ends up frightened of him.
  - Types: ATU 1060 and 1115 [recall, unverified].
- **Needs care:**
  - "Kachal" means bald and once implied scalp ringworm, a poor boy's affliction. The story should treat it as his name, never as mockery.
  - The night-time beating of the bed.
- **Age band:** 6–8. Bluff humour needs the child to understand pretending.
- **Softening:** the div pokes the pillow and is amazed that "the strong one" is unhurt. No blows. The div leaves Kachal and his mother alone.
- **Parent note:** Kachal is Iran's classic underdog. He has no money and no strength, but a lot of wit.
- **Values:** wit over force, caring for a parent, standing up to bullies without cruelty.
- **Questions:**
  1. Was Kachal really stronger than the div?
  2. Is it ever OK to trick someone?
  3. What makes someone truly strong?
- **Action before writing:** find a documented Kachal–div tale in Anjavi or Behrangi/Dehghani and cite it. If none is found, drop this candidate.

## 7. روباه و کوزه — The Fox and the Jug (Turkmen)

- **Region / people:** Turkmen.
- **Where documented:**
  - Abdolrahman Diehji (translator), *کله‌پوک و عاقل: ۲۰ افسانهٔ ترکمنی*, Ofogh, 1392 [opened, Taaghche page]. The tales were "passed down orally among the Turkmen." The page does not say whether they were collected in Iran (Turkmen Sahra) or in Turkmenistan.
  - Other titles in the book: «گنجشک دانا»، «روباه در چاه»، «قصر دیوها»، «حیوان عجیب»، «گلنار» [opened].
- **Plot:**
  - A fox waits in its den for days because it hears people talking nearby. When it comes out, it finds the "voices" came from a jug (the wind in it) and the people left long ago. The fox decides to take revenge on the jug [opened, synopsis].
  - What happens next, the fox tying the jug to its neck to drown it and being pulled under, is [recall, unverified] (perhaps ATU 68A-like). Check it in the book.
- **Needs care:**
  - The recalled ending (the fox drowns).
  - The Iranian origin is unconfirmed. The Persian app is Iran-only, so confirm the narrators are from Iranian Turkmen Sahra, or frame it as "a Turkmen tale from Golestan" only if the book supports that.
- **Age band:** 4–6. It is short, the misunderstanding is silly, and it is about anger at things.
- **Softening:** the jug sinks, and the fox scrambles out dripping and laughing at himself.
- **Parent note:** A Turkmen fox tale about a fox who got cross with a jug. Have you ever been cross with a thing?
- **Values:** checking before fearing, letting go of anger, laughing at ourselves.
- **Questions:**
  1. What was really making the noise?
  2. Can a jug be naughty?
  3. What do you do when you feel cross with a toy?
- **Note:** another tale in the same book has a fox trap a wolf, eat its meat and tell it to "close your eyes and sleep" [opened]. That is a mean trickster, so not recommended.

## 8. ننه سرما و پسرانش اهمن و بهمن — Naneh Sarma, Ahman and Bahman

- **Region / people:** central Iran. Told at a Tehran workshop together with Kashan's late-Esfand *ash* [opened].
- **Where documented:** Hamshahri Online 927926 [opened].
  - It names the winter grandmother Naneh Sarma (also «سرماپیرزن» and «خاله عجوزه»), her elder son Ahman and younger son Bahman, who quarrel, and a story running through the winter days.
  - It mentions a lost sister, Hut, and Naneh Sarma's broom.
- **Plot:** only the fragment above is [opened]. The familiar connection to "the old woman's cold days" at the end of winter, when cold returns after someone boasts that winter is over, is [recall, unverified].
- **Variants:** Naneh Sarma also appears in the Uncle Nowruz tale already in the app [recall, unverified].
- **Needs care:**
  - The plot is too thin to write from. Find Anjavi's or Enjavi-era calendar lore, or the *Jashn-ha va adab-o-rosum* sources, first.
  - Avoid any frightening "she will burn the world" line for small children [opened, the article quotes a threat-like line].
- **Age band:** 6–8. It explains the seasons and the calendar, from Yalda to Nowruz.
- **Softening:** Naneh Sarma as a grumpy but fair grandmother who sweeps winter away with her broom when spring comes.
- **Parent note:** Old Iranian families explained winter's last cold snaps with stories of Naneh Sarma and her sons. Tell it on a cold Esfand evening.
- **Values:** patience, the turning year, family quarrels mended.
- **Questions:**
  1. Why do you think the last days of winter can be so cold?
  2. Why did the brothers quarrel?
  3. What do you look forward to when spring comes?

## 9. ملانصرالدین — Mulla Nasreddin anecdotes

- **Region / people:** pan-Iranian; especially strong in Azerbaijani and Persian oral humour [recall, unverified].
- **Where documented:** not researched this round. Open the Persian Wikipedia page «ملانصرالدین» and a classic Persian anecdote collection before choosing.
- **Plot** [recall, unverified]: short anecdotes such as:
  - "Eat, my coat!": he is ignored at a feast in old clothes and honoured in fine ones.
  - "The pot that gave birth": he lends a pot, returns it with a small pot "born" to it, and later says the pot "died."
  - The donkey, the son and the critics.
- **Needs care:**
  - Some anecdotes mock wives, foreigners or religious figures. Avoid those.
  - The Mulla is shared across borders. The Persian app must not name other countries.
- **Age band:** 8–10 and 10–12. These are about irony and fairness.
- **Softening:** pick anecdotes without cruelty or ridicule of groups.
- **Parent note:** Iran's wise fool. Ask your child where the joke is.
- **Values:** honesty, seeing past appearances, laughing at greed.
- **Questions:**
  1. Was the Mulla foolish or clever here?
  2. What did he want people to notice?
  3. Can you think of a time when looks fooled people?

## 10. (Gilaki) The farmer who danced until dawn

- **Region / people:** Gilan.
- **Where documented:** Fidibo blog [opened], under the title «حسن با له می‌زند» as given there. The title may be garbled; check it in Ali Taslimi's *افسانه‌های مردم گیلان* (Rasht, 1390) [opened as a reference in https://fis.khu.ac.ir/article_3906.html via search; search].
- **Plot** [opened, thin]:
  - A farmer worried about his harvest leaves home before dawn, thinking it is morning.
  - At his field he finds creatures dancing around his cut crop and must stay and dance with them until sunrise.
  - Fidibo also says Gilaki tales centre on planting and harvest [opened].
- **Needs care:**
  - Supernatural night creatures (*aal* or jinn are likely in the local belief; [recall, unverified]).
  - The thin plot.
- **Age band:** 6–8, softened into a friendly midnight harvest dance.
- **Softening:** the creatures are friendly field folk celebrating the harvest. The farmer dances, the sun rises, and he finds his crop safe.
- **Parent note:** From the rice fields of Gilan, where farmers' worries became stories.
- **Values:** patience, respect for the land, worries that turn out fine.
- **Questions:**
  1. Why did the farmer go out so early?
  2. Who were the dancers, do you think?
  3. What worries go away when the sun comes up?

## 11. (Gilaki) The snake child who sheds his skin

- **Region / people:** Gilan.
- **Where documented:** Fidibo [opened].
  - A childless woman prays for a child and bears a snake-like son who becomes a handsome young man each time he sheds his skin. Fidibo links it to the local belief about snakes shedding skin.
  - Type: ATU 433B or 425A [recall, unverified].
- **Plot:** only the above is [opened]. The usual continuation (a bride, the burned skin, a search to win him back) is [recall, unverified].
- **Needs care:** the marriage plot, burning the skin, and a possibly frightening transformation.
- **Age band:** 10–12.
- **Softening:** focus on a mother's love for an unusual child and the son's choice to show his true self. Keep any marriage brief.
- **Parent note:** A Gilaki wonder tale about a child who was different, and loved.
- **Values:** acceptance, patience, loyalty.
- **Questions:**
  1. How did the mother feel about her unusual son?
  2. What does "shedding a skin" mean when we grow?
  3. Was it right to burn the skin without asking?

## 12. بَپ دریا — Bap-e Darya, the Father of the Sea (Hormozgan)

- **Region / people:** the Hormozgan coast.
- **Where documented:**
  - Fidibo [opened]: a sea-ruler with an ugly body covered in shells and sea creatures and fish-fin hands and feet, believed to attack anyone who comes near. Families kept children off the shore at night. He fears the sound of iron, so striking two irons together drives him away.
  - Gholamhossein Saedi described him in the ethnography *Ahl-e Hava* [opened via Fidibo]. Saedi's text is authored, so use only the motif.
- **Plot:** a legend rather than a plotted tale [opened].
- **Needs care:**
  - It is a fear legend used to control children, which goes against the app's parenting stance.
  - The scary description.
- **Age band:** 8–10, only as "a legend coastal grandparents told."
- **Softening:** frame it explicitly: grown-ups told it because the night sea is dangerous. Pair it with a real safety line (never go to the water without an adult). Make the clash of iron the brave, funny part.
- **Parent note:** On Iran's southern coast, Bap-e Darya was the story that kept children away from the dark sea. Talk with your child about why.
- **Values:** respect for the sea, safety, how stories carry warnings.
- **Questions:**
  1. Why did grown-ups tell this story?
  2. What is the real danger at the sea at night?
  3. What other warning stories do you know?

## Keep out, and why

- **بلبل سرگشته (Bolbol-e Sargashteh).** ATU 720 (*My Mother Slew Me*) [search, ensani 463634]: a child is murdered, the bones are buried under a tree and the child returns as a bird. It cannot be softened without losing the tale. Keep it out at every age.
- **خروس زری پیرهن پری.** An authored work by Ahmad Shamlou (Cheshmeh) [search]. A separate folk rooster-and-fox version exists on Beytoote [search], but its Iranian folk origin is unconfirmed. Not ours to copy.
- **حسن کچل (film plot).** Ali Hatami's 1970 film is authored [opened]. Use only the folk Kachal (#6), and only if documented.
- **Samad Behrangi's *Qesse-ha-ye Behrang*** (e.g. «کچل کفترباز»، «پیرزن و جوجهٔ طلایی‌اش»، «یک هلو هزار هلو»). Authored, or Behrangi's rewritten wording [opened, Wikisource index; folk/authored status not marked there]. Use only plots documented separately as folklore, e.g. in the Behrangi–Dehghani *Afsane-ha-ye Azarbaijan*, and still retell them in our own words.
- **Gorg o Bozghaleh variants** (بزبزقندی، بزک زنگوله‌پا). The same story as Shangul and Mangul, already in the app.
- **The mouse-tail chain** (موش دم‌بریده). The same chain type as the Bakhtiari fox tail already in the app [recall, unverified].
- **Tig-tig Khanim** (Azerbaijani). A character in the Khale Suske family [opened, listed on the "Azerbaijani fairy tales" page]. It duplicates Auntie Cockroach.
- **The Bakhtiari Lori matal with an ogre sister.** A study treats its characters as a sister-ogre and a divine brother [search]. Cannibal-sister tales are too frightening.
- **Haji Firuz.** Blackface tradition. Avoid, per the brief.
- **Adult romances and epics:** Mem and Zin, Khurshid and Kharaman (Kurdish) [search, shafaq.com title only], Hani and Shey Mureed (Baluchi) [recall, unverified], Ashiq Gharib and Koroghlu [opened, named on Fidibo]. They are tragic or adult love stories, or long battle epics. A 10–12 Koroghlu episode is possible later, with care.
- **The Pir-e Sabz (Chak-Chak) legend** (Zoroastrian, Yazd) [recall, unverified]. A princess fleeing an invading army: a religious and political conflict narrative. Not for this shelf.
- **Gorgi'an (گرگیعان), Khuzestan.** A mid-Ramadan children's door-to-door custom (verse: «ماجینه یا ماجینه…») [search]. It is a custom, not a tale, and religious in framing. It might fit a festivals or customs section, not stories.

### 3. Naneh Sarma: slightly fuller, still thin

- **From the Hamshahri workshop report** (927926, opened in round 1; extra lines surfaced by search [search]):
  - The Kashan winter tale covers the passing of «چارچار» (the great and the small *chelleh*), «جنگ اهمن و بهمن» (the fight between Ahman and Bahman), and «گم‌شدن آفتاب‌به‌هود (حوت)» (the loss of their sister, Aftab-be-Hud, i.e. Hut, the month of Esfand's sun).
  - Naneh Sarma rises from the korsi, swings her broom and calls for her sons. Her line threatens to "set the world on fire," which should be dropped for young children.
- **Mythic background** [search, ensani 632796]: Bahman is linked to Vohu Manah, the spirit of good mind, set against the destructive spirit. The end of Bahman brings Esfand and the news of spring.
- **Still missing:** a sequence of events. The Hamshahri "Yalda story" (154550) is a modern authored teen story by Samira Ghayoumi, not folklore [opened]. Do not use it.
- **Recommendation:** keep #8 at care. It is enough for a short "why the last winter days are cold" explainer only if a Kashan folklore source is read.
