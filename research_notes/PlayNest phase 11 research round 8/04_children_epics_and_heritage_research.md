# 04 — Old epics and folk tales with children: fear, death, morals, violence, heritage and gender

Research notes for PlayNest phase 11, round 8. Researched 20 Nov 2026.

## 1. Scope, and what is already in the app

The owner is adding many stories from the Shahnameh and other Persian heritage tales, adapted by age. This note asks what
research says about sharing old epics and folk tales (battles, monsters, death, cruelty, sad endings) with children aged
0 to 12, and about the benefits of heritage stories. It ends with house rules for adapting tales, an outline for one parent
guide that works in both apps, and short tips.

**Already in the app** (read for overlap, not fully audited):

- `stories-persian.ts` already softens epic material and says so in each `note`: Rostam's roar replaces striking Rakhsh;
  Kaveh leaves unsaid what the snakes were fed; Gordafarid's battle is less bloody; Jamshid's story stops before his fall.
  Arash (`minMonths: 96`, `stance: 'care'`), Kaveh (108, care) and Gordafarid (108, open) are already set for older children.
  This matches the evidence below. No problem found.
- `family-stories` (guides-more.ts): already cites Duke, Lazarus & Fivush 2008 and Fivush's "Do You Know?" post, says "the
  value is in the sharing" and "Family warmth may explain part of that link". That is correctly cautious (see §8). No change.
- `parent-ill-or-bereaved` (guides-more.ts): plain words for death, "Avoid 'gone to sleep'". Consistent with §4.
- `when-scary-things-happen` (guides-hard-times.ts) and `anxious-children`: real-world fear. The new guide should link to them
  and must not duplicate them.
- `reading-every-age` and the dialogic-reading source: shared reading evidence already researched in
  `research_notes/PlayNest phase 5 app research/09_folk_tales_and_shared_reading.md`. §9 below only adds what is new.

**No P1 or P2 problems were found in the content I looked at.**

## 3. Fright reactions by age, and what calms fear

### Findings

- **What frightens young children is how things look.** Cantor: for preschool children (about 2 to 7), "how something looks
  is the most important determinant of whether it will be scary": monsters, repulsive creatures, graphic injuries, deformity,
  and characters transforming before their eyes. Because they cannot fully separate fantasy from reality, "the fact that
  something is blatantly fantastic or impossible" does not make it less scary. **[opened]** Cantor J, "Children's fright
  responses to television and films", reprinted from *News on Children and Violence on the Screen*, UNESCO International
  Clearinghouse on Children and Violence on the Screen, Nordicom (Göteborg University). https://www.kidsfirst.org/articles-info/fright.htm
- **Older children fear realistic, abstract threats.** For about 8 to 12, appearance matters less; children weigh motives and
  become more frightened by real events (news) and by fiction that makes them aware of their own vulnerability. **[opened]**
  same source.
- **Experiments behind the shift.** Cantor's encyclopedia review: preschoolers were most frightened when a kind hero turned into
  a monstrous-looking form, while older children were least frightened because they knew it was still the hero; children aged
  about 6 to 10 judged a character by whether she was kind or cruel, preschoolers by her looks. Parent surveys found fear from
  fantasy programmes fell with school grade while fear from news rose. Teenagers were more disturbed than younger children by
  abstract possibilities (a conflict spreading) than by images. **[opened]** Cantor J, "Fear and the Media", *Encyclopedia of
  Communication and Information* (Gale), via https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/fear-and-media
- **Fear can last.** In a random phone survey in one US city, 43% of parents said a child had been frightened by TV beyond the
  viewing; in retrospective reports by university students, frights often lasted a week or more and a quarter still felt some
  residual fear years later. **[opened]** Cantor (kidsfirst page). Population: US parents and US university students
  (retrospective self-report, a link, not proof of cause). A Dutch telephone survey of children aged 7 to 12 reported 31%
  frightened by TV in the past year, 21% of them for weeks or months. **[search]** Valkenburg PM, Cantor J, Peeters AL.
  Fright reactions to television: a child survey. *Communication Research* 2000;27(1):82–99.
- **News frightens more than fiction, younger more than older, girls more than boys.** 572 Dutch children aged 8 to 12 rated
  eight violent threats (murder, war, house fires). Described as news, they caused more fright than when described as fiction;
  fright and worry were greater in girls and in younger children. **[abstract]** van der Molen JH, Bushman BJ. Children's direct
  fright and worry reactions to violence in fiction and news television programs. *J Pediatr* 2008;153(3):420–4.
  doi:10.1016/j.jpeds.2008.03.036. Implication for epics: older children may be more troubled by a tale presented as "this
  really happened" than as a story.
- **Girls report more fear** (meta-analytic effect around 0.41 on self-report; gap smaller on heart rate and facial measures,
  partly socialisation). **[opened]** Cantor, "Fear and the Media" (citing Peck 1999). Use only as "children differ"; never
  as a reason to give boys scarier stories.

### What calms fear, by age

- **Under about 7: non-verbal help works best.** Physical comfort and closeness (a hug, holding a toy or blanket, a warm drink),
  distraction, and gradual exposure in small doses for milder fears. Telling preschoolers a frightening scene "isn't real" did
  not reduce their fear in experiments, while it did help older children. **[opened]** Cantor, both sources.
- **Older children: explanation works.** For fantasy, a reminder that it isn't real; for real events, honest reassuring
  information, and how to stay safe. Explanations about low probability are "relatively ineffective". Cognitive strategies are
  consistently "more effective for older than for younger children". With young children, simple reassurance shown visually
  and repeated helps more than words alone. **[opened]** Cantor, both sources.
- **All ages want a parent.** "The sincere, concerned attention of a parent or adult care-giver is what children of all ages
  seem to seek the most." **[opened]** Cantor (kidsfirst page). Expert opinion summarising her research.
- **Children's own strategies change with age.** 48 children aged about 4, 5 and 7 heard stories about a child meeting a real
  or imaginary scary creature. With age, children suggested more "reality affirmation" (reminding oneself what is real) and
  fewer "positive pretence" strategies (imagining a friendly ghost), and better understood that thoughts can raise and lower
  fear. **[abstract]** Sayfan L, Lagattuta KH. Scaring the monster away: what children know about managing fears of real and
  imaginary creatures. *Child Dev* 2009;80(6):1756–74. doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01366.x. Small lab study.
  A companion study (N = 64, ages 3, 5, 7 and adults) found children increasingly understood that fear depends on what a person
  thinks. **[abstract]** Sayfan L, Lagattuta KH. *Child Dev* 2008;79(4):821–35. doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.2008.01161.x
- The children's coping scale used by Valkenburg et al. has four groups: physical intervention (close eyes, look away), cognitive
  reassurance ("it will end all right", "it's not real"), social support (sit on a parent's lap, talk to a parent, hold a toy)
  and escape. **[opened]** CCAM/ASCoR instrument page, https://www.ccam-ascor.nl/coping-strategies-to-reduce-fear-from-tv
  (items only; no age results on that page).

**Caveat.** Almost all of this research is about **screens** (TV and film). Oral telling lets the teller slow down, soften and
watch the child's face, and pictures matter most to young children; it is a reasonable inference, not a finding, that a told
or read story is less frightening than the same scene on screen. **We don't know** from direct studies.

## 4. Children's understanding of death, and heroes who die

- **Ages 5 to 7.** A review of three components (irreversibility, non-functionality, universality) concluded that "the majority
  of healthy children in modern urban-industrial societies achieve an understanding of all 3 components between 5 and 7 years
  of age." **[abstract]** Speece MW, Brent SB. Children's understanding of death: a review of three components of a death
  concept. *Child Dev* 1984;55(5):1671–86. doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.1984.tb00410.x. Population named in the abstract: children
  in urban-industrial societies; old review. The app's `parent-ill-or-bereaved` table already says "by about 5 to 7".
- **Biological understanding is linked with less fear.** 90 children aged 4 to 8: more mature death understanding was linked
  with lower fear of death, controlling for age and general anxiety, "some empirical support for the widely held belief that
  discussing death and dying in biological terms is the best way to alleviate fear of death in young children." **[abstract]**
  Slaughter V, Griffiths M. Death understanding and fear of death in young children. *Clin Child Psychol Psychiatry*
  2007;12(4):525–35. doi:10.1177/1359104507080980. Cross-sectional: a link, not proof.
- **Where children learn about death:** direct experience, parents' talk, and "portrayals of death in the media and the arts".
  **[abstract]** Longbottom S, Slaughter V. Sources of children's knowledge about death and dying. *Phil Trans R Soc B*
  2018;373(1754):20170267. doi:10.1098/rstb.2017.0267. (Full text blocked; its detail on media portrayals is unread.)
- **Open family talk matters.** 114 children aged 5 to 6 in one Chinese province: subcomponents were acquired in order but
  unevenly, and whether the family talked openly about death was significantly related to understanding of universality.
  **[abstract]** Jingjing L, Fuyan L. *Front Psychol* 2024;15:1376253. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1376253. Population: one region.
- **Bereaved children's questions** (710 anonymous questions, ages 5 to 17, one bereavement camp) span the body, illness, dying
  and grief; adults often feel unprepared to answer. **[abstract]** Eaton Russell C, et al. *Palliat Care Soc Pract*
  2026;20. doi:10.1177/26323524261460926.

**Implications for Arash, Sohrab, Siavash, Iraj** (inference from the above, not tested on epics):

- Under about 5, a child may not grasp that death is permanent, so a hero's death can be confusing ("When does he wake up?") or
  frightening without meaning. Leave on-page deaths of heroes and parents out of stories for under-6s.
- 6 to 7: the concept is forming. A death can be told if it is brief, in plain words ("he died"), not graphic, and followed by
  what the people left behind did (remembered him, made things right).
- 8 to 12: tragic endings (Sohrab killed by his father unknowingly; Siavash's unjust killing; Iraj killed by his brothers)
  can be told, with a parent present and time to talk. The hardest part for this age is likely to be the **injustice and
  betrayal** rather than the death itself (realistic, abstract threats; §3). Iraj and Siavash are murders within families or
  by a ruler; treat them as "care" stories for 10+ at least, and avoid detail of how they were killed.
- Arash's self-sacrifice: the app already sets 8+ with "care". Fine. Avoid presenting dying for a cause as something a child
  should copy; frame it as a legend about giving your all.
- Before a story with a death, check whether the child has had a recent loss; a story death can bring a real one back.
  (Expert opinion, consistent with `parent-ill-or-bereaved`.)

## 5. Fantasy and reality, monsters and divs

- **Children are often sceptics, not believers.** Woolley's review argues that "when assessing reality status, children are as
  likely to doubt as they are to believe", and that immature metacognition, not credulity, is the root of errors.
  **[abstract]** Woolley JD, Ghossainy ME. Revisiting the fantasy-reality distinction: children as naïve skeptics. *Child Dev*
  2013;84(5):1496–510. doi:10.1111/cdev.12081.
- **Fear blurs the line.** 80 children aged 4 to 6 with severe night-time fears showed more fantasy-reality confusion than 32
  non-fearful controls, more so in younger children. **[abstract]** Zisenwine T, Kaplan M, Kushnir J, Sadeh A. Nighttime fears
  and fantasy-reality differentiation in preschool children. *Child Psychiatry Hum Dev* 2013;44(1):186–99.
  doi:10.1007/s10578-012-0318-x. Preliminary, one country (Israel), cross-sectional.
- 62 preschoolers judging storybook pictures: about-5-year-olds but not about-4-year-olds told fantasy from real events, and
  frightening content made judgements worse. **[search]** Samuels A, Taylor M. *Br J Dev Psychol* 1994;12:417–27.
- Children aged 3 to 5 (N = 214, two preregistered studies) learned a cause-and-effect fact from fantasy storybooks; 5-year-olds
  and some 4-year-olds generalised it to the real world, while fantasy elements can interfere with younger children's learning
  ("the reader's dilemma"). **[abstract]** Brenner J, Steele K, Woolley JD. *J Exp Child Psychol* 2025;253:106198.
  doi:10.1016/j.jecp.2025.106198.
- Classic "imagined monster in a box" experiments (Harris et al. 1991) showing children hesitate near a box they had only
  imagined a monster in. **[recall, unverified]**

**Implications for divs, dragons and Zahhak's snakes:** under about 5, a vivid div is as scary as a real threat, and "it's only
pretend" does not help (§3). Describe divs by what they do, keep them outwitted or tamed, skip grotesque description and
transformation scenes, and avoid scary voices or pictures. From about 5 to 7, a calm "divs are in old stories, not in our
house" can start to help; from 8, children can enjoy the monster as a story device.

## 6. Do children take the "moral" from stories?

- **Young children miss the moral unless helped.** "The ability to spontaneously extract 'the moral' of a story develops
  relatively late"; children "represent stories at a concrete level". In two experiments with 5- and 6-year-olds, prompts to
  **explain** parts of the story helped them abstract the moral and apply it to new situations, and "in some cases, generating
  an explanation is more effective than being explicitly told the moral." **[abstract]** Walker CM, Lombrozo T. Explaining the
  moral of the story. *Cognition* 2017;167:266–81. doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2016.11.007. Lab studies, small samples.
- Asking 5- and 6-year-olds to explain (not just report) also helped them find abstract rules in a different task.
  **[abstract]** Brockbank E, Lombrozo T, Gopnik A, Walker CM. *Dev Sci* 2023;26(1):e13274. doi:10.1111/desc.13274.
- Moral theme comprehension improves through middle childhood: third graders did worse than fifth graders and adults, and
  moral themes were understood better than "prudential" ones. **[search]** Narvaez D, Gleason T, Mitchell C, Bentley J. Moral
  theme comprehension in children. *J Educ Psychol* 1999;91(3):477–87. (A secondary source claims third graders got the theme
  about 10% of the time: **unverified, do not use**.)
- **Realistic human characters beat talking animals for behaviour.** Preschoolers read a sharing story with human characters
  gave more afterwards; the same story with anthropomorphised animals, or a control story, decreased giving. **[abstract]**
  Larsen NE, Lee K, Ganea PA. Do storybooks with anthropomorphized animal characters promote prosocial behaviors in young
  children? *Dev Sci* 2018;21(3):e12590. doi:10.1111/desc.12590. One lab study; not yet shown to replicate.
- **Praise the good, don't threaten.** In 3- to 7-year-olds, "Pinocchio" and "The Boy Who Cried Wolf" did not reduce lying;
  "George Washington and the Cherry Tree" increased truth-telling, because it shows the **positive** consequences of honesty;
  when altered to stress punishment, it stopped working. **[abstract]** Lee K, Talwar V, McCarthy A, Ross I, Evans A, Arruda C.
  Can classic moral stories promote honesty in children? *Psychol Sci* 2014;25(8):1630–6. doi:10.1177/0956797614536401.
  One study, North American sample.

**Implications:** epics are full of punishment and revenge. For young children, a moral is more likely to land if the story
shows a character **rewarded or admired for doing right** (Gordafarid's wit, Kaveh's courage, Rostam choosing to help) than if
it dwells on the punishment of wrongdoers. Don't tack on a lecture; ask "Why do you think she did that?" and let the child
explain. Real people and human characters may carry a moral better than animal fables for under-6s (one study).

## 7. Violence in stories and aggression

- **Screens: most bodies see a risk, but the size is disputed.** The AAP is "concerned about children's exposure to virtual
  violence" and makes recommendations for parents. **[abstract]** AAP Council on Communications and Media. Virtual violence.
  *Pediatrics* 2016;138(2):e20161298. doi:10.1542/peds.2016-1298. A major review found "unequivocal evidence that media
  violence increases the likelihood of aggressive and violent behavior", clearest for TV and film. **[abstract]** Anderson CA,
  Berkowitz L, Donnerstein E, et al. The influence of media violence on youth. *Psychol Sci Public Interest* 2003;4(3):81–110.
  doi:10.1111/j.1529-1006.2003.pspi_1433.x. **Repeats:** later meta-analyses (Ferguson and others) found much smaller effects
  after correcting for publication bias, and the field disputes whether they matter. **[search]**. Rule from §4 of governance
  applies: "Bodies differ."
- **Print: very little evidence.** In two short-term experiments, people who read a physically aggressive story were then more
  physically aggressive, and readers of a relationally aggressive story more relationally aggressive. **[abstract]** Coyne SM,
  Ridge R, Stevens M, Callister M, Stockdale L. Backbiting and bloodshed in books. *Br J Soc Psychol* 2012;51(1):188–96.
  doi:10.1111/j.2044-8309.2011.02053.x. Sample: I recall university students (**[recall, unverified]**; the abstract does not
  say). No study I found tested **oral** telling of epics to children.
- **Positive content helps.** 168 kindergarten children (teacher-nominated as aggressive, China) were less aggressive right after
  a prosocial cartoon than a non-prosocial one. **[abstract]** Zhang Q. *Front Psychol* 2021;12:742568. Reading prosocial
  content in books was linked with prosocial behaviour in 602 adolescents (China; survey; a link, not proof). **[abstract]**
  Li W, et al. *Front Psychol* 2022;13:973481. Meta-analyses of prosocial TV (Mares & Woodard 2005) **[search]**.
- **Heroic/justified violence.** Media-violence researchers have argued that violence shown as justified, rewarded or done by an
  attractive hero is more likely to be imitated. **[recall, unverified]** (Paik & Comstock 1994; US National Television
  Violence Study). Not confirmed this session; do not cite.

**Implications:** there is no evidence that hearing an epic makes a child aggressive, and no evidence that it doesn't. The
cautious path: keep battles short and without gore; give the cost of fighting (grief, loss) its place; let cleverness, mercy,
help and keeping promises solve problems at least as often as strength; don't make killing the climax for young children;
and never map ancient enemies (Turan, "the enemy") onto living peoples, faiths or nations (also the governance rule on
political neutrality).

## 8. Family and heritage stories and child wellbeing

- **"Do You Know?" scale.** Fivush: "children and adolescents who know more of their family stories show higher well-being on
  multiple measures", including self-esteem and fewer behaviour problems; "it is the process of families sharing stories about
  their lives that is important", and the 20 questions are "a starting point". Cites Duke MP, Lazarus A, Fivush R. Knowledge
  of family history as a clinically useful index of psychological well-being and prognosis. *Psychotherapy* 2008;45:268–72.
  **[opened]** Fivush R, Psychology Today, 19 Nov 2016. The underlying study (66 adolescents aged 14 to 16, middle-class US
  families, correlational at one time point) **[search]**. Already used correctly in `family-stories`.
- **Intergenerational storytelling as a resource.** A theory/review paper argues the intergenerational transmission of stories
  about one's group supports positive development and is "especially important for groups who are marginalized".
  **[abstract]** Weststrate NM, McLean KC, Fivush R. Intergenerational storytelling and positive psychosocial development.
  *Pers Soc Psychol Rev* 2024;28(4):351–71. doi:10.1177/10888683241259902. Theory, not a trial.
- Open, elaborative parent–child talk was the common thread linked with better child mental health in a special issue on
  families during the pandemic. **[abstract]** Fivush R. *Dev Psychol* 2026;62(10):2070–4. doi:10.1037/dev0002206.
- **Cultural socialisation.** Hughes et al.'s review named four parental practices (cultural socialisation, preparation for bias,
  promotion of mistrust, egalitarianism); later papers report cultural socialisation (teaching heritage, history, traditions,
  pride) is the most common and most consistently linked with positive outcomes such as ethnic identity. **[search]** Hughes D,
  Rodriguez J, Smith EP, Johnson DJ, Stevenson HC, Spicer P. Parents' ethnic-racial socialization practices: a review of
  research and directions for future study. *Dev Psychol* 2006;42(5):747–70. A later review by Hughes treats identity and
  socialisation as "resources" for minority youth. **[abstract]** Hughes DL, Watford JA, Del Toro J. *Adv Child Dev Behav*
  2016;51:1–41. Latino adolescents with high family ethnic socialisation and identity (one of four profiles) showed better
  academic adjustment. **[abstract]** Martinez-Fuentes S, Umaña-Taylor AJ, et al. *J Youth Adolesc* 2026;55(6):1472–88.
  A school programme on cultural identity (the Identity Project, adapted in Italy, 173 adolescents): youth whose identity
  "resolution" grew had the best outcomes. **[abstract]** Ceccon C, ..., Umaña-Taylor AJ. *J Youth Adolesc* 2024;53(11):2443–59.
  **Population:** almost all this research is on ethnic-minority adolescents in the US or Europe, not young children and not
  majority-culture children at home. Use as "linked with", for older children, and never as proof.
- **Heritage language.** Longer early exposure to the home language at home was the main predictor of heritage-language
  vocabulary in narratives by 143 pre- and primary-school children. **[abstract]** Rodina Y, et al. *Front Psychol*
  2023;14:1101995. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1101995. Studies of Persian-speaking families abroad found success depended on parents'
  beliefs and small daily practices, and children drifted to the school language once school started. **[search]** Gharibi K,
  Mirvahedi SH. *J Multiling Multicult Dev* 2021 (doi:10.1080/01434632.2021.1935974); Gharibi K, *British Academy Review* 33.
  No study found that tested storytelling itself as a heritage-language intervention: **we don't know** if stories beat other
  home-language talk. The inference that stories are an enjoyable way to give more home-language input is reasonable.
  (Persian app: do not name the countries these studies were done in.)

## 9. Telling vs reading aloud; dialogic questions; repetition

(See the phase 5 note 09 for the main shared-reading evidence: AAP 2024, Dowdall et al. 2020, Mol et al. 2009, dialogic reading.)

- **New: the shared-reading effect is smaller than often claimed.** A meta-analysis found an effect on language of g = 0.215,
  smaller than earlier meta-analyses, and "negligible in studies with active control groups" (g = 0.021); no moderation by
  child age, SES, or use of dialogic techniques. **[abstract]** Noble C, Sala G, Peter M, et al. The impact of shared book
  reading on children's language skills: a meta-analysis. PsyArXiv preprint 2018 (doi:10.31234/osf.io/cu7bk), published in
  *Educ Res Rev* 2019. This is the "repeat" for the famous reading findings: reading helps a little, and talking in other
  ways may help about as much.
- **Telling vs reading.** Isbell et al. compared telling and reading stories to children aged 3 to 5: both helped; told stories
  were linked with better story comprehension in retelling, read stories with more complex language. **[search]** Isbell R,
  Sobol J, Lindauer L, Lowrance A. *Early Child Educ J* 2004;32:157–63. doi:10.1023/B:ECEJ.0000048967.94189.a3. Still not
  opened (Springer login). Present telling as "a good alternative", not as better.
- **Ask, don't tell** (Walker & Lombrozo 2017; Brockbank 2023, §6) supports dialogic questions after a story.
- **Repetition:** children asking for the same story again is welcome (already in `family-stories`). No new study opened.

## 10. Persian and Iranian research

- **Rewrites simplify language but rarely rethink stories for age.** Fazeli and Nasr Esfahani built criteria from language
  development, developmental psychology and fiction technique, then tested published Shahnameh rewrites. Rewriters "only paid
  attention to simplifying the original language" «فقط به ساده‌کردن زبان متن اصلی توجه کرده‌اند», not to readers' abilities at
  each age; most considered children's mental, emotional and social needs; creativity in technique was limited. **[opened]**
  (abstract page) فاضلی م، نصر اصفهانی ر. «بررسی اصول و معیارهای بازنویسی در ادبیات کودکان و نوجوانان (مطالعهٔ موردی:
  بازنویسی‌های شاهنامهٔ فردوسی)». *مطالعات ادبیات کودک* دانشگاه شیراز ۱۳۹۸؛ ۱۰(۱):۱۴۹–۱۷۲. doi:10.22099/jcls.2018.21849.1410.
  https://jcls.shirazu.ac.ir/article_4840.html
- **Types of adaptation.** Most children's adaptations of the Shahnameh are "closed" adaptations; re-creation, abridgement and
  revision are rarer; verse-to-prose is the commonest form. **[opened]** (abstract page) جلالی م، پورخالقی چترودی م.
  «اقتباس مفهومی و نگارشی از شاهنامه در ادبیات کودک و نوجوان». *مطالعات ادبیات کودک* ۱۳۹۲؛ ۴(۲):۱–۱۸.
  doi:10.22099/jcls.2014.1627. https://jcls.shirazu.ac.ir/article_1627.html
- **Women in rewrites.** A study of one author's teen rewrites found women (Gordafarid, Manizheh, Jarireh, Shahrnaz) given a
  more prominent role, with "their emotional and peace-seeking qualities outweighing their warrior, epic spirit"
  «ویژگی عاطفی و صلح‌جوی آن‌ها بر روحیه جنگاوری و حماسی‌شان غلبه دارد». **[opened]** (abstract page) موسوی ک، نجفی بهزادی س،
  ایزدی‌نژاد ا. «ساختارشکنی روایت‌گری شخصیت زنان در شاهنامه و متون بازنویسی (مطالعه موردی: آثار بازنویسی شده آتوسا صالحی
  برای نوجوان)». *روایت‌شناسی* ۱۴۰۳؛ ۸(۱۶):۶۲۷–۶۶۳. https://ecc.isc.ac/showJournal/28172/294547/3726469
  Note for adapters: softening women into only gentle roles is itself a change; Gordafarid fights **and** outwits.
- **Realistic Shahnameh stories can suit children** if rewritten, linked to children's daily lives, and when child characters
  invite identification. **[search]** «بررسی ظرفیت داستان‌های واقع‌گرای شاهنامه برای ارائه به کودکان», ecc.isc.ac (timed out).
- A conference paper tested Shahnameh storytelling sessions on pupils' self-esteem and depression; results not seen.

## 12. House rules for adapting heritage stories by age

Derived from the evidence above. Each says where it comes from; [I] = inference/expert judgement, not tested on epics.

1. **Under 6: no on-page death of a hero, parent or child, and no killing as the climax.** Death isn't understood as permanent
   until about 5 to 7 (Speece & Brent). Stop the story before it, as `jamshid-and-nowruz` does, or choose another episode.
2. **Under 6: monsters are outwitted, tamed or sent away, never described in gruesome detail.** Appearance drives young
   children's fear and "it's not real" doesn't help them (Cantor). No transformations, no grotesque bodies, no scary voices.
3. **Under 8: always end in safety.** Home, together, the danger gone, said in the last lines. Young children are calmed by
   comfort and closeness, not reasoning (Cantor); bedtime stories need a safe ending most of all [I].
4. **No gore at any age; one line is enough.** "There was a great battle and many were hurt" rather than wounds and blood.
   Visual threat and graphic injury are top fear triggers (Cantor) and print can prime aggression in the short term (Coyne).
5. **6 to 7: a death can be told only briefly, in plain words, with what came after.** "He died. Everyone missed him and they
   remembered him by …". No "fell asleep forever" (Slaughter & Griffiths; app guide `parent-ill-or-bereaved`).
6. **8 to 12: tragic stories (Sohrab, Siavash, Iraj) are "care" stories with a parent there and talk afterwards.** The hardest
   part at this age is injustice and betrayal (Cantor, van der Molen & Bushman). Keep how someone was killed off the page.
7. **Show the cost of fighting, and win by other means at least as often.** Let wit, mercy, keeping promises and helping solve
   problems (Gordafarid, Kaveh, Rostam choosing to help). Lee et al. 2014: positive models teach better than punishment [I for
   epics].
8. **Lead with the good deed rewarded, not the wrongdoer punished.** Revenge and cruel punishments are cut or summarised for
   under-10s (Lee et al. 2014).
9. **End with one "why" question, not a moral.** "Why do you think she did that?" Explaining helps 5- and 6-year-olds find and
   use the moral (Walker & Lombrozo 2017).
10. **Say once, simply, what is legend and what is history.** For 8+, "this is an old story; people told it for a thousand
    years"; news-like "this really happened" framing frightens older children more (van der Molen & Bushman) [I].
11. **No living enemies.** Turan, "the enemy king" or the div-worshippers are never linked to a present-day people, faith,
    country or ethnic group, and no story praises hating a group (governance §4 political neutrality) [I].
12. **Women keep their full roles.** Don't soften female characters into only gentle helpers, and don't leave them out.
    Where a tale's women matter (Gordafarid, Sindokht, Manizheh, Faranak), keep their courage and choices (McCabe 2011;
    Mousavi et al. 1403) [I].
13. **Say in the note what was changed.** Every story keeps a `note` stating what was softened or left out, as the current
    Shahnameh stories do (transparency; Fazeli & Nasr Esfahani: rewrites should be judged against the original).
14. **Check the source text and give the source.** Each plot checked against Ganjoor, the source cited; no invented episodes
    presented as Ferdowsi (existing app rule in `stories-persian.ts`).
15. **Ask about recent losses before death stories, and let a child stop.** A story death can bring a real one back; any
    child can say "not that one" (expert opinion; `parent-ill-or-bereaved`).

## 13. Parent guide outline (works in both apps; names no culture or country)

**Title:** Old tales with battles, monsters and sad endings
(Persian draft: «قصه‌های کهن با جنگ، دیو و پایان غمگین»)

**Summary (≤ 1 line):** How to share old stories by age: what to skip, how to calm fear, what to say when a hero dies, and
questions that help the meaning land.

**Ages:** 24 to 144 months. **Topic:** talking/reading. **Related:** `family-stories`, `reading-every-age`,
`parent-ill-or-bereaved`, `when-scary-things-happen`, `anxious-children`.

**Sections**

1. *Why old stories are worth it* — they connect children with the people before them and give words for courage, loss and
   doing right. Sharing family and group stories is linked with children's wellbeing (a link, not proof) (Fivush; Weststrate
   et al. 2024). If your family speaks another language at home, old stories are an enjoyable way to hear more of it (inference
   from Rodina 2023; no trial).
2. *What scares children changes with age* — under about 7, how things look; older, real dangers and unfairness (Cantor;
   van der Molen & Bushman).
3. *You're the editor* — you can skip, soften or stop. Steps: read it yourself first; cut gore and cruel detail; keep it short;
   end somewhere safe for young children.
4. *When a hero dies* — plain words, what came after, let them ask (Speece & Brent; Slaughter & Griffiths).
5. *Monsters* — for little ones, comfort beats explaining; for older ones, "it's an old story" works (Cantor; Sayfan & Lagattuta).
6. *Ask, don't preach* — one "why" question at the end (Walker & Lombrozo; Lee et al.).
7. *Heroes come in all kinds* — clever, kind, brave women and men; winning without fighting.

**Table by age**

| Age | What usually works | Leave out or soften | If they're scared |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 to 5 | Short tales, clever animals and people, danger outwitted, home at the end | Deaths, blood, gruesome monsters, transformations, children in danger | Hold them close, stay calm, switch to a happy part. "It's only pretend" doesn't help much yet |
| 6 to 7 | Brave and clever heroes, friendly or outwitted monsters, a short battle in one line | Graphic fights, cruel punishments, long sad endings | A cuddle, then a simple "That's an old story. Divs aren't real, and you're safe" |
| 8 to 12 | Sad and unfair endings told with you there; talking about choices and fairness | Gore, detailed killing, hating a whole group | Talk it through: what happened, why, what else could they have done. Answer honestly |

**Science box:** Most fear research is about screens, not told stories, and children were mostly from a few countries. The
moral-of-the-story findings come from small lab studies. **We don't know** whether hearing old battle stories changes how
children behave; there are no studies on that.

**Don't**
- Don't use a scary tale to make a child behave ("the div will take you").
- Don't force a child to hear the end of a story that's upsetting them.
- Don't say a dead hero "went to sleep".
- Don't turn an old story's enemies into today's people or groups.
- Don't tell a death story at bedtime soon after a real loss.
- Don't quiz for the moral; ask what they think.

**Safety callout:** If fears after a story last weeks, spoil sleep most nights or come with tummy aches, see `anxious-children`
and talk to your doctor.

**Sources:** Cantor (UNESCO Clearinghouse article; "Fear and the Media"); van der Molen & Bushman 2008; Sayfan & Lagattuta
2009; Speece & Brent 1984; Slaughter & Griffiths 2007; Woolley & Ghossainy 2013; Walker & Lombrozo 2017; Lee et al. 2014;
Larsen et al. 2018; Fivush 2016 (Psychology Today); Weststrate, McLean & Fivush 2024; Noble et al. 2019.
(Persian-only extra block, if wanted: UNESCO naqqali 2011 and Fazeli & Nasr Esfahani 1398, without naming other countries.)

## 14. Parent tips (≤ 200 characters)

1. "Under about 6, stop a story before a hero or parent dies. Children this age don't yet understand death is forever." —
   Speece & Brent 1984 [abstract]
2. "Little ones scared by a monster in a story? A cuddle and calm voice help more than 'it's only pretend'." — Cantor
   [opened]; Sayfan & Lagattuta 2009 [abstract]
3. "From about 8, sad endings are fine if you're there. Ask: 'Was that fair? What else could he have done?'" — Cantor
   [opened]; van der Molen & Bushman 2008 [abstract]
4. "After a story, ask one 'why' question instead of saying the moral. Explaining helps children find it themselves." —
   Walker & Lombrozo 2017 [abstract]
5. "Stories where honesty is admired worked better than stories where liars are punished, in a study of 3 to 7-year-olds." —
   Lee et al. 2014 [abstract]
6. "You're the editor: skip blood and cruel detail. 'There was a great battle' is enough for any age." — Cantor [opened]
   (expert opinion)
7. "When a hero dies, use plain words: 'He died.' Talking about death simply is linked with less fear of it." — Slaughter &
   Griffiths 2007 [abstract]
8. "Telling an old story from memory is a good alternative to reading it. Watch their face and slow down or skip as you go."
   — house-style tip from Cantor [opened] (adults can tailor what a child hears) and phase 5 note 09; no claim that telling is
   better (Isbell 2004 is search-only).
9. "Pick old tales where women and men win with wit and kindness, not only strength." — inference from McCabe 2011 [search];
   house rule, not a finding. **Use as a house-style tip without a science claim.**
10. "End bedtime stories somewhere safe: home, together, the danger gone." — Cantor [opened] (comfort for young children;
    expert opinion)

Tips 8 and 9 are house-style advice, not findings: ship them without "research shows" wording.

## 15. Claims the app must not make

- That old epics or Shahnameh stories improve self-esteem, mood, identity or behaviour (no trial opened; the Persian
  conference paper is unread).
- That violent tales make children aggressive, or that they are harmless (no evidence on oral or printed epics for children).
- That "knowing family stories is the best predictor of wellbeing" (press phrasing; correlational).
- Any percentage from Valkenburg 2000 or Narvaez 1999 (search-only).
- That telling is better than reading (Isbell unopened; Noble shows small effects overall).
- That girls should get gentler stories (gender differences in fear are average and partly social).
- Any country names from the diaspora studies in the Persian app.
