## 1. Diarrhoea and vomiting ("tummy bugs")

### 1.1 What the bodies say

**WHO** (fact sheet "Diarrhoeal disease", 7 Mar 2024) [opened] — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diarrhoeal-disease
- Diarrhoea = **3 or more loose or liquid stools a day**, or more often than normal for that person. Frequent formed stools, and **loose "pasty" stools in breastfed babies, are not diarrhoea**. (Useful reassurance line.)
- Third leading cause of death in children aged 1–59 months; about 443,800 under-5 deaths a year.
- Treatment: **ORS** (clean water, sugar, salt); **zinc for 10–14 days** shortens diarrhoea; IV fluids only for severe dehydration or shock. **Keep feeding, including breast milk**, during the illness.
- Severe dehydration (at least two of): lethargy or unconsciousness; sunken eyes; unable to drink or drinks poorly; skin pinch goes back very slowly (2 seconds or more).
- Prevention: safe water, sanitation, **handwashing with soap**, food hygiene, exclusive breastfeeding to 6 months, **rotavirus vaccination**.
- The fact sheet does not use the phrase "low-osmolarity". WHO/UNICEF switched to **reduced (low) osmolarity ORS in 2004** [recall, unverified]. Iranian pharmacy sachets are generally the WHO formula [recall, unverified]. Copy only needs: "use ready-made ORS sachets and mix exactly as the pack says".

**WHO 2024 guideline update on zinc** [opened via BIGG-REC mirror] — *Guideline on management of pneumonia and diarrhoea in children up to 10 years of age*, WHO 2024 (ISBN 9789240103412). https://bigg-rec.bvsalud.org/en/recommendations/e9620e500e6f977da8bfbac6a69eba9544933b09 and …/d8e94e493a776ccfafe2dae48cfe4ffa35e3f86b
- "In children up to 10 years of age with acute watery diarrhoea, WHO recommends adjunctive treatment with oral zinc." Strong recommendation, moderate certainty. Same for **persistent** diarrhoea.
- Remarks: zinc **gluconate** causes less vomiting; duration stays 10–14 days; **the 2024 guideline moved to a lower dose than older guidance** because it causes less vomiting with no worse diarrhoea outcome [opened, remark text]; a separate conditional recommendation names the new dose [search]. **App consequence:** older leaflets (and Iran's MANA booklet, below) still show the older amounts. This is one more reason the app must never print a zinc amount: "ask your health centre or pharmacist about zinc and the amount".
- Evidence behind it: systematic review of 38 RCTs commissioned by WHO — zinc shortened acute diarrhoea by about **13 hours** on average (moderate certainty) and raised vomiting (RR 1.46); lower doses caused less vomiting. Ali et al., *J Glob Health* 2024, doi:10.7189/jogh.14.04212 [abstract]. Honest framing: zinc helps a little, mainly in settings where children may be zinc-deficient; ORS and feeding matter most.

**NHS** ("Diarrhoea and vomiting", reviewed 21 Dec 2023, next review due 21 Dec 2026) [opened] — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/diarrhoea-and-vomiting/
- Usual course: **diarrhoea stops within 5–7 days, vomiting within 1–2 days.**
- Home care: plenty of fluids, **small sips** if feeling sick; **keep breastfeeding or bottle feeding**, smaller feeds more often if being sick; formula-fed or weaning babies can have small sips of water between feeds; eat when able.
- Avoid: **fruit juice and fizzy drinks**; **diluting formula** (use usual strength); **anti-diarrhoea medicine under 12**; aspirin under 16.
- Pharmacist can suggest ORS. Loperamide not for under-12s.
- **School/nursery: stay off until no vomiting or diarrhoea for at least 2 days (48 hours).** No swimming pool until 48 hours after symptoms stop.
- Hygiene: wash hands with soap and water often; wash soiled clothes and bedding separately on a hot wash; clean toilet seats, flush handles, taps, surfaces and door handles daily; don't share towels, flannels, cutlery; avoid preparing food for others.
- **Call 111 if:** worried about a baby **under 12 months**; child **stops breastfeeding or bottle feeding**; child under 5 has **signs of dehydration such as fewer wet nappies**; still dehydrated after ORS (5 and over); **can't keep fluids down**; **bloody diarrhoea or bleeding from the bottom**; **diarrhoea more than 7 days or vomiting more than 2 days**.
- **999 / A&E if:** vomiting blood or "coffee grounds"; **yellow-green or green vomit (children)**; may have swallowed something poisonous; stiff neck and pain looking at bright lights; sudden severe headache or **sudden severe tummy ache**; blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue; severe breathing difficulty or rapid shallow breaths; confused or not responding as usual.

**AAP** (HealthyChildren "Diarrhea", updated 25 May 2021) [opened] — https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/abdominal/Pages/Diarrhea.aspx
- Mild diarrhoea without vomiting: most children need **no special drink or diet change**.
- With vomiting: small amounts of ORS, often for 1–2 days; **continue breastfeeding**.
- "**Do not try to prepare your own electrolyte solutions at home.**" Use commercial products.
- Soft drinks, juices, sports drinks, soups and boiled milk have the wrong sugar/salt balance. In *severe* diarrhoea it also lists plain water and tea as not enough on their own.
- **Fasting is not a treatment**; back to a normal balanced diet quickly. **BRAT diet no longer recommended** (too low in nutrients).
- OTC anti-diarrhoeals **not recommended under 2 and can be harmful in older children**; ask the doctor first. Avoid homemade remedies.
- Call the paediatrician: any **signs of dehydration (right away)**; fever over 24–48 hours; **bloody stools**; **vomiting more than 12–24 hours**; **green, blood-tinged or coffee-ground vomit**; **swollen belly**; not eating or drinking; **severe tummy pain**; rash or jaundice.

**NICE CG84** (Diarrhoea and vomiting caused by gastroenteritis in under 5s, 2009) — site blocked, all [recall, unverified]:
- Usual course: diarrhoea 5–7 days, **stops within 2 weeks**; vomiting 1–2 days, **stops within 3 days**.
- Higher dehydration risk: **under 1 year, especially under 6 months**; low birth weight; **more than 5 diarrhoea stools or more than 2 vomits in 24 hours**; not offered or not keeping down fluids; stopped breastfeeding during the illness; signs of malnutrition.
- Discourage fruit juice and fizzy drinks; continue breastfeeding and other milk feeds; ORS supplementation for those at risk; no antidiarrhoeals; school/childcare exclusion 48 hours after last episode.
- I could not check whether NICE has replaced CG84. **Recheck.**

**Where bodies differ (tell the writer):**
| Point | NHS | AAP | NICE (recall) | WHO / Iran MANA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Too long" for diarrhoea | > 7 days | (fever > 24–48 h; no day count for diarrhoea) | stops within 2 weeks; 7+ days is a red flag | 14+ days = "persistent", needs a doctor |
| "Too long" for vomiting | > 2 days | > 12–24 hours | > 3 days | — |
| Anti-diarrhoeal medicine | not under 12 | not under 2; can harm older children | not recommended | not part of home care |
| Zinc | not mentioned | not mentioned | not mentioned | recommended, 10–14 days (WHO 2024 updated the dose) |
- **Cautious path for copy:** "Get advice the same day if vomiting goes on for more than a day, or diarrhoea for more than 5–7 days." AAP's 12–24 hours applies to babies and toddlers in practice; for **under 6 months** say "get advice early, the same day". Never suggest anti-diarrhoea medicines for children.

### 1.2 Iran

- **Rotavirus vaccine IS now in Iran's national schedule.** This corrects the brief's assumption.
  - South Khorasan (Birjand University of Medical Sciences health deputy) news, **13 Azar 1403 (3 Dec 2024)**: integration of rotavirus vaccine into the child immunisation programme began that day "**همزمان با سراسر کشور**" (at the same time across the country). Target: **babies at 2, 4 and 6 months**, at urban and rural health centres. [opened] https://hltb.bums.ac.ir/ (news archive item "شروع ادغام واکسن روتا ویروس به برنامه ایمن سازی کودکان در استان خراسان جنوبی")
  - Mehr News, **25 Esfand 1403 (Mar 2025)**: Dr Seyed Mohsen Zahraei, head of the Ministry of Health's vaccine-preventable diseases office: "در سال جاری، دو واکسن جدید پنوموکوک و روتاویروس به برنامه ملی ایمن‌سازی کودکان کشور اضافه شده‌اند." Article says rotavirus causes about half of watery diarrhoea in under-5s and about 50 child deaths a year in Iran, and vaccination could prevent up to 75% of cases (official claim, not data). [opened] https://www.mehrnews.com/news/6408596/
  - Moosazadeh et al., *Health Sci Rep* 2026: "In 2024, Iran introduced the pentavalent oral vaccine **Rotasiil®** into its national immunization program." [abstract] doi:10.1002/hsr2.72564. Brand not confirmed by an official source.
  - A deputy minister said it is **free at all health centres**, and charging for it is an offence. [search] (Tabnak)
  - Before this, a 2024 Hamadan paper said it had not been introduced [search]; that is now outdated.
  - **Copy:** "Rotavirus vaccine is now given free at Iranian health centres at 2, 4 and 6 months. Ask your health centre if your baby missed it — there are age limits." (Age limits for late doses: [recall, unverified]; don't state them.) Children born before late 2024 will not have had it, so older siblings can still get rotavirus.
- **How common:** rotavirus found in **34.6%** of 301 under-5s with gastroenteritis in southern Iran (2020); rotavirus-positive children more often had severe dehydration (20.9% vs 9.2%). Tariverdi et al., *Iran J Microbiol* 2026 [abstract]. In Tehran Province 2023–24, about half of 203 samples had a virus, mostly **rotavirus and adenovirus**, peaking in mild-to-cold months. Fakhr et al., *Virol J* 2026 [abstract].
- **Iran's IMCI ("مانا" — مراقبت‌های ادغام‌یافته ناخوشی‌های اطفال), non-physician booklet** (posted by Iran University of Medical Sciences, Nov 2022; children 2 months–5 years) [opened, text extracted locally] — https://shahriar.iums.ac.ir/uploads/54/2022/Nov/01/ (file "بوکلت مانا غیرپزشک.pdf"):
  - "**برنامه الف: اسهال را در منزل درمان کنید**" — four home-treatment rules for the mother: (1) **extra fluids**, as much as the child wants; breastfeed more often and for longer; if exclusively breastfed give ORS or clean water *as well*; otherwise ORS, **watery foods such as soup, yoghurt and doogh (دوغ)**, or safe water; "**از مایعات شیرین مثل نوشابه و آب میوه های صنعتی استفاده نشود**" (no sweet drinks such as fizzy drinks and packaged juices); if the child vomits, **wait 10 minutes, then continue more slowly**, sip by sip with a cup or spoon; continue until diarrhoea stops. (2) **Zinc supplement ("مکمل روی") for 10–14 days.** (3) **Keep feeding** (under 6 months, breast milk only). (4) **Know when to come back immediately.**
  - So **zinc is part of Iran's national IMCI home care**. The booklet gives amounts by age — **do not copy them** (WHO revised the dose in 2024).
  - Danger signs the health worker checks: unusual sleepiness or reduced alertness; restless and irritable; sunken eyes; unable to drink or drinks poorly vs drinks eagerly and thirsty; skin pinch returns very slowly (> 2 s); **blood in stool**; **diarrhoea lasting more than 14 days** ("اسهال پایدار"). Return immediately for: not feeding well or weak sucking, getting worse or new fever, fast or difficult breathing, **blood in stool**.
- University hospital pamphlets (Tabriz, Urmia, Tehran, Isfahan, Shiraz 1404) say the same: one ORS sachet in the stated amount of boiled-and-cooled water, keep usual feeding, formula at the usual strength, **no industrial drinks such as "ساندیس", fizzy drinks or packaged juice**, small frequent sips when vomiting; urgent return for bloody diarrhoea, worsening, or a baby with heavy vomiting and dehydration. [search]
- **«او.آر.اس»** is a household word; sachets are sold in pharmacies and given free at health centres in IMCI [search; MANA says "give the mother one ORS packet"].
- **IV drip ("سرم") pressure:** phase 6 notes flagged requests for a drip for a quick recovery. WHO: IV only for severe dehydration or shock [opened]. AAP: ORS at home works for moderate dehydration [opened]. Copy should normalise ORS as the real treatment, without shaming parents who ask.
- **Water and power cuts in summer** (see §4.3) make hand hygiene and safe water harder; worth one line: keep some bottled or boiled water and soap for handwashing.

**Copy direction:**
- Lead with reassurance: most tummy bugs pass in a few days; the main job is **fluids**.
- "Keep breastfeeding — more often, not less." "Keep formula at its usual strength." "Offer food when they want it; no special diet needed."
- ORS: "Use ready-made sachets and mix with exactly the amount of water the pack says. Too strong or too weak can harm." "Give small sips often, with a spoon or cup. If they vomit, wait about 10 minutes and try again, more slowly." (Wait-10-minutes is MANA/WHO practice; no volumes.)
- Avoid fruit juice, fizzy drinks, sweet packaged drinks and sports drinks. Don't give anti-diarrhoea medicines to children.
- Zinc: "In Iran, your health centre may give zinc for 10–14 days. Ask them or your pharmacist about the amount." Never more than that.
- Back to school or nursery **48 hours after the last episode**. Hands with soap after the toilet, nappy changes and before food.
- Rotavirus vaccine line (above).

**Don't say:** any ORS volume or zinc amount; "homemade salt-sugar water" as a recipe (AAP says don't; WHO allows recipes only in specific settings — skip); "starve the tummy" or "rest the gut"; "BRAT diet"; "rice water/herbal teas instead of ORS"; "antibiotics for diarrhoea" (only for specific causes a doctor finds); "teething causes diarrhoea" (NHS: no evidence — phase 6).

**Draft table — tummy bugs**
| Get help now (emergency: 115, or go to hospital) | See a doctor or health centre today / soon | Care at home |
|---|---|---|
| Green (bile) or yellow-green vomit; vomit with blood or like coffee grounds | Baby **under 6 months** with diarrhoea or vomiting (lower threshold: same day) | Small, frequent sips; ORS from sachets mixed exactly as the pack says |
| Very sleepy, floppy, hard to wake, confused | Signs of dehydration: fewer wet nappies, dark pee, dry mouth, no tears, sunken eyes | Keep breastfeeding (more often); formula at usual strength |
| Pale, blue, grey or blotchy skin or lips; fast or struggling breathing | Can't keep any fluids down; stops feeding | Food when they want it; no special diet |
| Severe, constant tummy pain or a swollen, hard belly | **Blood in the stool** | No juice, fizzy drinks or sweet packaged drinks |
| Stiff neck, pain with light, or a rash that doesn't fade under a glass | Vomiting more than a day (sooner for babies); diarrhoea more than 5–7 days | Handwashing; separate towels; hot wash for soiled things |
| Might have swallowed medicine or a chemical | Fever over 2 days, or you are worried | Back to nursery/school 48 h after the last episode |

## 2. Coughs, colds, sore throats and earache

### 2.1 How long they normally last

- **UKHSA/RCGP TARGET leaflet "Respiratory tract infections" v4.0, Nov 2024** (cites NICE NG91, NG84, NG120 and Thompson 2013) [opened, text extracted]:
  - **Earache: most better by 3 days (can last 7–8 days)**
  - **Sore throat: most better by 7–8 days**
  - **Cold: most better by 14 days**
  - **Cough: most better by 3–4 weeks**
  - "If you are not starting to improve a little by the times given above, visit a pharmacist or contact your GP practice." Urgent signs listed (adult-oriented): severe headache, very cold skin, trouble breathing, confusion, chest pain, problems swallowing, coughing blood, feeling a lot worse.
- **Thompson et al., *BMJ* 2013** (systematic review, children): symptoms gone in **90% of children** by: earache **7–8 days**, sore throat **2–7 days**, croup **2 days**, bronchiolitis **21 days**, acute cough **25 days**, **common cold 15 days**. Authors note earache and colds last longer than parents are usually told. doi:10.1136/bmj.f7027 [abstract]
- NHS pages agree: ear infections "most clear up within 3 days, though symptoms can last up to a week" (reviewed 16 Jan 2025) [opened]; sore throat "normally better within a week" (reviewed 8 Apr 2024) [opened]; cough "within 3 to 4 weeks", see a GP if over 3 weeks (reviewed 8 Dec 2023) [opened]. Croup usually improves within **48 hours** (reviewed 2 Aug 2023) [opened].
- **The brief's "cough up to 3 weeks" is the older figure** (TARGET 2018/2020 said 21 days [search]). Current UK figure is **3–4 weeks**; copy can say "a cough can hang on for 3 to 4 weeks after a cold, and that's usually normal".

### 2.2 Antibiotics: why they mostly don't help, and why it matters

- NHS sore throat: antibiotics "usually aren't needed", rarely relieve symptoms or speed recovery [opened]. TARGET: "Most common infections get better without antibiotics." [opened]
- **Ear infections, Cochrane 2023** (Venekamp et al., doi:10.1002/14651858.CD000219.pub5) [abstract]: antibiotics **probably have no effect on pain at 24 hours**, only a slight effect in the following days; **for every 14 children treated, one gets vomiting, diarrhoea or rash** they would not otherwise have had; most cases settle on their own; for most children with mild illness in high-income countries, **watchful waiting with good pain relief** is justified. Caution: the evidence is from high-income countries.
- NHS ear page does not say "antibiotics not needed"; it says a pharmacist or GP may give antibiotics depending on cause [opened]. So copy should say "often", not "never".
- Resistance: one line is enough — "Using antibiotics when they can't help makes germs harder to treat later, for your child and everyone." (General WHO/NHS message [recall, unverified wording].)

**Iran data:**
- **Arak, 2019–20, 1,483 households: 61.6% of children under 6 had antibiotics without a prescription in a year** (mother's report cross-checked against insurance records). More likely with older children, more children in the family, younger mothers and fathers in temporary work. Nazari et al., *PLoS One* 2022, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0278843 [abstract]. One city; method is unusual; treat as "a large share", not a national figure.
- **GP prescribing:** 1.43 million prescriptions from 73 GPs at one central public clinic (about 7% of national GP prescriptions): antibiotic prescriptions rose **87% during COVID and stayed 94% above baseline afterwards**; antibiotic share of all drugs doubled (7.6% → 14.4%); azithromycin up 120%. Alijaniha et al., *BMC Infect Dis* 2025, doi:10.1186/s12879-025-12108-6 [abstract]. All ages.
- Iran's **MANA booklet** [opened]: harmless home treatments for cough and cold — **breastfeed more often; more fluids; salt-water (sodium chloride) nose drops, carefully; clean the nose; lemon juice with honey "over 2 years"; avoid cigarette and waterpipe (قلیان) smoke**. **Not recommended:** cough or anti-allergy syrups **under 2**, and **any herbal or traditional remedy by mouth or as steam inhalation ("بخور")**. For ear discharge, dry the ear with a clean twisted cloth wick and "**به هیچ عنوان چیز دیگری نظیر روغن… داخل گوش نریزید**" (never put oil or anything else in the ear).
- **Disagreement — honey age:** NHS/AAP and the app say honey is fine **from age 1** (botulism risk under 1). MANA's lemon-and-honey line says **over 2**. The app's existing "honey over 1" is correct by WHO/NHS/AAP standards; for Persian copy, consider "never under 1 year; your health centre may advise waiting until 2" [cautious; reviewer to decide].
- **Honey evidence:** low to very low-quality evidence that honey may ease cough and improve sleep more than placebo or OTC cough medicine. Kuitunen & Renko, *Eur J Pediatr* 2023 (10 RCTs), doi:10.1007/s00431-023-05066-1 [abstract]. NHS: "evidence is limited"; not for under 1; no hot drinks for small children [opened].

### 2.3 Croup

- NHS [opened]: **barking, seal-like cough**, hoarse voice, **high-pitched rasping sound breathing in (stridor)**, worse at night, after cold-like start. Usually better within 48 hours. Home care once a doctor or 111 has confirmed it: sit upright, keep calm (crying worsens it), fluids, check regularly overnight. **"Don't use steam."** Contact a doctor if you think it's croup — **a doctor may give medicine that shortens it**. Babies **under 3 months** may be sent to hospital.
- NHS 999: struggling to breathe (grunting, **sucking in under the ribs or at the tummy**, unusual noises); blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue (on brown or black skin check palms and soles); unusually sleepy, hard to wake, limp or floppy; very distressed and can't be calmed; **drooling, trouble swallowing, prefers to sit rather than lie**.
- AAP (HealthyChildren "Croup in Young Children", updated 10 May 2024) [opened]: stridor while crying is common in mild croup; **"stridor when resting" can signal severe croup** — seek urgent help, as also for whistling getting louder, unable to speak for lack of breath, blue lips, drooling. **Steam not proven; no studies show moist cool night air helps.**
- **Iran:** steam inhalation (بخور), often with herbs, is a common home practice [recall, unverified prevalence]; MANA says no herbal steam [opened]; NHS says no steam [opened]. Burns from hot-water bowls are a known risk [recall, unverified]. Copy: "Steam doesn't help croup and can scald. Keep your child upright and calm and call the doctor."

### 2.4 Earache and sore throat details

- NHS earache [opened]: babies may pull or rub the ear, be irritable, off food, lose balance. **No cotton buds or fingers in the ear**; no decongestants or antihistamines (no evidence). See a GP if **under 12 months**, earache lasts beyond 3 days, or repeated infections. Urgent (111/urgent GP): generally unwell, very high temperature, **swelling around or behind the ear**, **fluid coming from the ear**, new hearing loss, dizziness, severe sore throat; baby under 12 months with pain in both ears.
- NHS sore throat [opened]: warm salt-water gargle **not for children**; soft or cool foods, fluids; no hard sweets or ice for young children (choking). GP: **child under 5** with sore throat; no better after a week. 999: **trouble breathing, can't swallow, drooling, stridor**.
- Iranian folk remedy of putting oil (or breast milk) in a sore ear [recall, unverified]; MANA says never [opened].

### 2.5 General warning signs (fit with the existing fever guide and trouble-breathing card)
- Fast breathing or working hard to breathe (ribs or tummy sucking in, nostrils flaring, grunting); MANA counts "fast breathing" as **60 or more a minute under 2 months** [opened]; WHO IMCI uses **50+ at 2–12 months and 40+ at 1–5 years** [recall, unverified]. App should describe what working-hard breathing looks like, not ask parents to count, except perhaps in a later "how to count breaths" explainer.
- Drowsy, floppy, hard to wake; dehydration; ear discharge or swelling behind the ear; **stiff neck**; **rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass on it** [recall, unverified wording; standard NHS meningitis advice]; drooling or can't swallow; blue or grey lips.

**Copy direction:**
- Normalise duration with the TARGET numbers ("ear 3 days, up to a week; sore throat about a week; cold up to 2 weeks; cough 3–4 weeks").
- Explain antibiotics kindly: "Most of these are caused by viruses. Antibiotics don't work on viruses, can cause tummy upsets and rashes, and help germs become resistant." Never blame parents who have used them.
- What helps: fluids, rest, saline drops and gentle nose clearing for babies, honey (over 1) for cough, soft cool foods for sore throats, pain relief as the medicines guide says (link; no doses), smoke-free air (link to bad-air guide; waterpipe named in Persian copy).
- Croup: calm and upright, no steam; doctor today; emergency for stridor at rest or struggling.

**Don't say:** "a cough after 2 weeks means an infection needs antibiotics"; "green snot means bacteria" [common myth, recall]; any cough-medicine age other than linking to the existing medicines guide; "steam/بخور for croup"; "oil in the ear"; any breathing-rate number without a source check.

**Draft table — coughs, colds, throats, ears**
| Get help now (115 / hospital) | See a doctor soon (today or next day) | Care at home |
|---|---|---|
| Struggling to breathe: ribs or tummy sucking in, grunting, can't speak | Baby under 3 months with any illness (link fever card); under 12 months with earache | Fluids, rest, more breastfeeds |
| Noisy breathing in (stridor) **while calm and resting** | Barking cough you think is croup (medicine can help) | Saline drops, gentle nose clearing |
| Blue, grey or very pale lips or skin | Earache over 3 days; fluid from the ear; swelling behind the ear | Honey for cough — only over age 1 |
| Drooling, can't swallow, wants to sit forward | Sore throat in a child under 5, or no better after a week | Soft, cool foods for sore throats |
| Very drowsy, floppy, hard to wake | Cough over 3–4 weeks, or getting worse not better | Smoke-free home (no cigarettes, no قلیان) |
| Stiff neck or a rash that doesn't fade under a glass | Signs of dehydration; fever over the fever-guide limits | Nothing in the ear; no herbal steam |

## 3. Eczema, dry skin and nappy rash

### 3.1 Eczema care

- **NHS "Atopic eczema"** (reviewed 6 Sep 2024) [opened] — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/atopic-eczema/
  - In babies often on the **face**; comes and goes in flares; often improves with age; **not contagious**.
  - Emollients **at least twice a day, and keep going when skin looks better**; **wash with an emollient instead of soap**; use a spoon or pump, don't dip fingers in the pot; **don't use aqueous cream** (irritates); don't share. **Emollients soak into fabric and make clothes and bedding catch fire more easily — keep away from flames** (relevant to heaters and stoves).
  - Treatments: emollients plus **topical steroids prescribed by a GP**, or referral to a dermatologist; follow the prescriber.
  - Triggers: soap, detergents, some fabrics, pets, dust mites, pollen, **heat**, infections, stress; certain foods for some children — but **"don't change the diet unless a doctor advises it, including when breastfeeding"**.
  - Itch: rub gently with a finger instead of scratching; short nails; **scratch mittens for babies**; keep cool.
  - **Urgent GP or 111 if** eczema is **blistered, crusty, leaking fluid or has pus spots; painful, swollen or warm; suddenly worse or spreading; with a high temperature or feeling unwell** — "These can indicate infection or **eczema herpeticum**."
- **AAP** (HealthyChildren "Eczema", updated 19 May 2025) [opened]: skin-barrier problem (filaggrin), runs in families; babies — scalp and face; school-age — elbow and knee creases, neck, around eyes. "Many children with eczema also have food allergies, but **foods themselves do not cause eczema**." Infection signs: **yellow or honey-coloured crusts**, weeping, blisters or pus bumps, or a rash not improving with usual treatment.
- **Eczema herpeticum** (DermNet, updated May 2023) [opened] — https://dermnetnz.org/topics/eczema-herpeticum: herpes simplex (cold-sore virus) spreading over eczema; **clusters of blisters or "punched-out" sores**, often blood-stained or crusted, often face and neck, with **fever**; "**one of the few dermatological emergencies**"; can spread to eyes, brain, lungs, liver; prompt antiviral treatment. Appears 5–12 days after contact with someone who may or may not have a visible cold sore.
  - NICE CG57: if suspected, start antiviral immediately and refer same day; eye involvement → same-day ophthalmology [recall, unverified].
  - **Practical line:** "Anyone with a cold sore should not kiss a child with eczema" [recall, unverified, but standard advice] — fits Iranian family culture of kissing babies; phrase warmly.
- Bathing: short lukewarm baths, soap-free wash, pat dry, apply emollient straight after [recall, unverified — AAD/NICE CG57 standard; AAD page 404].
- **Topical steroids:** parents often fear them [recall, unverified]. App must not give strength or duration, but can say "steroid creams prescribed for flares are safe when used as your doctor says; using too little, too briefly, is a common reason eczema doesn't settle" [recall, unverified — check NICE CG57/NHS steroids page before using].

### 3.2 Does moisturising from birth prevent eczema? (say it plainly, but accurately)

- **BEEP trial** (UK, 1,394 high-risk newborns; Chalmers et al., *Lancet* 2020, doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(19)32984-8) [abstract]: daily emollient in year 1 — eczema at 2 years **23% vs 25%, no meaningful difference**; **more skin infections** in the emollient group (0.23 vs 0.15 per child; IRR 1.55). Authors: families with allergies "**should not use daily emollients to try and prevent eczema in their newborn**."
- **PreventADALL** (Norway/Sweden, 2,397 general-population infants; Skjerven et al., *Lancet* 2020, doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(19)32983-6) [abstract]: emollient baths and face cream from 2 weeks **did not reduce eczema** at 12 months (11% vs 8% control; no safety concerns found).
- **Cochrane 2022** (Kelleher et al., 33 RCTs, IPD meta-analysis; doi:10.1002/14651858.CD013534.pub3) [abstract]: skin-care interventions in healthy infants "**probably not effective for preventing eczema**; **may increase risk of food allergy**; and **probably increase risk of skin infection**" (moderate certainty for eczema and infection).
- **Newer, conflicting trials — the writer must know these exist:**
  - **CASCADE** (US, 1,247 infants not selected for risk; Simpson et al., *JAMA Dermatol* 2025, doi:10.1001/jamadermatol.2025.2357) [abstract]: daily full-body moisturiser from before 9 weeks — eczema by 24 months **36.1% vs 43.0% (RR 0.84)**; no difference in skin adverse events.
  - **STOP-AD 5-year** (Ireland, high-risk, twice-daily emollient for first 8 weeks; Cremin et al., *Pediatr Allergy Immunol* 2026, doi:10.1111/pai.70517) [abstract]: 12.3% vs 24.0% at 5 years, borderline (p = .048), only 177 children followed.
- **Honest summary for copy:** "Big trials found that daily moisturiser from birth does **not** reliably prevent eczema, and it may slightly raise the chance of skin infections. A newer trial found a small benefit. You don't need to do it. If your baby's skin is dry, moisturise the dry patches." This is the cautious path: the largest pooled evidence (Cochrane) says no benefit and some harm; one recent trial disagrees.
- **Food allergy and eczema:** link to the food guide only. Key safe messages: foods don't cause eczema (AAP); don't cut foods without a doctor (NHS); babies with **severe eczema** may benefit from **early, not delayed**, introduction of allergens with medical advice [recall, unverified — check food guide wording].

### 3.3 Nappy rash

- **NHS** ("Nappy rash", reviewed 28 Aug 2026) [opened] — https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/nappy-rash/
  - Do: change wet or dirty nappies **promptly**; clean and **pat** dry; **nappy-free time** when possible; absorbent, well-fitting nappies; water or **fragrance-free, alcohol-free wipes**; bathe daily but not more than twice a day.
  - Don't: soap, baby lotion, bubble bath; **talcum powder**; **antiseptic creams**; tight nappies; plastic pants.
  - Pharmacist may suggest a **thin layer of barrier cream** at each change.
  - See a health visitor or GP if it doesn't clear, **gets worse or spreads**, baby has a **high temperature**, or seems very uncomfortable. GP may prescribe mild steroid, antifungal (thrush) or antibiotics.
- **AAP** (HealthyChildren "Diaper Rash", updated 18 Dec 2024, Section on Dermatology) [opened]: most common cause is irritation from urine and stool, more likely with diarrhoea; **thrush (yeast)** often after antibiotics — shiny, sharp-edged, worse in the folds, red dots at edges; use a **thick zinc oxide or petrolatum paste**, add more on top rather than scrubbing it off; squirt-bottle rinse; air-dry; avoid OTC antibiotic ointments and fragranced wipes. **Call the doctor** if not better after **2–3 days** of care; **pimples, blisters, pus, oozing or crusty sores**; bright red rash with spots at the edges while on antibiotics; very painful; **fever with the rash**.
- Air time: one small Turkish RCT (130 infants 0–3 months) — 12 short nappy-free periods a day healed mild nappy rash faster than 6 (about 2 vs 3 days). Bilgiç et al., *BMC Pediatr* 2026 [abstract]; retrospectively registered — supportive only.
- **Iranian traditional practices (only sourced ones):**
  - **Henna (حنا)**: an Iranian triple-blind trial (82 children, Qazvin) found a 25% henna-oil product worked as well as or better than 1% hydrocortisone for nappy rash. Keshavarz et al., *Iran Red Crescent Med J* 2016 [abstract]. **BUT henna can trigger life-threatening haemolysis in G6PD-deficient babies** — e.g. "Henna causes life threatening haemolysis in G6PD deficiency", *Arch Dis Child* 2001 (PMID 11668106) and a 2023 neonatal case report (*Cureus*, PMID 36879691) [titles seen via Europe PMC; abstracts not read]. G6PD deficiency is common in Iran (app has a G6PD guide). **Copy: do not put henna on a baby's skin, especially if G6PD status is unknown or deficient.** Strong safety message; link G6PD guide.
  - **Shampoo clay (گل سرشور)** healed nappy rash faster than calendula in a small Iranian trial (60 infants, Khomein). Adib-Hajbaghery et al., *Nurs Midwifery Stud* 2014 [abstract]. Bentonite/calendula and breast-milk trials also exist [titles only]. Small, single-centre, unblinded or weak trials; clay products are unregulated and may be contaminated [recall, unverified]. **Copy:** don't recommend; if asked, "a few small studies, not enough to recommend; plain barrier cream works".
  - MANA booklet forbids herbal remedies only for cough/cold [opened]; no national skin guidance found.

**Copy direction:**
- Eczema: "Moisturiser is the everyday treatment — lots of it, at least twice a day, even on good days. Wash with a soap substitute." "Flare creams come from a doctor; use them as they say." "Eczema isn't caused by bad care and isn't contagious."
- Emphasise the **two urgent pictures**: infected eczema (crusts, pus, weeping, hot, sudden spread, fever) and **eczema herpeticum** (clusters of small blisters or punched-out sores, unwell, fever) → **same day**, and emergency if the child is very unwell or the eyes are involved.
- Nappy rash: frequent changes, air time, thick barrier cream, no talc, no antiseptic, no soap; see someone if no better in 2–3 days, spots, blisters, pus, spreading, or fever.

**Don't say:** "moisturise from birth to prevent eczema"; "eczema is a food allergy" or "cut out milk/eggs"; "steroid creams thin the skin so avoid them" (or any steroid strength/duration); "talc keeps the bottom dry"; "henna/clay heals nappy rash".

## 4. Heat waves and sun

### 4.1 Heat

- **WHO "Climate change, heat and health"** fact sheet (31 Jul 2026) [opened] — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health
  - **Never leave children or animals in parked vehicles, even briefly.**
  - Avoid direct sun at peak hours; shade or indoors (shade can cut felt heat by **more than 10 °C**).
  - **"Never cover an infant stroller or pram with dry fabric"**; WHO suggests a thin **wet** cloth, re-wetted, with a portable fan. (NHS: don't cover with a blanket [opened]. Copy: "never cover the pram with a blanket or dry cloth".)
  - Light, loose clothing; give children water regularly; cool showers or baths, damp cloths; **2–3 hours a day in a cool place**; follow home-cooling guidance.
- **NHS "Heatwave"** (reviewed 12 Jun 2026) [opened]: under-5s among those most at risk; close windows, curtains and blinds during the day, open at night when cooler; **"Electric fans can help if the temperature is below 35 degrees"**; cold drinks and food; check rooms where higher-risk people sleep.
- **NHS "Heat exhaustion and heatstroke"** (reviewed 28 May 2026) [opened]:
  - **Heat exhaustion:** tiredness, dizziness, headache, nausea or vomiting, **heavy sweating with pale clammy skin**, cramps, high temperature, strong thirst, irritability; usually same in children.
  - Do: cool place, remove extra clothes, water or ORS, cool skin with water and fan, wrapped cold packs at armpits or neck. **Should feel better within 30 minutes.**
  - **Heatstroke = emergency:** very high temperature; **hot skin that isn't sweating**; fast heartbeat, fast breathing; confusion or clumsiness; **seizure**; **unconscious**. Call emergency if not better after 30 minutes of cooling, or any of these signs. Cool with wet sheet, fanning, cold sponging while waiting.
  - (App's heatstroke/hot-car first-aid card covers the action steps; link to it.)
- **CDC** "Heat and Infants and Children" (25 Jun 2024) [opened]: young children depend on adults to keep them cool and hydrated; car temperature can rise almost **20 °F (about 11 °C) in 10 minutes**; cracking a window isn't enough; leave a reminder (e.g. toy on front seat) and check the back seat.
- **NHS baby hydration in heat** ("Safety in the sun", reviewed 27 Sep 2024) [opened]: **fully breastfed babies under 6 months don't need water** — they may want more breastfeeds; bottle-fed babies may have a little cooled boiled water between feeds; from 6 months sips of water with meals; never in a parked car; supervise paddling pools; cool bath before bed; fan not pointed at the child; minimal bedclothes. (Iranian families often give water to young babies in heat [recall, unverified] — gentle copy needed.)
- **Iran MoH-attributed advice** (various news sites, mostly undated) [search]: under-5s most vulnerable; loose cotton clothes; stay in during peak heat; lukewarm water and cool compresses (neck, groin, armpits) if overheated; regular water; don't leave children in a parked car; Ministry's community nutrition office: protect children from direct heat and dry wind.

### 4.2 Sun

| Point | NHS (reviewed 27 Sep 2024) [opened] | AAP (updated 9 Aug 2024) [opened] | WHO (2026) [opened] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 6 months | **Out of direct sunlight.** Sunscreen only from 6 months. | Shade and clothing; **small amounts of sunscreen on small areas (e.g. face) only if shade and clothing aren't available**. | — |

| Reapply | Every 2 hours and after swimming or sweating | Every 2 hours, after swimming, sweating, towelling; apply 15–30 min before going out | — |

### 4.3 Iran summers

- **Heat-wave mortality, Iran meta-analysis:** heat waves raised all-cause mortality (RR 1.23) and cardiovascular mortality; elderly and men most affected; no child-specific estimate. Hadei et al., *Sci Rep* 2024, doi:10.1038/s41598-024-74821-7 [abstract]. No Iranian child-specific heat-illness data found.
- **2025 heat waves:** Khuzestan reached about **52 °C**; rolling **power cuts** nationwide; **government offices closed or shortened hours in 14+ provinces** (July) and at least half of the 31 provinces (early August) to save electricity; water supplies also hit. [search — AFP/AP/IRNA via Gulf News, Al-Monitor, IranWire, Jul–Aug 2025]
- **Sistan and Baluchestan, Khuzestan, Bushehr, Hormozgan**: also frequent **dust storms** (link bad-air guide) [recall, unverified for current-year details].
- **Power cuts in heat — practical lines** (mostly [recall, unverified], framed as practical tips not medical claims):
  - Know the outage schedule; plan the hottest hours in the coolest room.
  - Fans don't cool well above about 35 °C (NHS [opened]); in an outage they're off anyway: use **damp cloths, lukewarm-to-cool showers, wet light clothing** (WHO [opened]).
  - Keep drinking water and **ORS sachets** at home.
  - Medicines that need the fridge (e.g. some antibiotics syrups, insulin): ask the pharmacist what to do in an outage.
  - **Never run a generator or charcoal/gas heater indoors** — link the CO guidance in round 4 note 02 [opened there].
  - Food spoils fast: when in doubt, throw it out (tummy-bug prevention).
- **Copy direction:**
  - Name the danger plainly but calmly: "Babies and young children heat up faster and can't tell you they're thirsty."
  - Signs to watch: unusually sleepy or cranky, hot red or very sweaty skin, fewer wet nappies, dark pee, vomiting, refusing feeds.
  - What to do: cool room, extra breastfeeds or water (by age), lukewarm bath or damp cloths, light cotton clothes, no outdoor play in the hottest hours, shade and hat.
  - Heat exhaustion vs heatstroke: "If your child seems confused, very drowsy, has a fit or hot dry skin, or isn't better after 30 minutes of cooling — this is an emergency (115)."
  - Hot car: one line plus link — "Never leave a child in a parked car, not even for a minute."
- **Don't say:** "ice baths" or rubbing alcohol for cooling [AAP hot-car advice says no ice bath — phase 5 note 04]; "give babies under 6 months water" to exclusively breastfed babies; "fan pointed at baby's face"; "cover the pram with a muslin to keep the sun off" (dry cloth); sunscreen as the main protection for under-6-months; any claim about Iran's official heat warning system (not checked).

| Confused, very drowsy, floppy, or unconscious | Sunburn with blisters, or over a large area, or with fever or feeling unwell [recall, unverified] | Coolest room; curtains closed by day; open at night if cooler outside |

### B. Daily tips (12–16, ≤ 30 words each)

1. Breastfed babies often have loose, yellow poos. Diarrhoea means poos that are much looser or more frequent than usual for your baby. (WHO diarrhoea fact sheet, 2024)
2. With a tummy bug, keep breastfeeding — more often, not less. Breast milk helps your baby stay hydrated. (NHS; WHO; Iran MANA)
3. Mix ORS sachets with exactly the amount of water on the pack. Too strong or too weak can make things worse. (AAP HealthyChildren; NHS)
4. If your child vomits, wait about ten minutes, then offer small sips again, slowly, with a spoon or cup. (Iran MANA booklet)
5. Fruit juice and fizzy drinks can make diarrhoea worse. Stick to breast milk, usual milk, water and ORS. (NHS; MANA)
6. After a tummy bug, keep your child home until 48 hours after the last vomit or runny poo. (NHS)
7. Rotavirus vaccine is now part of Iran's free schedule at 2, 4 and 6 months. Ask your health centre if your baby has had it. (Iran MoH, Dec 2024)
8. Most colds last up to two weeks and coughs can linger three to four weeks. That's usually normal, not a sign antibiotics are needed. (UKHSA TARGET leaflet 2024)
9. Antibiotics don't work on cold viruses. They can upset tummies and cause rashes, and overuse makes germs harder to treat. (NHS; Cochrane 2023)
10. A spoon of honey can soothe a cough — but never give honey to a baby under one year old. (NHS; Kuitunen 2023)
11. Steam doesn't help croup and can cause burns. Keep your child upright and calm, and call the doctor. (NHS croup; AAP)
12. Don't put oil, cotton buds or anything else into a sore ear. Ask a doctor if there's pain or discharge. (NHS; Iran MANA)
13. For eczema, moisturiser is the everyday treatment: use plenty, at least twice a day, even when skin looks better. (NHS atopic eczema)
14. Anyone with a cold sore shouldn't kiss a baby or child with eczema; the virus can cause a serious skin infection. (DermNet; recheck NICE wording)
15. Nappy rash: change often, let the skin air, use a thick barrier cream. Skip talc, perfumed wipes and henna. (NHS; AAP; henna-G6PD reports)
16. On very hot days, never cover a pram with a blanket or dry cloth — it traps heat. Use a parasol and check often. (WHO 2026; NHS)

- That antibiotics are never needed.
- That daily emollient from birth prevents eczema, or that it's harmful in every case.
- That foods cause eczema.
