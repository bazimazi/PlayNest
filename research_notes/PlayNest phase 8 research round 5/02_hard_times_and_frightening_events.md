## 1. What international guidance says about talking with children

### 1.1 WHO / War Trauma Foundation / World Vision, *Psychological First Aid: Guide for field workers* (2011)

[opened] Full English PDF (mirror of WHO ISBN 9789241548205). A **Persian translation exists** (ISBN 9789645191465, listed on the WHO publication page [opened]). That could be a good source of standard Persian wording.

- "In general, **children cope better when they have a stable, calm adult around them**." How children react "depends on their age and developmental stage. It also depends on the ways their caregivers and other adults interact with them."
- Typical reactions:
  - **Young children:** "may return to earlier behaviours (for example, bedwetting or thumb-sucking), they may cling to caregivers, and reduce their play or use repetitive play related to the distressing event."
  - **School-age children:** "may believe they caused bad things to happen, develop new fears, may be less affectionate, feel alone and be preoccupied with protecting or rescuing people."
  - **Adolescents:** "may feel 'nothing', feel different from or isolated from their friends, or they may display risk-taking behaviour."
- **"Things caregivers can do to help children"** (box, pp. 32–33):
  - **Infants:** keep them warm and safe, "away from loud noises and chaos", cuddles and hugs, a regular feeding and sleeping schedule if possible, a "calm and soft voice".
  - **Young children:** extra time and attention. "Remind them often that they are safe." "Explain to them that they are not to blame." Don't separate them from caregivers or siblings. Keep routines. "Give simple answers about what happened **without scary details**." Let them stay close if clingy. Be patient with regression. Give a chance to play and relax.
  - **Older children and adolescents:** time and attention, routines, "Provide facts about what happened and explain what is going on now." "Allow them to be sad. **Don't expect them to be tough**." Listen without judging. Set clear rules. Talk about dangers and how to stay safe. "Encourage and allow opportunities **for them to be helpful**."
- **Keep safe:** "Protect them from being exposed to any gruesome scenes", "from hearing upsetting stories about the event", and "from the media or from people who want to interview them."
- "Older children and adolescents can often help in crisis situations. Finding safe ways for them to contribute ... may help them to feel more in control."
- **Not debriefing:** PFA "is not about pressuring people to tell you their feelings and reactions." Psychological debriefing "is not recommended". WHO's mhGAP group (2009) concluded PFA, rather than debriefing, should be offered.

### 1.2 Save the Children Denmark, *Psychological First Aid Training Manual for Child Practitioners* (2013)

[opened] Full PDF from the Save the Children Resource Centre. The age lists below are from slides 20–37. Hand-out 5, "Advice for parents", is adapted from the NCTSN/NCPTSD *PFA Field Operations Guide* (2006).

- **All ages:** most children fear the event will happen again and that loved ones will be hurt or separated from them. All may have sleep changes and crying. "Children do not always ask the questions they are thinking about. Perhaps they are **protecting their care-givers** from distress."
- **0–3 years:** cling more, regress, sleep and eating changes, cry more or more irritable, new fears, hyperactivity and poor concentration, play changes (less play, repetitive or aggressive play), more demanding, "very sensitive to how others react".
- **4–6 years:** "magical thinking": "The child may think that he or she is the cause of events." Children "fill in the blanks" with imagination. "This has nothing to do with lying." Reactions: clinging, regression (thumb-sucking), stop talking, inactive or hyperactive, stop playing or repetitive games, worry, nightmares, eating changes, confusion, poor concentration, "at times take on adult roles", irritability.
- **7–12 years:** understand cause and effect and death, "preoccupied with justice". Reactions: activity changes, confusion, withdrawal, repetitive talk about the event, reluctance to go to school, fear, memory and concentration problems, sleep and appetite changes, aggression, **somatic complaints**, concern for other affected people, **self-blame and guilt**.
- **Advice for parents (hand-out 5)**, useful as an "If your child... / Understand that... / Ways to help" table:
  - **Sleep problems:** "let your child sleep beside you. Let him or her know that this is just for now." Keep a bedtime routine ("a story, a prayer, cuddles"). "Understand that the child is not being difficult on purpose."
  - **Fears something will happen to you:** "Remind your child and yourself that right now you are safe. If you are not safe, talk about what you are doing to keep the child safe." Make sure someone else could care for the child.
  - **Cries a lot:** let them be sad and comfort them. "If you yourself are overwhelmed by sadness, seek support. **Your child's well-being is connected to your own well-being.**"
  - **Preschooler fears it will return:** "Explain the difference between the event and memory of the event." Keep young children away from TV, radio and computers coverage.
  - **Not talking:** "**You can't force children to talk**, but you can let them know they can talk to you any time." Use toys, drawings and feeling faces.
  - **School-age child feels responsible:** "tell them why it was not their fault."
  - **Retelling or playing out the event again and again:** "This is a normal reaction to a crisis." Encourage "positive problem-solving through play and drawing", e.g. "draw how you would like your school to be rebuilt to make it safer."
  - **Confused:** correct misunderstandings and "avoid details that would scare your child."

### 1.3 UNICEF parenting page, "How to talk to your children about conflict and war"

[opened] (no date shown on the page). Eight steps:
1. **Find out what they know and feel.** Pick a calm time, not bedtime. Ask what they have heard and correct wrong information.
2. **Keep it calm and age-appropriate.** "Children take emotional cues from adults", so don't share your fears in detail. It is fine not to know every answer.
3. **Spread compassion, not stigma.** Avoid labels like "bad people" or "evil". "Bullying and discrimination is always wrong."
4. **Focus on the helpers.** Small positive action: "The sense of doing something, no matter how small, can often bring great comfort."
5. **Close conversations with care.** Check body language, tone and breathing before you end.
6. **Keep checking in.** Watch for stomach aches, nightmares and sleep problems. "Most are temporary." If they persist, the child may need specialist support. Slow belly breathing helps.
7. **Limit the flood of news.** Turn news off around younger children. Watch what adults say nearby. Do games and walks instead.
8. **Take care of yourself.** Set specific times to check the news instead of staying online all day.

### 1.4 UNICEF Iran (Persian), «چگونه در زمان بحران‌ها و درگیری‌ها از فرزند خود حمایت کنیم؟» (14 Aug 2025)

[opened] Interview with a UNICEF child-psychology specialist (Dr Marcia Brophy). **Wording is neutral**: «بحران»، «درگیری»، «راه‌حل صلح‌آمیز». No party is named. (Other items linked from the same page do refer to specific events; don't copy those.)
- Fear is normal. Telling your child that you are scared too can help them feel you are facing it together. Speak in «لحنی اطمینان‌بخش» (a reassuring tone).
- **Deep belly breathing** («تنفس عمیق شکمی»). For young children, make it a game. If you feel you are losing control, pause for 10 seconds and talk to a trusted adult.
- **Keep daily routines** («روال و روتین روزانه»), such as meals and bedtime.
- **Loud explosions:** talk about the sounds in advance at the child's level, and plan to go together to a safe place («به مکان امنی می‌رویم»). During the noise, hug, stay together, talk about happy memories or read a book. Don't sit silently focused on the noise.
- **Separated family:** be honest that you don't know yet, and say you are seeking accurate information. **A death:** don't hide or delay the truth.
- **Violence:** with younger children, say some people sometimes do bad things, it is not their fault or their family's, and many people are working to stop it. Hope for peace "without making promises". With older children who have phones, gently monitor what they watch, and remind them most people are kind.
- No advice on when to seek help and no helplines on this page.

[search] UNICEF Iran also has a Persian page «چگونه با کودکان درباره جنگ و درگیری حرف بزنیم» (probably a translation of §1.3) and Instagram infographics reprinted by Digiato. Not opened.

### 1.5 American Academy of Pediatrics (HealthyChildren.org), Schonfeld, "How to talk with kids about tragedies & other traumatic news events" (updated 28 Aug 2025)

[opened]
- Most children have already heard something. Ask what they heard, what questions they have and how they feel.
- Even preschoolers will hear about big events, so it is better **they hear it first from a parent** than from peers, media or overheard talk. "Even the youngest child needs accurate information, but you don't want to be too vague."
- School-age: basic facts (what, where, who), and reassurance that adults and authorities are working to keep people safe. Teens: more detail, but no unnecessary detail.
- Developmental delay: go by developmental level, not age. Autism: use what comforts *this* child (cuddling may not).
- **Media:** "Graphic information and images should be avoided." Keep young children away from repeated graphic images and sounds. With older children, preview coverage, watch together and pause to talk. Children meet news on phones, so talk ahead of time.
- Signs a child may need extra help: sleep problems, physical complaints, eating changes, regression, irritability, clinginess, unusual sadness, anxiety or fear. "Don't wait for symptoms. Start the discussion early, and keep the dialogue going."

### 1.6 AACAP Facts for Families

[opened] **No. 87, "Terrorism and War: How to Talk to Children"** (updated Sep 2023):
- "**Don't force children to talk** about things until they're ready." Offer drawing, toys or writing instead.
- "Children will usually know if you're not being honest." Expect to repeat answers, because repeat questions may be a request for reassurance. Be reassuring "without making promises you can't keep."
- **Avoid stereotyping** groups by race, nationality or religion. Children overhear adult conversations.
- It is fine to say you feel anxious, but don't pass your worries on in a way that weighs them down. Keep routines, school and family traditions. "Let children play and be kids."
- Watch for: ongoing sleep trouble, persistent upsetting thoughts or images, intense fears about death, and trouble separating from parents or going to school. A child who is preoccupied or very stressed "should be evaluated".

[opened] **No. 36, "Disaster: Helping Children Cope"** (updated Sep 2023):
- **Preschoolers:** fear of separation, bedwetting, trouble falling asleep, tantrums.
- **School-age:** poor concentration, irritability, misbehaviour, refusing school, clinging, nightmares, stomach aches and headaches.
- Children are "often more sensitive to parental worry during a crisis". Acknowledge that the danger is real. Pretending won't stop the worry.
- Higher risk if someone close was killed or seriously hurt, or the home or school was badly damaged. PTSD "often surfaces several months or even years later". Repetitive re-living play and nightmares are warning signs. "Treatment is available and can help."

### 1.7 US National Child Traumatic Stress Network (NCTSN), "Age-Related Reactions to a Traumatic Event" (2010)

[opened] PDF converted locally.
- **Preschool and young school-age:** helplessness, uncertainty about whether danger continues, generalised fear, difficulty putting feelings into words. They may lose skills: can't fall asleep alone, can't separate at school, won't play outside without family. Some speech and toileting loss, nightmares, night terrors. **"Traumatic play"** is "a repetitive and less imaginative form of play" that may be an attempt to change the outcome.
- **School-age:** persistent worry about own and others' safety, guilt or shame over what they did or didn't do, constant retelling, sleep problems, poor concentration, headaches and stomach aches, "unusually reckless or aggressive behavior".
- **How to help:** comfort, rest, "an opportunity to play or draw". Reassure them "that the traumatic event is over and that the children are safe". Keep caretaking consistent: **pick-ups on time, tell children where parents are**. "Tolerate regression in developmental tasks for a period of time." For older children: say their feelings are normal and correct distortions. Tell teachers when it affects learning.
- Family note: "the entire family is affected", including parents' "guilt in not being able to protect children."

### 1.8 US NIMH, *Helping Children and Adolescents Cope With Traumatic Events* (NIH Pub. 22-MH-8066)

[opened]
- **5 and under:** clinging, tantrums, regression, more fearful, the event shows up in pretend play.
- **6 and over:** school problems, withdrawal, avoiding reminders, anger, disruptive behaviour.
- "Many of these reactions are normal and will lessen with time."
- **Do:** let them cry, talk, write or draw. Keep routines. **Offer choices** to give a sense of control. A short time sleeping in the parent's room, or with a light on, is fine.
- **Don't:** expect them to be brave, push them to talk before they are ready, get angry at bedwetting or acting out, or make promises you can't keep.
- **Timing:** "If symptoms last **more than a month**", contact a health provider. Also seek help if flashbacks, racing heart and sweating, being easily startled, numbness or deep sadness last "more than a few weeks".
- Caregivers should "take steps to support their own ability to cope."

### 1.9 Expert consensus: Hobfoll et al., "Five essential elements"

[opened: abstract] Hobfoll et al., *Psychiatry* (2007; reprinted 2021). A world panel agreed on five principles for early to mid-term help after mass trauma, promoting:
1. a sense of **safety**
2. **calming**
3. **self- and community efficacy**
4. **connectedness**
5. **hope**

This is a useful organising frame for the guide. It is consensus extrapolated from related research, not trial evidence.

### 1.10 IASC MHPSS Guidelines (2007)

[recall, unverified] The IASC guidelines (2007) set out an intervention pyramid: basic services and security, then community and family supports, then focused non-specialised supports, then specialised services. They stress supporting caregivers so they can care for children, and restoring routines and child-friendly spaces. Not opened this session.

**Copy direction (section 1):**
- Lead with "you don't need perfect words: being calm, close and honest is what helps most" (WHO PFA, UNICEF).
- Use a four-step talk:
  1. Ask what they've heard.
  2. Give a short, true answer for their age.
  3. Say what grown-ups are doing to keep them safe.
  4. Close warmly and leave the door open.
- Name the "helpers" (rescuers, doctors, neighbours) as UNICEF does. Never name an enemy.
- Tell parents that repeated questions and repeated play are normal. They are how children digest events.

**Don't say:**
- "Nothing bad will ever happen" or "I promise we're safe forever." Every source warns against promises you can't keep.
- "Bad people / evil people / [any country or group] did this." UNICEF and AACAP warn against labels and stereotyping, and the app must stay neutral.
- "Make your child tell you everything that happened."
- "Be brave / big boys don't cry."

## 2. Common reactions by age, what fades, and when to get help

### 2.1 How common are lasting problems?

- **Most children recover.** In pooled data on children after single traumatic events, "low internalizing" symptoms was the most common and stable pattern (Sadeh et al., *JCPP* 2026, 9 studies, n=787) [opened: abstract].
- **About 1 in 6 develop PTSD.** Alisic et al. (2014), meta-analysis, 43 samples, n=3,563: the overall PTSD rate in trauma-exposed children was **15.9%** (95% CI 11.5–21.5). It was lowest in boys after non-interpersonal trauma (8.4%) and highest in girls after interpersonal trauma (32.9%) [opened: abstract]. Disasters count as non-interpersonal; war and attacks are mixed.
- **Natural recovery is front-loaded.** Hiller et al. (2016) meta-analysis: PTSD prevalence **fell about 50% between 1 and 6 months**, with most of the change in the first 3 months. "Little evidence of further change ... after 6 months", so a child still struggling then is unlikely to improve without help [opened: abstract]. This is the strongest evidence-based reason to say "if it's still getting in the way after about a month, ask for help; don't just wait it out."
- **Timing in guidelines:** NIMH says more than a month, or more than a few weeks for flashbacks, startle, numbness or deep sadness [opened]. NICE NG116 recommends **active monitoring within the first month** for less severe symptoms [search: NICE rationale page]. [recall, unverified] The PTSD diagnosis needs symptoms lasting more than one month (DSM-5). ICD-11 has no fixed month but needs "several weeks".
- **Delayed onset is possible:** AACAP says PTSD can surface "several months or even years later" [opened]. So the guide should say "also watch for new problems later, e.g. around anniversaries".

### 2.2 Red flags: send to a professional

Pooled from AACAP 36 and 87, NIMH, AAP and NCTSN [all opened]:
- Problems still **interfering with sleep, school, play or family life after about a month**, or getting worse instead of better.
- **Re-living the event**: frequent nightmares, flashbacks, play that stays grim and repetitive and doesn't calm the child.
- Strong avoidance (refusing school or going outside), being constantly on edge or very jumpy.
- Persistent sadness, withdrawal, numbness. In older children: intense fears about death, guilt, risk-taking, or talk of revenge.
- Loss of skills (speech, toileting) that doesn't come back over weeks.
- **Urgent, the same day:** any talk of wanting to die or self-harm (NIMH: "take seriously—even those said by children" [search]), hurting others, a child who stops eating or drinking, or a parent who can't keep themselves or the child safe → **115** (medical emergency, [recall, unverified]: check this matches the app's existing emergency guidance) / **123** social emergency / nearest health centre.

**Copy direction:** Use a "normal / keep an eye on / get help now" traffic light. Make the "normal" column long, so parents aren't alarmed by clinging, bedwetting, nightmares and war-play in the first weeks.

**Don't say:** "Your child has PTSD" or "is traumatised" (that is diagnosis). Don't say "children are resilient, they'll forget". Some don't, and help works better earlier.

## 3. News and media exposure

- **Holman, Garfin & Silver (2014), PNAS** [opened: abstract]. Surveyed 4,675 US adults 2–4 weeks after the Boston Marathon bombing. **Six or more hours a day of bombing-related media** in the first week was linked with **higher acute stress than being there in person** (b=15.61 vs 5.69), after adjusting for prior mental health. "Repeatedly engaging with trauma-related media content for several hours daily ... may prolong acute stress." These are adults, and the study is cross-sectional.
- **Holman, Garfin & Silver (2024), PNAS** review "It matters what you see": graphic images of war and terror may amplify distress, and the link may be cyclical (distressed people watch more). It calls for media literacy about viewing and **sharing** graphic images [opened: abstract].
- **Pfefferbaum et al. (2001), *Psychiatry*** [opened: abstract]. Over 2,000 middle-school students, 7 weeks after the Oklahoma City bombing. TV viewing was "extensive". **Among children with no physical or emotional exposure, more TV was directly related to more PTSD symptoms.** The authors frame it as "a small contribution". Heavy viewing could also be a *sign* of distress, so it "should be monitored".
- **Otto et al. (2007), *J Anxiety Disord*** [opened: abstract]. 166 children with no direct exposure to 9/11. **For younger children, the amount of TV viewing predicted PTSD symptoms.** Parental depression went with more symptoms, and family support with fewer. "Parental monitoring of media exposure may be important for younger children."
- **Dann & Gueron-Sela (2026), *Res Child Adolesc Psychopathol*** [opened: abstract]. Daily diary, 193 parents of preschoolers (mean age 4.5) during regional conflict. On days children had **more war-related media exposure, they had more negative mood and more parent–child strain**. **Parents' own news engagement affected children through the parent's mood and strain.** It is in a conflict setting close to the app's audience. Neutral to cite as "a 2026 study of families during a conflict".
- **Caveat:** Pfefferbaum et al. (2022) found **inconclusive** evidence linking mass-trauma media and *depression* in children [opened: abstract]. The evidence is strongest for stress and PTSD symptoms, and it is correlational.

**Copy direction:**
- "Turn the news off when little ones are around. That includes the radio, the TV in the background, and videos on your phone."
- "With older children, watch or read together, choose a trusted source, and stop if images are graphic."
- "Check the news at set times rather than all day. Constant updates raise everyone's stress, including yours." (UNICEF self-care, Holman 2014.)
- "Think before forwarding frightening videos in family groups." (Holman 2024. Relevant to WhatsApp, Telegram and Eitaa use in Iran; keep the wording generic.)

**Don't say:** "TV causes PTSD." The link is an association, and heavy viewing may also be a sign of distress. Don't tell parents to stop following news entirely: they may need safety information.

## 4. What helps, and what doesn't

### 4.1 Parent calm and parent stress (handle carefully)

- **Morris, Gabert-Quillen & Delahanty (2012), *J Pediatr Psychol*** meta-analysis, 35 studies [opened: abstract]. Parent post-traumatic stress and child post-traumatic stress were correlated (**r = 0.31**), and so were parent depression and child PTSS (**r = 0.32**). These are moderate associations, not causation, and partly shared exposure.
- **Ni et al. (2026), *Front Psychiatry*** [opened: abstract]. **343 Iranian mothers** of children aged 5–10 in conflict-affected areas, about 3 months after "a major military escalation". War stressors went with both maternal and child anxiety, and **maternal anxiety was associated with child anxiety**. **Religiosity and social support buffered** the link between war stress and maternal anxiety. Problem-focused coping and "challenge" appraisal were protective for mothers. Cross-sectional, self-report: mothers rated their own children.
- **Eruyar, Huemer & Vostanis (2018)** systematic review of refugee children (82 studies) [search]: parents' stress weakens parenting capacity, which predicts child problems, while **parental support and family connectedness protect**.
- **Rhamelani et al. (2026)** scoping review of family-centred post-disaster interventions [opened: abstract]: "improvements in caregiver wellbeing and family functioning were consistently associated with better child emotional and behavioral outcomes."
- Hahn et al. (2019) [opened: abstract]: when children get trauma-focused treatment, **parents' own symptoms often improve too** (62% of parents with clinical-level symptoms improved meaningfully). This is a hopeful, non-blaming message.

**Copy direction:** "Children read our faces. When you look after yourself, you are also looking after them." Offer concrete self-care: breathing, set news times, talking to a trusted adult, sleep, and prayer or faith practice if that is part of the family's life (Ni 2026 found religiosity buffered stress; mention it as one option, never as a prescription). Say "it's OK to show some feeling and name it: 'I was scared too, and now I'm calmer'" (UNICEF Iran, AACAP).

**Don't say:** "Your anxiety is causing your child's problems" or "your stress is harming your child." Say instead: "Children and parents affect each other; looking after yourself helps you both."

### 4.2 What helps (consistent across WHO PFA, UNICEF, NCTSN, NIMH, AACAP, AAP; all [opened])

1. **Safety and closeness:** stay together, extra cuddles, allow temporary co-sleeping or a night light, tell them where you'll be and be on time for pick-ups.
2. **Routines:** meals, bedtime, school, family traditions, as normal as possible.
3. **Honest, age-fit truth:** short and true, no scary details, correct rumours and "magical thinking" (it wasn't your fault). Say "I don't know" when you don't.
4. **Reassurance about what adults are doing:** rescuers, doctors, teachers, the family's own plan. Don't promise outcomes.
5. **Let them ask, again and again:** repeat questions are a request for reassurance.
6. **Play, drawing, stories, physical activity:** repetitive play about the event is normal. Gently steer it toward rescue or rebuilding. A 2026 meta-analysis of sport, play and movement interventions in crisis settings found small-to-moderate benefits for PTSD, depression and anxiety symptoms (Kara 2026, [opened: abstract]; low certainty).
7. **Small helping roles and choices:** pack the safe-bag, make a card, help a neighbour or a younger sibling (WHO PFA, UNICEF, NIMH "offer choices").
8. **Calm adult and calm body:** belly breathing together, make it a game for little ones.
9. **Prepare for loud noises and drills** at their level, with a plan of where to go together (UNICEF Iran). Links naturally to the existing earthquake-preparedness guide.
10. **Hope and connection:** time with relatives and friends, talk about good plans ahead (Hobfoll's five elements).

### 4.3 What doesn't help

- **Forcing children to talk or "get it all out".** Rose et al., Cochrane (2002) [opened: abstract]: single-session debriefing "did not reduce psychological distress nor prevent the onset of PTSD". One trial found **higher PTSD risk at one year** (OR 2.88). "Compulsory debriefing ... should cease." The trials were in adults; NICE NG116 extends this to children ("no benefit for children or adults, and some suggestion of worse outcomes", [search]). WHO PFA: "not recommended" [opened]. WHO 2013 also advises against benzodiazepines for acute stress or sleep problems in the first month (adults) [opened: WHO news release, 6 Aug 2013].
- Lying, or hiding a death (UNICEF Iran: "don't hide or delay the truth").
- Promises you can't keep. Getting angry at regression ("you're too big to wet the bed").
- Graphic news and adult worry-talk in the child's hearing.
- Labelling a whole group as "bad". It teaches fear and prejudice (UNICEF, AACAP).

**Copy direction:** "Let them lead. Some children talk, some draw, some play it out, some need time. All are OK."

**Don't say:** "Talking about it is the only way to heal." Don't present one "debrief" chat as therapy.

## 5. Effective help exists (treatment)

- **Trauma-focused CBT** is first-line for children with PTSD:
  - NICE NG116: individual TF-CBT. Consider **group TF-CBT for ages 7–17 after large-scale shared trauma in the last month**. EMDR is second-line for children [search].
  - WHO 2013 stress guidelines: TF-CBT and EMDR for children, adolescents and adults with PTSD [search; the WHO news release I opened mentions CBT and EMDR but not the child wording].
  - [recall, unverified] WHO mhGAP Intervention Guide 2.0 and the 2023 mhGAP guideline update keep this recommendation.
- **Iran:** Shooshtary, Panaghi & Moghadam (2008), *J Adolesc Health* [opened: abstract]. Adolescents after the Bam earthquake (135 given CBT, 33 comparison): post-traumatic symptoms "significantly decreased" with CBT given by therapists trained in a 3-day course. Non-randomised, small comparison group. (The abstract says "2004 earthquake". Bam was **26 Dec 2003** [recall, unverified, but widely documented]; use "2003".)
- Raeisi et al. (2026) [opened: full text]: Iran's MoHME counsellors were trained in WHO-PFA-based psychosocial support (18 h) plus 4 h of telephone crisis counselling, with referral to primary health care and **SERAJ community mental-health centres**.

**Copy direction:** One reassuring paragraph: "If your child is still struggling after a few weeks, there are short, proven talking-and-play treatments for children. A doctor, health centre or counselling line can help you find them." No therapy names in the main text; maybe a "learn more" line naming trauma-focused CBT.

**Don't say:** "Therapy will cure..." or any outcome promise. Don't recommend medicines.

## 6. Iran-specific

### 6.1 Earthquake studies (children)

- **Parvaresh & Bahramnezhad (2009), *Arch Iran Med*** [opened: abstract]. 433 Bam students who moved to Kerman, 4 months after the quake. PTSD (questionnaire) in **51.6% of under-15s** and 36.3% of over-15s, and behaviour problems in 31.3%. Risk factors: **younger age**, girls, **living apart from family**, injury, family loss. Questionnaire-based, so expect overestimates.
- [search] Mousavi et al. (2006): 45.1% PTSD in 284 adolescents 7–9 months after Bam. Mohamadi et al. (2010): 20% in 1,000 Bam students aged 11–16, versus Tehran controls. Khosropour et al. (2010) studied PTSD in Kerman students who only **watched Bam on TV** (abstract not available) [opened: record only].
- **Kermanshah 2017:** no child mental-health study found this session (gap). Peyravi et al. (2019) [opened: abstract]: in the first 10 days, mental health and mother-and-child care were handled by different organisations, leading to "a chaotic situation".
- **Copy use:** "Studies after the Bam earthquake found many children had stress symptoms months later, especially younger children and those separated from family. Keeping children with their families matters." Avoid quoting a %, because the figures vary with method and would alarm parents.

### 6.2 Conflict (2025–2026)

- **UNICEF Iran story "A Safe Space to Smile Again"** (12 Nov 2025) [opened]. Children showed "fear of loud noises, restless sleep, and sudden anger". UNICEF and a local NGO (IFDAA) ran group sessions (painting, storytelling, crafts) plus parenting sessions in Tehran, Alborz, Qom, Khuzestan and East Azerbaijan, reaching about 20,000 by Sep 2025. Families reported children "behaving less aggressively and are happier at home." This is a programme story, not an evaluation.
- **Ni et al. (2026)**: see §4.1.
- [search] A narrative review in an Iranian journal (via SID) on children's mental health after the 12-day conflict, and a letter on "hypervigilance" in Iranian society (Brieflands, *AMHSR*). Neither was opened, and neither has measured child data.

### 6.3 Helplines and services

- **123 and 1480** [opened: Mehr News, 2 Tir 1404 / 23 Jun 2025]. Behzisti's social emergency deputy said people who feel they "or those around them" need counselling can call 123 or 1480, **free, at any hour, day or night**. Fear, anxiety and hopelessness, "especially among women and children", were named. [search] During the crisis, 1480 moved from 8am–midnight to 24 hours, and recorded about 71,700 calls in a month, mostly about anxiety, stress, family relations and anger. **The app currently says "usually 8am to midnight", which matches normal times.** Copy could add "may extend hours during emergencies". Don't promise 24 hours.
- **MoHME crisis counselling: 190, extension 9** [opened: Raeisi et al., *Commun Med* 2026, full text, authors include MoHME staff]:
  - Activated nationwide for the 2026 conflict. About 700 psychologists, 08:00–24:00. Free. PFA, supportive counselling and stress management, with referral to primary health care and SERAJ centres.
  - **190** is MoHME's general public-health line. The paper says it is "distinct from emergency response services".
  - Similar phone support ran in the June 2025 conflict. The line "has remained active after the ceasefire" and is planned as a sustained service.
  - `helplines.ts` lists "190 or 1490" as an unverified poison line. This paper clarifies what 190 is. **Recheck on behdasht.gov.ir before adding "190 ext 9"**, because extension numbers change.
- **SERAJ** (Community-Based Mental and Social Health Centres) are named in the same paper [opened]. A possible "where to get help in person" option alongside the local health centre (مرکز خدمات جامع سلامت).

### 6.4 Moving, displacement and staying with relatives

- WHO PFA: keep children with caregivers and siblings, and don't let them get separated [opened]. Bam: living apart from family was a risk factor [opened]. Eruyar 2018: family connectedness protects [search]. UNICEF Iran: prepare children before leaving home and explain the steps simply. It's OK not to know if or when you'll return [opened].
- **Copy direction:** a short box, "If you have to leave home for a while":
  - Tell them simply what's happening and who's coming.
  - Let them bring a comfort item.
  - Keep bedtime and meal rituals even in a relative's home.
  - Keep siblings together.
  - Explain that strange feelings in a new place are normal.
  - Return to school or learning as soon as possible.
  - Keep in touch with family by phone.

**Don't say (Iran):**
- Any name of a country, government, army or leader.
- "Martyr" or "enemy" framing, or any opinion on who is right.
- Specific dates or places of attacks.
- Casualty figures.
- Use «حادثه‌های ترسناک»، «جنگ»، «حمله»، «انفجار»، «خبرهای ترسناک»، «بلایای طبیعی مثل زلزله و سیل».
- Avoid «دشمن» (enemy).

## Recommended outline: Guide A — "When scary things happen: talking with children about war, disasters and frightening news"

1. **Why it helps to talk:** children notice more than we think, and they "fill in the blanks". It's better they hear it from you (AAP, Save the Children).
2. **Before you talk: steady yourself:** 3 slow breaths. It's fine to say "I felt scared too" (UNICEF, UNICEF Iran).
3. **Four steps:**
   1. Ask what they've heard.
   2. Tell a short, true answer for their age.
   3. Say who is helping and what your family's plan is.
   4. End warmly: "You can ask me anytime."
4. **By age** (table below).
5. **Loud noises, alarms and shaking:** prepare, plan the safe place, hug, talk about happy memories or read together (UNICEF Iran). Link to the earthquake-preparedness guide.
6. **News and phones:** off around little ones. For older children, watch together, choose trusted sources, no graphic images, think before forwarding.
7. **Questions you can't answer:** "I don't know yet. I'll tell you when I know." No promises.
8. **Kindness, not blame:** no "bad people" labels. Notice the helpers. Small helping actions.
9. **If someone was hurt or died:** link to the grief guide. Don't hide or delay the truth.
10. **Look after yourself:** news at set times, talk to an adult, sleep, faith practice if it helps you; 1480 / 190 ext 9 (if verified).

## Recommended outline: Guide B — "Helping children in the weeks after"

1. **What's normal in the first weeks:** long list, by age.
2. **Routines and closeness:** temporary co-sleeping and night lights are fine; on-time pick-ups; say where you'll be.
3. **Play and drawing:** repeat play about the event is normal. Steer it gently toward rescue and rebuilding.
4. **Don't force talk:** let them lead (Cochrane, WHO PFA).
5. **Helping roles and choices.**
6. **Your own stress:** a parent check-in, and when you need help too.
7. **If you had to leave home** (short box, §6.4).
8. **When to get help:** traffic light. Still interfering after about a month, getting worse, or re-living the event → doctor, health centre or 1480. Any talk of wanting to die → today, 115/123 (check).
9. **Help works:** one paragraph (§5).
10. **Anniversaries and reminders:** reactions can return. Same steps again.

### Age table: reactions and what helps

| Age | Common reactions (usually ease over weeks) | What helps | Get help if… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Babies (0–1)** | Fussier, more crying, feeding and sleep changes, startle easily, very sensitive to parents' stress (Save the Children 0–3; WHO PFA) | Keep them close and warm, away from loud noise and chaos; regular feeding and sleep; calm, soft voice; look after the carer (WHO PFA) | Feeding or sleep problems that don't settle; the carer is too overwhelmed to cope |
| **Toddlers (1–3)** | Clinging, crying at separation, losing skills (words, toileting), tantrums, new fears, sleep problems, repetitive or aggressive play (Save the Children; NCTSN; AACAP) | Extra cuddles, don't separate, same routines, simple words ("It was a big noise. It's over. I'm here."), patience with regression (WHO PFA; NCTSN) | Lost skills don't return over weeks; play stops entirely; constant distress |
| **3–5 years** | Magical thinking ("Did I cause it?"), many repeated questions, playing out the event, nightmares, bedwetting, fear it will come back, not wanting to be alone (Save the Children; NIMH; AACAP) | Short true answers without scary details; "Not your fault"; event vs memory ("Thunder isn't an explosion"); news off; let them play it out and steer toward rescue (WHO PFA; Save the Children) | Still interfering after about a month; play stays grim and repetitive and doesn't calm them; won't leave you at all |
| **6–9 years** | Worry about family safety, retelling, stomach aches and headaches, poor concentration, reluctance about school, guilt ("I should have…"), more irritable (NCTSN; Save the Children 7–12; AACAP) | Facts and what's happening now; correct rumours; say what adults are doing; small jobs (safe-bag, help a sibling); routines and school; tell the teacher (NCTSN; WHO PFA) | Nightmares or flashbacks for weeks; avoiding school or outings; strong guilt; physical complaints with no cause that persist |
| **10–12 years** | Wants detail, follows news on phones, sense of unfairness, withdrawal, anger, may talk of revenge, may hide feelings to protect parents (Save the Children; NCTSN; NIMH) | Honest talk; watch and read news together; trusted sources; no graphic content; real helping roles; time with friends; "Don't expect them to be tough" (WHO PFA; AAP; UNICEF) | Numbness, persistent low mood, risk-taking; **any talk of self-harm or wanting to die → same day** |

### Short scripts parents can say (plain words; Persian drafts need native review)

**Toddler or young preschooler (after a loud event):**
- "That was a very big, loud noise. It scared us. It's over now. I'm here with you."
- «صدای خیلی بلندی بود. ترسیدیم. الان تمام شده. من پیشت هستم.»

**4-year-old (asks "Will the bombs/earthquake come again?"):**
- "Something scary happened, and lots of grown-ups are working hard to keep people safe. Our job is to stay together. If we hear a loud noise, we go to our safe place together and I hold you tight. It wasn't anything you did. You can ask me again anytime."
- «یک اتفاق ترسناک افتاد و آدم‌بزرگ‌های زیادی دارند تلاش می‌کنند همه در امان باشند. کار ما این است که کنار هم بمانیم. اگر صدای بلندی شنیدیم، با هم می‌رویم جای امن‌مان و من محکم بغلت می‌کنم. تقصیر تو نبود. هر وقت خواستی دوباره بپرس.»

**4-year-old (bedtime fears):**
- "Your body remembers the scary day. That's why it's hard to sleep. You can sleep near me for now, and we'll leave the little light on."
- «بدنت آن روز ترسناک را یادش مانده؛ برای همین خوابیدن سخت است. فعلاً می‌توانی نزدیک من بخوابی و چراغ کوچک را روشن می‌گذاریم.»

**9-year-old (has seen news on a phone):**
- "What have you seen or heard? ... Some of that is true, and some isn't. Here's what we know: [one or two facts]. I don't know everything yet. When I learn more, I'll tell you. Doctors, rescuers and lots of people are helping. Some videos online are made to scare people, so if you see something upsetting, come and show me and we'll talk."
- «چه چیزهایی دیدی یا شنیدی؟ ... بعضی‌اش درست است و بعضی‌اش نه. چیزی که ما می‌دانیم این است: [یکی دو واقعیت]. من هم همه‌چیز را نمی‌دانم؛ هر وقت بیشتر فهمیدم بهت می‌گویم. پزشک‌ها، امدادگرها و آدم‌های زیادی دارند کمک می‌کنند. اگر چیز ناراحت‌کننده‌ای دیدی، بیا به من نشانش بده تا با هم حرف بزنیم.»

**9-year-old (feels guilty or helpless):**
- "Lots of children, and grown-ups too, keep thinking 'I should have done something.' It wasn't your fault. Would you like a job in our family safety plan?"
- «خیلی از بچه‌ها و حتی بزرگ‌ترها مدام فکر می‌کنند "کاش کاری کرده بودم". تقصیر تو نبود. دوست داری در برنامه‌ی ایمنی خانواده یک کار به عهده بگیری؟»

**Parent to self:**
- "I'm scared too, and that's normal. My child needs me calm enough, not perfect."
- «من هم می‌ترسم و این طبیعی است. بچه‌ام به آرامشِ کافی من نیاز دارد، نه بی‌نقص بودنم.»

## Daily tips (≤30 words each)

1. Ask "What have you heard?" before you explain anything. Then correct what's wrong, gently. (UNICEF; AAP)
2. Keep answers short and true. No scary details for young children, and it's fine to say "I don't know yet." (WHO PFA; UNICEF)
3. Turn off news, including background TV and phone videos, when young children are around. (AAP; WHO PFA; Otto 2007)
4. Check the news at set times, not all day. Constant updates raise stress for adults and children alike. (UNICEF; Holman 2014)
5. Keep meals and bedtime at the usual times where you can. Familiar routines help children feel safe. (WHO PFA; UNICEF Iran)
6. Clinging, bedwetting and nightmares are common after scary events. Be patient; for most children these ease over weeks. (NCTSN; NIMH)
7. If your child plays out the scary event, that's normal. Gently join in and help the story end with rescue or rebuilding. (Save the Children; NCTSN)
8. Don't push your child to talk. Let them know they can come to you anytime, or draw it instead. (AACAP; NIMH; Cochrane)
9. Give your child a small helping job, like packing the safe-bag or making a card. Helping eases helplessness. (WHO PFA; UNICEF)
10. Practise slow belly breaths together. For little ones, make it a game: "Smell the flower, blow out the candle." (UNICEF Iran)
11. Tell your child it wasn't their fault. Young children often secretly think they caused bad things. (WHO PFA; Save the Children)
12. Say where you'll be and be on time for pick-ups. Predictable care rebuilds a sense of safety. (NCTSN)
13. Avoid "bad people" labels. Point to the helpers instead: doctors, rescuers, neighbours. (UNICEF; AACAP)
14. Look after yourself too. Children and parents affect each other, and support for you helps your child. (Save the Children; Morris 2012)
15. If worries still get in the way of sleep, school or play after about a month, ask a doctor or call 1480. (NIMH; Hiller 2016)
