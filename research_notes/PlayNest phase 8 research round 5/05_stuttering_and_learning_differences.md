## 1. Stuttering (ages about 2–8)

### 1.1 What it is, and how it differs from ordinary bumpy speech

- **Typical disfluency** (everyone does it, more so while language is growing fast): "um"/"uh", repeating a whole word or phrase, revising a sentence, unfinished thoughts. **Stuttering** shows as repeated sounds or syllables ("b-b-ball"), repeated one-syllable words, stretched sounds ("sssun"), and blocks (getting stuck with no sound). Head nods or eye blinks may come with it. It varies from day to day. [opened: CHSA "Stuttering and cluttering", last reviewed 1 May 2026, https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/stuttering-and-cluttering]
- The Stuttering Foundation (SFA) brochure separates typical disfluency (repeats once or twice, comes and goes, usually ages 1½–5) from milder signs of stuttering (repeats sounds more than twice, facial tension, pitch rising during repeats, silent blocks of several seconds, disfluency present more often than not). Its more severe signs are stuttering on more than 10% of speech, effortful and tense stuttering, frequent complete blocks, swapping or avoiding words, and disfluency in most situations. It also says disfluency that vanishes "for several weeks" and returns may be a normal stage. [opened: SFA, "If you think your child is stuttering", compiled by Guitar & Conture, undated, https://www.stutteringhelp.org/if-you-think-your-child-stuttering]
- UK bodies say "stammering". US and Australian bodies say "stuttering". They mean the same thing. Persian: **«لکنت»** or **«لکنت زبان»**. An Iranian university leaflet uses **«ناروانی»** (disfluency) for the wider category. [opened: leaflet below]

### 1.2 How common, and when it starts

- **Early Language in Victoria Study (ELVS), Melbourne, 1,619 children followed from 8 months:**
  - **By age 3:** cumulative incidence of onset was **8.5%**. Onset was often **sudden, over 1–3 days (49.6%)**, and almost always came after the child began combining words (97.1%). Children who stuttered were **not more shy or withdrawn** beforehand. Male sex, being a twin, higher vocabulary at age 2 and higher maternal education predicted onset, but together these explained only **3.7%** of the variation. The authors say clinicians "can reassure parents that onset is not unusual up to 3 years of age". [opened: Reilly et al., *Pediatrics* 2009, PMID 19117892]
  - **By age 4:** cumulative incidence was **11.2%** (95% CI 9.7–12.8%). At age 4 the children who stuttered had **slightly stronger** language and nonverbal scores and better parent-reported quality of life, and were "otherwise similar" to peers. Conclusion: "adverse affects are not the norm in the first year after onset". [opened: Reilly et al., *Pediatrics* 2013, PMID 23979093]
- MPC: about **5%** of children stammer at some point ("more recent studies suggest up to 8%"); about **1% of adults**. Onset is usually **2–5 years**, but it can start later, gradually or suddenly. Boys and girls are about equal at first, but girls more often stop, so later there are **4–5 times as many boys** as girls. [opened: MPC "Facts about stammering", undated (© 2026), https://michaelpalincentreforstammering.org/about-stammering/parents/more-about-stammering]
- CHSA (ASHA-backed): stuttering usually starts **between 2 and 6**. [opened]
- **Why the figures differ:** ELVS counted every new onset in a community cohort, with a speech pathologist confirming each case. Older figures (about 5%) are lifetime estimates from other methods. Prevalence (who stutters right now) is always much lower than incidence (who ever started). [inference from the sources above]

### 1.3 Natural recovery: real, but slower than many leaflets suggest

- **Yairi & Ambrose (Illinois):** 84 preschoolers followed for at least 4 years from near onset. Their "conservative" estimates were **74% recovery and 26% persistence**. Recovery was **spread over about 4 years after onset**. [opened: *JSLHR* 1999, PMID 10515508]
- **ELVS:** only **6.3% (9 of 142)** had recovered **within 12 months** of onset. [opened: Reilly 2013] By **age 7**, **65%** had recovered. [opened: Kefalianos et al., *JSLHR* 2017, PMID 28979988]
- A 2026 preprint GWAS (not peer-reviewed) says that stuttering "affects up to 11% of children, with around one-fifth developing a persistent stutter". [opened: Jackson et al., medRxiv preprint 2026, DOI 10.64898/2026.09.15.26363183]
- **Tension to note:** MPC says recovery "usually happens within about a year of onset, often before school". [opened] ELVS found that recovery within a year was rare (6.3%). Most recovery happens, but over **years** rather than months. Copy should say "many children stop, often over a few years" and not promise a time frame.
- STAMMA: "it can't be predicted which children will stop", and continuing to stammer is "OK"; many people who stammer live happy, successful lives. [opened: STAMMA "If your child is stammering", undated (leaflets dated Oct 2024), https://stamma.org/get-support/parents]

### 1.4 What raises the chance that stuttering continues

- **Meta-analysis, 11 longitudinal studies:** children who persisted were **older at onset**, had **more frequent stuttering-like disfluencies**, **less accurate speech sounds** and **lower expressive and receptive language** scores. **Boys** and children with a **family history of stuttering** were more likely to persist. [opened: Singer et al., *JSLHR* 2020, PMID 32772868]
- **52 preschoolers followed:** family history, poorer articulation/phonology scores, more frequent stuttering, and poorer nonword repetition all predicted persistence. A model combining them predicted best. [opened: Walsh, Christ & Weber, *JSLHR* 2021, PMID 34260279]
- MPC lists family history, being a boy, and having (or having had) speech sound difficulties. These "increase vulnerability but don't guarantee persistence". [opened]
- ELVS at age 7: girls with stronger communication skills at age 2 had higher odds of recovery; the same was not seen in boys. [opened: Kefalianos 2017]
- **Time since onset:** this is the referral criterion in most guidance (see §1.6). Yairi's group also reports that children still stuttering 12+ months after onset are less likely to recover without help. [recall, unverified]
- None of these predicts reliably for one child. The app should present them as **reasons to get an assessment sooner**, never as a forecast.

Sources [all opened]: SFA "7 tips for talking with your child" (Guitar & Conture, undated), https://www.stutteringhelp.org/7-tips-talking-your-child; STAMMA "Talking with a child who stammers" (© 2018–2026), https://stamma.org/talking-child-who-stammers; STAMMA parents page (above); MPC "Tips for parents" (undated, © 2026), https://michaelpalincentreforstammering.org/about-stammering/parents/tips-for-parents; CHSA (above).

**Where they agree:** slow, unhurried family talk; wait; never finish the child's words; listen to the message; daily calm one-to-one time; stay warm and calm; parents did not cause it. STAMMA says "nothing you have done has caused it". It also says stammering is not caused by poor parenting, anxiety, or speaking more than one language, and that research points to brain-wiring differences and genetics. [opened]

**Where they differ, or where the evidence is thin:**
1. **Praising fluent speech.** In the **Lidcombe Program**, parents, under a speech therapist's guidance, give "verbal contingencies". These include praise for stutter-free speech and occasional gentle requests to self-correct. [recall, unverified; the Lidcombe site I opened does not describe them] STAMMA and MPC warn against praising fluency in everyday life. **These fit together if the app says:** "Praise what your child says. If a speech therapist teaches you a specific programme, follow their instructions." The app must not teach a do-it-yourself Lidcombe.
2. **Parents changing their own speech.** All three charities recommend it. Whether a slower parent speaking rate *by itself* reduces stuttering has only small, mixed studies behind it. [recall, unverified] Frame it as making talking feel unhurried, not as a treatment.
3. **Acceptance vs fluency.** UK charities lead with "it's OK to stammer". Fluency-focused programmes aim for zero or near-zero stuttering. A 2025 published "clinical conversation" between researchers and clinicians disagreed about whether early intervention should centre on reducing stuttering or on ease of communication and the child's feelings. Its authors say the evidence "should be interpreted cautiously". [opened: Edwards et al., *Int J Speech-Lang Pathol* 2025, PMID 39218003] The app can hold both: get help early, and make sure the child knows they are fine as they are.
4. **Wait or act?** See §1.6. The MPC explicitly says to seek advice even if someone has told you to "wait and see".

### 1.6 When to see a speech therapist (cited criteria; present as "reasons to ask", not a score)

- **CHSA/ASHA:** contact a speech-language pathologist if the child has stuttered for **6–12 months or more**, **started after age 3½**, stutters **more often** over time, **tenses up or struggles**, or **avoids talking or says talking is too hard**. [opened]
- **MPC:** seek a specialist if you think your child might stammer, if it has lasted **over a year without lessening overall**, or if you are **worried for any reason**, including after being told to "wait and see". [opened]
- **SFA:** stuttering on more than 10% of speech, effort or tension, avoidance, frequent blocks, disfluency in most situations. [opened]
- **From the persistence research** (§1.4): a **family history** of stuttering, or **speech sound or language difficulties** as well, are reasons to ask sooner. [opened: Singer 2020]
- **Always ask** if the child is upset, embarrassed or avoiding talking, or if the parent is worried. Every body above says this in some form.
- **Iran:** the head of the Iran Speech Therapy Scientific Association said misbeliefs such as "it will go by itself" cause families to lose the "golden window" («پنجره طلایی») and urged early consultation. [opened: Mehr News, 29 Azar 1403 / 19 Dec 2024, https://www.mehrnews.com/news/6321449] An Iranian university leaflet tells families to see a speech-language pathologist («آسیب‌شناس گفتار و زبان (گفتاردرمانگر)») when signs appear. [opened: leaflet below]

### 1.7 Treatment evidence (brief)

- **Lidcombe Program RCT** (New Zealand, 54 children aged 3–6, at least 6 months after onset). At 9 months, the treatment group stuttered on **1.5%** of syllables vs **3.9%** for controls. The difference was more than double the pre-set clinically worthwhile margin. [opened: Jones et al., *BMJ* 2005, PMID 16096286]
- **RESTART-DCM trial** (Netherlands, 199 children aged 3–6). Lidcombe (direct) was compared with RESTART-DCM, an indirect approach that changes the demands placed on the child's speech. At 18 months, **76.5% vs 71.4%** were non-stuttering, which is not a significant difference. Lidcombe reduced stuttering faster in the first 3 months. Both approaches did better than natural recovery would be expected to. [opened: de Sonneville-Koedoot et al., *PLoS One* 2015, PMID 26218228]
- **School age:** a telehealth Lidcombe Phase II trial with 37 children aged 6–12 found that about a third reached near-zero stuttering at 12 months, and psychosocial measures improved. There was no control group. [opened: Johnson et al., *J Fluency Disord* 2024, PMID 38613876; 2-year follow-up 2026, PMID 43019803]
- **Outside English-speaking countries:** Lidcombe works, but reaching near-zero stuttering can take **up to 3 times longer**, mostly because parents need more time to learn the verbal contingencies. [opened: Subasi et al., *Folia Phoniatr Logop* 2022, PMID 34289470] An Iranian case report treated a Baluchi–Persian bilingual 8-year-old in both languages. [opened: Bakhtiar & Packman, *Folia Phoniatr Logop* 2009, PMID 19786790]
- **Palin Parent–Child Interaction (Palin PCI)**, from the MPC, is for children under 7. It works with the parents to build the child's confidence. A one-year follow-up of 55 children reported less stammering and more confident parents. [search: MPC therapy page]
- **Copy direction:** "Speech therapy for young children who stutter has good evidence. Different programmes seem to work about equally well. Your therapist will choose one with you." Name no programme as best.

### 1.8 Teasing and school

- Speech-therapy literature recommends: teaching the child about stuttering and about bullying; practising assertive replies through role-play; desensitisation and acceptance work; with the child's agreement, a short classroom presentation about stuttering; and helping teachers create a class where "it is not okay to bully, but it is okay to stutter". In one case study, peers' negative comments fell after a classroom presentation. [opened: Murphy, Yaruss & Quesal, *J Fluency Disord* 2007, PMID 17499126; Yaruss et al., *Semin Speech Lang* 2018, PMID 30142645]
- **Adolescents who stutter:** 85% had self-esteem in the normal range and 65% did not see stuttering as stigmatising, but **60% "rarely" or "never" talked about it**. Younger teens felt it more negatively. [opened: Blood et al., *J Fluency Disord* 2003, PMID 12809749]
- **Iran, teacher attitudes:** 150 elementary teachers in Zahedan were surveyed with the Persian POSHA-S. Their overall attitude score was at the **24th percentile** of the international database. Beliefs about causes and personality were less favourable, but self-reactions were near average. The authors call for teacher education. [opened: Asadollahpour et al., *J Commun Disord* 2026, PMID 42715654]
- MPC runs a "What pupils want you to know" page for teachers. [search; not opened]

### 1.9 Bilingual children

- STAMMA: stammering is not caused by speaking more than one language. [opened]
- **Over-reporting:** in a large US cohort (ECLS-K:2011), the reported prevalence, sex ratio, onset and recovery of "stuttering" did not match clinical patterns. The author argues that teachers and parents may call typical disfluency "stuttering", and that this inflates figures for bilingual children. [opened: Gahl, *Front Psychol* 2023, PMID 37546483]
- **Severity once a child stutters:** a 2025 meta-analysis (26 studies, Iranian authors) concluded that bilingual children who stutter show **greater severity** than monolingual children, more so in their second language, but respond to treatment just as well. The authors note inconsistent definitions. [opened: Rasoli Jokar et al., *Int J Pediatr Otorhinolaryngol* 2025, PMID 40158476] A 2026 study of 22 bilingual Lebanese children found **no difference** in overall severity between their two languages. [opened: Merouwe et al., *Folia Phoniatr Logop* 2026, PMID 42102010]
- **Iranian expert opinion that differs:** the Iran Speech Therapy association head suggested a speech evaluation **before** raising a child bilingually, saying that multiple languages "may increase the risk" for some children. [opened: Mehr News 2024] This is opinion in a news report, not evidence. It conflicts with STAMMA and with the app's bilingual guide. Many Iranian families speak Azeri, Kurdish, Arabic, Baluchi, Gilaki or another language with Persian, so this needs careful handling.
- **Copy direction:** "Speaking two languages does not cause stuttering. Don't drop a home language because of it. A therapist can assess both languages." Signal for the speech-therapy note: ask for an assessment in both languages.

### 1.10 Iran: services, studies, attitudes

- **Workforce and cost:** over **4,000** speech therapists hold a medical-council number (about 3,000 with bachelor's degrees). Insurance coverage is **partial**: supplementary insurance pays up to a ceiling and basic insurance covers some of the cost. Insurers pay 5–6 months late, so many therapists have ended their insurance contracts. [opened: Mehr News, 19 Dec 2024] I found nothing on the city/rural distribution. [gap]
- **Parent leaflet:** Iran University of Medical Sciences, rehabilitation faculty (author M. Tarameshloo, PDF dated June 2019). It says stuttering exists in all cultures and is unrelated to intelligence or economic level; its cause is unknown; prevalence is quoted as **1–5%**; onset is mostly **2–3½**; girls recover sooner and more often; it often resolves spontaneously; early treatment can remove or greatly reduce it; and families should go to a speech-language pathologist. [opened: https://rehab.iums.ac.ir/uploads/33/2022/Sep/03/اختلالات روانی گفتار و لکنت.pdf]
- **Prevalence studies:** Ardabil primary pupils, **4.91%** (boys 6.08%, girls 3.16%). The abstract does not say how stuttering was identified, and the case-control part had only 60 children. [opened: Aghajani & Ghorbani, *School Psychology* (UMA) 2018, https://jsp.uma.ac.ir/article_654.html] An Arak study gave about 1% within an 11.9% overall speech-disorder rate. [search; digits garbled in the snippet] **No national figure was found.**
- **Parent reactions:** a Persian Reaction to Speech Disfluency Scale was given to 110 parents of 3–6-year-olds who stutter. Cognitive reactions (worry, thinking about it) were most frequent and behavioural reactions least. Fathers and mothers differed in emotional reactions. [opened: Salehpoor et al., *Int J Pediatr Otorhinolaryngol* 2020, PMID 32388081]
- **Therapists' priorities:** 74 Iranian speech therapists ranked reducing overt stuttering highest and communication participation lowest, at all ages. Themes included "pressure for fluency", relapse and acceptance. [opened: Rasoli Jokar et al., *AJSLP* 2026, PMID 42868313] Families may meet a strongly fluency-focused model. The app can gently add the child's confidence and participation to the picture.
- **Caution:** the Ardabil study reports worse family communication and parent-child relationship scores in families of children who stutter. It is cross-sectional, so cause and effect could run either way. **Do not use it to imply that parents cause stuttering.**
- **Folk beliefs:** fright («ترسیدن»), imitating a stutterer, tickling, or forcing a left-handed child to use the right hand are commonly blamed. [recall, unverified; no Iranian survey opened] The teacher study (Zahedan) supports "less favourable beliefs about etiology". Handle these with respect: "Many families have heard that a fright can cause stuttering. Researchers now think it mostly comes from how the brain organises speech, and it often runs in families."

**Copy direction (stuttering):** Normalise it ("about 1 in 10 young children stutter for a while, often starting suddenly as sentences grow"). Make clear it is nobody's fault. Give practical calm-talk habits. Present referral reasons plainly, and add "you can ask any time you're worried". Present therapy as effective and parent-involved. Keep the child's feelings at the centre.

**Don't say:**
- "Most children grow out of it within a few months", or any recovery timeline.
- "Stuttering is caused by anxiety, shock, bilingualism, or parenting."
- "Tell them to slow down / take a breath / start again."
- "Your child will / won't grow out of it" (risk factors don't predict for one child).
- "Ignore it completely." (The guidance says stay calm and neutral, and acknowledge it if the child notices.)
- Anything that coaches parents through Lidcombe-style praise or correction without a therapist.
- "Stuttering children are shy / less clever." ELVS found no pre-onset shyness, and stronger language at age 4.

## 2. Dyslexia and other learning differences (ages about 5–12)

### 2.1 Definitions in plain words

- **IDA, revised definition (approved October 2025):** "Dyslexia is a specific learning disability characterized by difficulties in word reading and/or spelling that involve accuracy, speed, or both and vary depending on the orthography." These difficulties "occur along a continuum of severity and persist even with instruction that is effective for the individual's peers". Causes are "complex and involve combinations of genetic, neurobiological, and environmental influences". Phonological and morphological weaknesses are "common but not universal", and "early oral language weaknesses often foreshadow literacy challenges". Secondary effects include reading-comprehension problems and less reading experience; well-being and employment may be affected. Support "before and during the early years of education is particularly effective". The reference to "other cognitive abilities" (the old IQ-discrepancy wording) was removed. [opened: National Center on Improving Literacy summary, 2026, https://improvingliteracy.org/?p=5698; IDA definition page itself returned 403]
- **NHS:** dyslexia is "a common learning difficulty where your brain works differently to most people". It mainly affects reading, writing and using numbers, and it **does not affect intelligence**. A GP cannot diagnose it, but can check hearing and may suggest an eye test. Assessment is by an educational psychologist or specialist teacher, starting with the school's SENCO. [opened: NHS "Dyslexia", reviewed 11 Mar 2026, https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dyslexia/]
- **DSM-5 "specific learning disorder" (APA plain-language page):** persistent difficulty learning reading, writing or maths that lasts **at least 6 months despite targeted help**, puts skills well below what is expected for the child's age, and starts in the school years. It is **not better explained** by intellectual disability, vision or hearing problems, lack of teaching, economic disadvantage, or learning in a new language. APA notes that "learning difference" is a gentler term used with children. [opened: APA "What is specific learning disorder", reviewed Mar 2024, https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/specific-learning-disorder/what-is-specific-learning-disorder]
- **Other learning differences** to name briefly, without detail: maths (dyscalculia, «اختلال ریاضی»), written expression or spelling («اختلال نوشتن» / «دیکته‌نویسی»), and developmental language disorder. They often overlap with each other and with ADHD and anxiety. [opened: APA]
- **AAP/AAO and partners (2011):** dyslexia is "a receptive language-based learning disability", usually arising from a "deficit in the phonologic component of language". [opened: Handler et al., *Pediatrics* 2011, PMID 21357342]
- **Persian terms:** «نارساخوانی» (most common), «خوانش‌پریشی», «اختلال یادگیری ویژه/خاص», «ناتوانی یادگیری». The Ministry of Education wording is «مشکلات ویژه یادگیری». [opened: SNN 2020 screening article] «دیرآموز» means slow learner (low-average IQ), which is a different group; don't conflate the two. [opened: YJC 2025 names both separately]

### 2.2 How common, and why figures vary so much

- APA: **5–15%** of school-age children have a learning disability; about **80%** of them have a reading difficulty. The same page says dyslexia "is estimated to affect 20% of the population" and affects males and females equally. [opened] The 20% figure is at the very top of the range and depends on a broad cut-off. **Don't use it.**
- Cochrane: 16% of children read below the average range for their age, and **5%** have significant, severe reading problems. [opened: McArthur et al. 2018, below]
- **Iran:**
  - Meta-analysis (18 studies, 28,952 pupils): **5.7%** (95% CI 4.5–6.9%); boys 7.5%, girls 4%. The figure depends on the test used: **3.4%** with informal reading tests vs **8.8%** with the screening inventory reading test. [opened: Sakhai et al., *Iran J Psychiatry* 2025, PMID 40521282]
  - A newer survey of 1,400 grade 2–5 pupils (non-word reading test plus WISC-IV) found **10.4%**; boys 14.5%. [opened: Asadollahpour et al., *Indian J Psychol Med* 2026, PMID 41211087]
  - Qom: 5.2% of 1,562. [opened: Pouretemad et al., *Arch Iran Med* 2011, PMID 21726102]
- **Arab countries:** pooled **11%** (18 studies). [opened: Aldakhil, *Res Dev Disabil* 2024, PMID 39079361]
- **Why figures vary:** dyslexia is a **continuum** (IDA), so the cut-off chosen sets the rate. Results also depend on the test, on whether teaching quality and IQ are taken into account, on sample size, on the language's spelling system, and on whether "boys" figures reflect referral bias. [synthesis of the above]
- **Copy direction:** "Roughly 1 in 20 to 1 in 10 children find reading much harder than classmates despite good teaching."

### 2.3 Early signs by age (prompts to watch and ask, not a test)

- **Before school (3–5):** children at family risk of dyslexia, as a group, show **later language development as toddlers**. Before school they show difficulties with phonological skills (rhyme, hearing sounds in words), with **letter knowledge**, and with **rapid naming** (quickly naming familiar colours or pictures). Family risk is a continuous risk, not a sentence: some at-risk children read normally. [opened: Snowling & Melby-Lervåg, *Psychol Bull* 2016, PMID 26727308] Persistent speech-sound problems at school entry add a small risk. Co-occurring language difficulty adds a larger one. [opened: Hayiou-Thomas et al., *JCPP* 2017, PMID 28102620]
- **School age:** NHS signs were listed in round 4 (reading well below expected, slow hesitant reading aloud, spelling errors, trouble with sequences and organisation). [opened in round 4] IDA adds that difficulty is **in spite of teaching that works for classmates**. [opened]
- **Persian-specific:**
  - Pouretemad (Qom) found **phonological errors** most common, then pragmatic and visual errors. The authors argue that **Persian without short-vowel marks behaves like an opaque script**, so it is not as "easy" as it looks in grade 1. [opened: Arch Iran Med 2011]
  - So a child who coped while the short-vowel marks («اِعراب») were printed may struggle when they disappear (by about grade 3, per round 4). Repeated guessing of words from their outline is also worth noticing. [inference; recheck with an Iranian reading specialist]
- **Emotional signs:** avoiding reading, stomach-aches before school, "I'm stupid". Adolescents with dyslexia reported more anxiety, depression and physical complaints than peers; in primary-age children the difference was not significant. [opened: Giovagnoli et al., *Front Psychol* 2020, PMID 32265786]

### 2.4 What helps

- **What Works Clearinghouse, K–3 foundational skills (2016, revised 2019):** **strong** evidence for teaching awareness of speech sounds and how they link to letters, and for teaching decoding, word parts, and writing/recognising words. **Moderate** evidence for daily reading of connected text. **Minimal** evidence for academic language and vocabulary instruction (minimal means few studies, not "doesn't work"). [opened: https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/21]
- **Cochrane review, phonics for English-speaking poor readers (14 RCTs, 923 participants):** phonics training **probably improves word-reading fluency** (moderate-quality evidence, SMD about 0.45) and **may improve** accuracy and spelling (low quality). It **only slightly** improved reading comprehension (SMD 0.28, CI crosses zero). [opened: McArthur et al., *Cochrane* 2018, CD009115.pub3, PMID 30480759]
- **AAP 2011:** remedial programmes should include decoding, fluency training, vocabulary and comprehension. Most effective programmes use **daily, intensive, explicit** teaching of phonemic awareness and phonics. [opened]
- **Iran:**
  - Pupils attending provincial learning-disability centres (Ardabil, 248 pupils) did better in reading, maths, writing and GPA than untrained peers (pre/post with a control group). [opened: Narimani et al., *J Learning Disabilities* (UMA) 2012, https://jld.uma.ac.ir/article_93.html]
  - A structured programme (Barton) improved fluency in Ilam grade 2–3 pupils. [opened: Azizifar et al., *J Educ Health Promot* 2019, PMID 31867352]
  - These are small, non-blinded studies. They are encouraging but not strong.
- **At home:** keep reading **to** the child and enjoying books, so vocabulary and comprehension keep growing even while decoding is hard (round 4: shared reading continues to matter). Audiobooks and the parent as reader also help. [inference from IDA "reduced reading experience" as a secondary consequence]

### 2.5 What does not help (or has weak evidence)

- **Vision-based treatments.** The joint statement of the AAP, the American Association for Pediatric Ophthalmology and Strabismus (AAPOS), the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) and orthoptic bodies (2009, revised 2011) says children with dyslexia have the **same visual function and eye health** as other children. Science does not support vision therapy, eye exercises, tracking exercises, "training" glasses, prisms, or **coloured lenses and filters**. It also says there is "no valid evidence" that children who do vision therapy respond better to teaching. [opened: Pediatrics 2009, PMID 19651597; Handler et al. 2011]
  - Disagreement: a 2002 review by the overlay researcher Arnold Wilkins claims reliable, non-placebo speed gains for about 5% of children. [opened: *Ophthalmic Physiol Opt* 2002, PMID 12358317] A 2016 systematic review (51 papers) found most studies at high or unclear risk of bias. The better studies gave **less** support, and effects were small or similar to placebo. [opened: Griffiths et al., *Ophthalmic Physiol Opt* 2016, PMID 27580753]
  - Copy direction: an eye test is still sensible, because ordinary vision problems should be treated, but coloured overlays are not a dyslexia treatment.
- **Brain-training apps.** Working-memory training improves the trained task but shows **no convincing transfer** to word decoding, reading comprehension or arithmetic (87 publications). [opened: Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme, *Perspect Psychol Sci* 2016, PMID 27474138] Fast ForWord had **no significant effect** on any reading or language outcome. [opened: Strong et al., *JCPP* 2011, PMID 20950285]
- **Waiting to "grow into" reading** after clear, persistent difficulty: IDA stresses early support. [opened]
- **Not covered this session:** motor, balance or "cerebellar" exercise programmes, fish-oil supplements, special dyslexia fonts. [recall, unverified: evidence is weak] Leave them out or mark them "not shown to help" after checking.

### 2.6 Intelligence, effort and strengths

- Dyslexia is not linked to intelligence (NHS). The IDA 2025 definition dropped the comparison with other cognitive abilities. [opened] Children of any ability can have dyslexia.
- It is **not laziness and not a lack of effort.** The IDA definition says difficulties persist "even with instruction that is effective for the individual's peers". [opened]
- **"Dyslexic superpower":** a meta-analysis of 20 studies found **no creativity advantage** for people with dyslexia as a group. Adults showed a small advantage, and verbal creativity was weaker. [opened: Erbeli, Peng & Rice, *J Learn Disabil* 2022, PMID 33899570] Copy can say "every child has strengths; find and grow theirs". It should not claim dyslexia brings special gifts.

### 2.7 Iran: how families reach help

- **School-entry screening,** «سنجش سلامت جسمانی و آمادگی تحصیلی نوآموزان»:
  - Run by the **Exceptional Education Organisation** («سازمان آموزش و پرورش استثنایی») for all children entering grade 1. It covers vision, hearing, height and weight, academic readiness («آمادگی تحصیلی») and developmental disorders.
  - Parents book online through **«سیرت۲» (sirat2, medu.ir)** using «ورود با دولت من». About 2 million children were expected in 1404–05.
  - The Organisation says its comprehensive centres («مراکز جامع») would pay more attention to learning disorders and slow learning. [opened: YJC/ISNA, 11 Mehr 1404 ≈ 3 Oct 2025, https://www.yjc.ir/fa/amp/news/9009344]
  - An earlier report (2020) lists the aim of identifying **seven groups**, including **«مشکلات ویژه یادگیری»**. It also says children flagged at about 2,200 base sites go to about 800 specialist sites. [opened: SNN, 15 Tir 1399, https://snn.ir/fa/news/859653]
  - A 2015-era report lists vision, colour vision, hearing, **speech**, readiness, BMI, dental and autism screening. [search]
  - Preschoolers may take part voluntarily. [search]
  - **Limitation:** at age 6 the screening can only flag readiness or risk. Dyslexia is usually identified only after the child has had reading teaching.
- **Learning-disability centres** («مراکز اختلالات یادگیری» / «مراکز مشکلات ویژه یادگیری»): province-level centres under education departments. Teacher referral is a usual route. [opened: Ardabil study; search: Isfahan teacher-referral study] Whether they are free, how a parent self-refers, and waiting times were **not confirmed**. [gap] Believed to be run by آموزش و پرورش استثنایی, offering sessions outside school hours, often free or low-cost. [recall, unverified]
- **Copy direction:** "Start with your child's teacher. Ask whether a referral to your area's learning-difficulties centre (مرکز اختلالات یادگیری آموزش و پرورش) makes sense. Have hearing and vision checked too." Do not name fees or timelines.

**Copy direction (dyslexia):** Lead with "reading is hard for this child despite good teaching". Make clear it's common, not about intelligence, and not laziness. Structured sound–letter teaching helps. Keep enjoying books together. Ask the teacher early. Get hearing and vision checked. Avoid quick fixes.

**Don't say:**
- "Dyslexia means seeing letters backwards." Reversals are common in all early readers (round 4).
- "Coloured glasses/overlays or eye exercises treat dyslexia."
- "This app/checklist can tell if your child is dyslexic."
- "Dyslexic children are especially creative / gifted."
- "Try harder / they're lazy / they'll catch up if you push."
- Any prevalence figure without saying it depends on the test (and never the 20% figure).
- "Persian is phonetic, so dyslexia is rare in Persian."

## 3. When to ask about attention (school age; short)

- **What a diagnosis requires (DSM-5, APA plain-language summary):** at least **6 symptoms** of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, present for **at least 6 months**, with **some before age 12**, causing real difficulty at home, at school or with friends. The page states the "more than one setting" requirement for adolescents and adults. The DSM-5 criterion for children also requires several symptoms in **two or more settings**. [opened: APA "What is ADHD", Oct 2025, https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/adhd/what-is-adhd; children's two-settings wording: recall, unverified, check DSM-5-TR] Learning disorders, anxiety, mood problems, sleep problems, thyroid conditions and some medicines can look similar. [opened: APA; CDC "ADHD signs and symptoms", updated Jun 2026]
- **No single test:** a paediatrician, psychiatrist or psychologist assesses through a multi-step process. [opened: CDC "About ADHD", updated 21 Oct 2026, https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/about/index.html] The AAP guideline (2019) is the main US clinical guideline. Its abstract does not spell out the multi-informant requirement. [opened abstract: Wolraich et al., *Pediatrics* 2019, PMID 31570648] Getting information from parents and teachers is standard practice. [recall, unverified]
- **Sugar:** a meta-analysis of 23 double-blind placebo studies found that sugar **does not affect** children's behaviour or cognition. [opened: Wolraich et al., *JAMA* 1995, PMID 7474248]
- **Parenting:**
  - NHS Trust and university sources say ADHD "is certainly not caused by bad parenting". [search: Berkshire Healthcare NHS FT blog 2020; Derbyshire Healthcare NHS FT slides; NCMH Cardiff]
  - Genes play an "important role". [opened: CDC]
  - **Nuance:** CDC's 2026 page lists "parental mental health and family environment" among possible risk factors, alongside lead, and alcohol or tobacco in pregnancy. [opened] Copy should say that ADHD is not caused by bad parenting, and that family stress can make any child's attention and behaviour harder. It should not say "the family has nothing to do with it".
- **Iran:** a meta-analysis of 9 questionnaire-based studies estimated **11.2%** among primary pupils. Results varied widely by tool, and questionnaires overestimate compared with clinical diagnosis. [opened: Tabibi et al., *Child Psychiatry Hum Dev* 2025, PMID 40810952] APA gives about 8.4% of children. [opened]
- **Signs that justify asking (not a checklist):** the teacher and the parent *both* notice, over months, that the child can't sustain attention on tasks they can actually do, loses things constantly, can't wait their turn, or is always on the go, and this is causing real trouble with learning or friendships. Most of these behaviours are normal at times in all children (CDC lists daydreaming, fidgeting, talking a lot, losing things). [opened: CDC]
- **Link to the existing ADHD-and-active-play guide** rather than repeating it.

**Copy direction:** "If you and the teacher are both seeing this, over months and in more than one place, it's reasonable to ask your child's doctor. Only a professional can assess it." **Don't say:** "Sugar makes kids hyper"; "it's bad parenting"; "every active child has ADHD"; or anything that scores the child.

## 4. Recommended outlines

### Guide A — "Stuttering: what's typical and how to help" (ages ~2–8)
1. **What you might notice:** bumpy speech that is common at 2–5 vs stuttering signs (repeated sounds, stretched sounds, getting stuck), with 2–3 examples. It can start suddenly and come and go. (CHSA, SFA)
2. **You didn't cause it:** about 1 in 10 young children stutter for a while (ELVS). It is linked to how the brain organises speech and often runs in families. Not caused by fright, parenting, or two languages. (STAMMA, Reilly)
3. **Will it go away?** Many children stop, often over a few years. No one can predict which ones will. Getting help doesn't have to wait. (Yairi, Kefalianos, STAMMA)
4. **How to help every day:** slow, unhurried talk; wait; no finishing words; fewer questions; listen to the message; 5 minutes of special time; turn-taking; rest. (SFA, STAMMA, MPC)
5. **What to say:**
   - "Take your time. I'm listening." / «عجله‌ای نیست، من گوش می‌دم.»
   - "That word was a bit sticky. That's OK." / «این کلمه یه کم گیر کرد، اشکالی نداره.»
   - "Lots of people get stuck on words sometimes." / «خیلی‌ها گاهی سر کلمه‌ها گیر می‌کنن.»
   - "I loved hearing about your drawing." (praise the message, not the fluency) / «چه جالب تعریف کردی!»
   - Avoid: "Slow down", "Take a breath", "Say it again properly" / «آروم‌تر بگو»، «نفس بکش»، «درست بگو».
6. **When to see a speech therapist:** reasons to ask (6–12+ months, started after 3½, tension or struggle, avoiding talking, upset, family history, other speech or language difficulties, or simply worried). In Iran: «گفتاردرمانگر». Ask for an assessment in both languages if the child is bilingual.
7. **What therapy looks like:** parents are usually involved; programmes have good evidence; follow your therapist's instructions. (Jones 2005, RESTART 2015)
8. **At preschool and school:** tell the teacher; a teasing plan; the child can choose whether to explain stuttering to classmates. Child script: "I stutter sometimes. Just wait, I'll finish." / «من گاهی لکنت دارم. صبر کن، حرفمو تموم می‌کنم.»
9. **Talking with grandparents and relatives:** a short respectful note on fright/imitation beliefs.

### Guide B — "When learning is hard: dyslexia and other learning differences" (ages ~5–12)
1. **What a learning difference is:** reading, spelling, writing or maths stays much harder than for classmates despite good teaching. Common (roughly 1 in 20 to 1 in 10). Not about intelligence or effort. (IDA, NHS, APA)
2. **Signs worth noticing,** by age: preschool sound-play, letter and naming difficulty, family history; school-age reading, spelling, sequencing, avoidance, distress. Persian note on short-vowel marks disappearing. Present these as "reasons to talk to the teacher", never as a score.
3. **First steps:** talk to the teacher; check hearing and vision; in Iran, ask about the learning-difficulties centre; use the school-entry screening (سیرت۲) for 6-year-olds.
4. **What helps:** structured, explicit teaching of sounds and letters, little and often; keep reading **to** them; audiobooks; praise effort and strategies.
5. **What doesn't:** coloured lenses/overlays, eye exercises, brain-training apps, "miracle" programmes.
6. **Feelings:** protect confidence. Scripts:
   - "Reading is hard work for your brain, and you're not stupid. We'll find what helps." / «خوندن برای مغز تو کار سختیه، نه این‌که تو باهوش نیستی. با هم راهش رو پیدا می‌کنیم.»
   - "Let's share it: I'll read a page, you read a line." / «بیا نوبتی بخونیم: من یه صفحه، تو یه خط.»
   - To the teacher: "I've noticed reading is much harder for her than for her classmates. Could we look at this together?" / «احساس می‌کنم خوندن برای دخترم خیلی سخت‌تر از هم‌کلاسی‌هاشه. می‌شه با هم بررسی کنیم؟»
7. **Strengths, honestly:** find what they enjoy and are good at. Don't promise superpowers.
8. **Short box: "When to ask about attention".** Both home and school notice it, it has lasted months, and it's affecting learning or friendships → ask the doctor. Not caused by sugar or bad parenting. Link to the ADHD-and-active-play guide.

## 5. Daily tips (one sentence each, ≤30 words)

1. When your child gets stuck on a word, wait calmly and keep looking at them kindly; they'll finish, and you won't need to help. (STAMMA, SFA)
2. Swap "slow down" for slowing down yourself: speak a little slower and pause before you answer. (SFA; STAMMA)
3. Try five minutes of special play today: your child picks, you follow, phones away, full attention. (MPC)
4. Ask one question, then wait; comment on what your child says more than you quiz them. (SFA; STAMMA)
5. Praise what your child told you, not how smoothly they said it. (STAMMA; MPC)
6. If your child notices a stutter, say it calmly, "That word was sticky, that's OK", then carry on. (MPC; STAMMA)
7. Stuttering for over six months, struggling, or seeming upset? Ask a speech therapist; you don't have to wait and see. (CHSA/ASHA; MPC)
8. Speaking two languages doesn't cause stuttering, so keep using your home language. (STAMMA; Gahl 2023)
9. Play a sound game: "What sound does باران start with?" Hearing sounds in words is the base of reading. (WWC 2016)
10. Keep reading aloud to your child even after they can read, so stories and new words keep growing. (IDA 2025; round 4)
11. If reading stays much harder than for classmates, talk to the teacher early; early help works best. (IDA 2025)
12. Coloured overlays and eye exercises don't treat dyslexia, but a normal eye and hearing check is still worth doing. (AAP/AAO 2011; NHS)
13. Struggling to read isn't laziness: tell your child their brain is working hard, and notice their effort. (IDA 2025; NHS)
14. Sugar doesn't cause hyperactivity; if attention worries you at home and school for months, talk to a doctor. (Wolraich 1995; APA)
