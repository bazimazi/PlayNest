**Evidence scale** (as in rounds 3–4):
- **Strong:** replicated RCTs or meta-analyses.
- **Moderate:** several cohorts or small RCTs.
- **Weak:** single correlational studies.
- **Contested:** good studies disagree.

## PART A — What decades of research say matters most

### 1.1 Dunedin (New Zealand): childhood self-control and adult life

**Key findings**
- **Sample:** about 1,000 children followed from birth to age 32. [opened]
- **Self-control predicts adult outcomes:** self-control in childhood predicted adult physical health, substance dependence, personal finances and criminal offending, in a **gradient**. Each step up in self-control went with better outcomes; there was no single cut-off. [opened]
- **Not just IQ or class:** the effects were separable from intelligence and social class, and from mistakes made in adolescence. [opened]
- **Sibling check:** in a second cohort of 500 sibling pairs (the UK E-Risk study), the sibling with lower self-control did worse despite the shared family background. [opened]
- **The authors' framing:** programmes that build self-control "might" reduce societal costs. This is a hypothesis, not a tested intervention result. [opened]
- **Change over time:** the paper also reported that children whose self-control improved over childhood did better. [recall, unverified — not in the abstract]

**Source:** Moffitt TE et al. "A gradient of childhood self-control predicts health, wealth, and public safety." *PNAS* 2011;108(7):2693–8. PMID 21262822, doi:10.1073/pnas.1010076108, PMC3041102.

**Evidence:** **Moderate–strong** (large prospective cohort with a sibling replication, but observational).

**Copy direction**
- "One of the world's longest studies followed 1,000 New Zealand children into their thirties. Self-control in childhood (waiting, planning, managing frustration) was linked with better health, money and life outcomes as adults, even after allowing for IQ and family wealth."
- "Self-control grows with practice. Games like 'Simon says', taking turns and waiting for a timer are everyday practice." Tie this to the games already in the app, and frame it as practice, not as proven to change adult outcomes.

**Don't say**
- "A child's self-control at age 3 decides their future."
- "These games will raise your child's adult income."
- Don't label a child as "low self-control". It's a gradient, and children change.

### 1.2 UK Millennium Cohort: regular bedtimes

**Key findings**
- **Behaviour (Kelly, Kelly & Sacker 2013, *Pediatrics*):**
  - **Sample:** 10,230 seven-year-olds, with bedtimes recorded at ages 3, 5 and 7. [opened]
  - **Irregular bedtimes:** linked with more behavioural difficulties, rated by both mothers and teachers. [opened]
  - **Dose response:** the more ages at which bedtimes were irregular, the worse the scores. [opened]
  - **Change helped:** children who **moved from irregular to regular** bedtimes showed clear improvements in behaviour scores (difference-in-differences). Children who moved the other way got worse. [opened]
  - This within-child change is stronger evidence than a simple cross-sectional link, but it is still not an RCT.
- **Cognition (Kelly, Kelly & Sacker 2013, *J Epidemiol Community Health*):**
  - **Sample:** 11,178 seven-year-olds. [opened]
  - **At age 3:** irregular bedtimes were independently linked with lower reading, maths and spatial scores at 7, in both girls and boys. [opened]
  - **At age 7:** the link was seen in girls but not boys. Effects were cumulative. [opened]
- **Routines at age 3 (Anderson et al. 2017, *Int J Obesity*):**
  - **How common routines were:** 41% always had a regular bedtime and 47% a regular mealtime. [opened]
  - **Self-regulation:** regular bedtimes, regular mealtimes and TV limits at 3 were each linked with better emotional self-regulation. [opened]
  - **Obesity:** an irregular bedtime at 3 independently predicted obesity at age 11 (OR 1.87). [opened]

**Sources**
- Kelly Y, Kelly J, Sacker A. "Changes in bedtime schedules and behavioral difficulties in 7 year old children." *Pediatrics* 2013;132(5):e1184–93. PMID 24127471, doi:10.1542/peds.2013-1906.
- Kelly Y, Kelly J, Sacker A. "Time for bed: associations with cognitive performance in 7-year-old children." *JECH* 2013;67(11):926–31. PMID 23835763, PMC3812865.
- Anderson SE et al. *Int J Obes* 2017. PMID 28435162, PMC5626576.

**Evidence:** **Moderate.** Large national cohort with a within-child change analysis.

**Copy direction**
- "A big UK study of over 10,000 children found that regular bedtimes were linked with calmer behaviour and better school-type skills at age 7."
- "When families moved to a regular bedtime, children's behaviour improved." The point to make is that "regular" matters, not an exact clock time.
- "It's never too late. The improvements showed up when routines changed, too."

**Don't say**
- "Irregular bedtimes damage the brain."
- "Late bedtimes lower IQ."
- Don't set an exact bedtime as a rule. Iranian family schedules often run late: the message is **consistency**, not 7 pm.

### 1.3 Breastfeeding and IQ: Pelotas (Brazil) vs PROBIT (Belarus) vs sibling studies

**Key findings**
- **Pelotas 1982 cohort (Victora et al. 2015, *Lancet Global Health*):**
  - **Sample:** 3,493 adults assessed at age 30. [opened]
  - **Results:** after adjusting for 10 confounders, people breastfed for ≥12 months vs <1 month had, on average:
    - 3.76 more IQ points (95% CI 2.20–5.33)
    - 0.91 more years of schooling
    - higher income [opened]
  - **Why the authors trusted it:** they chose Pelotas because breastfeeding there in 1982 was **not strongly socially patterned**, which reduces confounding. [opened]
  - **Evidence type:** still observational.
- **PROBIT (Kramer et al. 2008, *Arch Gen Psychiatry*):**
  - **Design:** cluster-RCT in 31 Belarusian hospitals; 17,046 infants enrolled and 13,889 followed at age 6.5. [opened]
  - **Did promotion work?** Exclusive breastfeeding at 3 months was 43.3% vs 6.4%. [opened]
  - **IQ differences:** verbal IQ +7.5 (95% CI 0.8 to 14.3). Full-scale IQ +5.9, but its CI crossed zero (−1.0 to +12.8). [opened]
  - **Teacher ratings:** higher for reading and writing. [opened]
  - **Caveat:** testers were not blinded. [recall, unverified — widely discussed]
- **PROBIT at age 16 (Yang et al. 2018, *PLoS Med*):**
  - **Sample:** 13,557 followed up, with computerised self-administered tests. [opened]
  - **Main analysis (intention to treat):** **no significant differences**. [opened]
  - **Extra adjustment:** a small verbal advantage of 1.4 points. [opened]
  - **Authors' conclusion:** "no benefit of a breastfeeding promotion intervention on overall neurocognitive function"; the only benefit was on verbal function. [opened]
- **Sibling and maternal-IQ studies:**
  - **Der, Batty & Deary 2006 (*BMJ*):** the mother's IQ predicted breastfeeding more strongly than race, education or poverty. An unadjusted gap of about 4 IQ points shrank to 0.52, which was non-significant, after adjusting for confounders; sibling pairs and a meta-analysis agreed. [opened]
  - **Colen & Ramey 2014 (US NLSY sibling comparisons):** breastfed children did better on 10 of 11 outcomes in standard models. Within families, the estimates "dramatically decrease" and lose significance on all but one. [opened]
- **Overall:** breastfeeding has clear **short-term** infection and nutrition benefits. Long-term IQ and income effects are **contested**: likely small, possibly near zero, and heavily confounded in high-income settings.
- **Recent synthesis:** a 2026 narrative review (Panteris et al., *Children*) reached the same conclusion. Adult associations are generally small and should be "interpreted cautiously". [opened]

**Sources**
- Victora CG et al. *Lancet Glob Health* 2015;3(4):e199–205. PMID 25794674, PMC4365917.
- Kramer MS et al. *Arch Gen Psychiatry* 2008;65(5):578–84. PMID 18458209.
- Yang S et al. *PLoS Med* 2018;15(4):e1002554. PMID 29677187, PMC5909901.
- Der G, Batty GD, Deary IJ. *BMJ* 2006;333:945. PMID 17020911, PMC1633819.
- Colen CG, Ramey DM. *Soc Sci Med* 2014;109:55–65. PMID 24698713, PMC4077166.

**Evidence:** **Contested** for IQ and income. Short-term health benefits are **strong** [recall: WHO/UNICEF].

**Copy direction**
- "Breastfeeding has real benefits, especially protection from infections in the early months. Whether it raises IQ in the long run is honestly debated. Families who breastfeed often differ in other ways, and when researchers compare siblings, the IQ gap mostly disappears."
- "However your baby is fed, the talking, holding and play during feeds is something every parent can give."

**Don't say**
- "Breastfed babies are smarter" or "earn more."
- Anything that shames formula feeding or mothers who couldn't breastfeed.
- Don't dismiss breastfeeding either. Islamic and Iranian tradition values breastfeeding highly (the Qur'an mentions two years). The app should support it warmly without inflating the IQ claims, and should make no religious ruling.

### 1.4 NICHD Study of Early Child Care: quality vs hours

**Key findings**
- **To age 12 (Belsky et al. 2007, *Child Development*):**
  - **Sample:** 1,364 children followed to grade 6. [opened]
  - **Parenting came first:** parenting was a "stronger and more consistent predictor" of development than childcare experience. [opened]
  - **Quality:** higher-quality care predicted higher vocabulary. [opened]
  - **Centre hours:** more exposure to centre care predicted more teacher-reported externalizing (acting-out) problems. The effects were small. [opened]
- **To age 15 (Vandell et al. 2010):**
  - **Quality:** higher-quality care predicted higher achievement, with escalating benefits at higher quality, and youth reports of less externalizing. [opened]
  - **Hours:** more hours of non-relative care predicted slightly more risk-taking and impulsivity. [opened]

**Sources**
- Belsky J et al. *Child Dev* 2007;78(2):681–701. PMID 17381797.
- Vandell DL et al. *Child Dev* 2010;81(3):737–56. PMID 20573102, PMC2938040.

**Evidence:** **Moderate.** Observational and US-only, from the 1990s.

**Copy direction**
- "In the largest US study of childcare, what happened at home mattered more than childcare did. Good-quality care was linked with slightly better language and school results, while very long hours in group care were linked with slightly more restless behaviour. The effects were small either way."
- "If you use childcare, look for warm, responsive carers and small groups. Your relationship at home stays the biggest influence."

**Don't say**
- "Daycare harms children" or "working mothers harm children."
- "Childcare makes children smarter."
- Avoid guilt. The app speaks to both employed and at-home parents.

### 1.5 Bucharest Early Intervention Project (BEIP): family care and timing

**Key findings**
- **Design:** randomised trial. Abandoned children living in Romanian institutions were assigned either to stay or to move to foster care. [opened]
- **Cognition:** children who stayed scored "markedly below" never-institutionalised children and those placed in foster care. The gains were **largest for those placed youngest**, which suggests a possible sensitive period. [opened]
- **Attachment at 42 months (Smyke 2010):** foster-care children had much better attachment. Children placed **before 24 months** were more likely to be securely attached. [opened]

**Sources**
- Nelson CA et al. *Science* 2007;318(5858):1937–40. PMID 18096809.
- Smyke AT et al. *Child Dev* 2010;81(1):212–23. PMID 20331663, PMC4098033.

**Evidence:** **Strong** (randomised), for the specific case of severe deprivation.

**Copy direction**
- "A rare randomised study in Romania showed that young children moved from orphanages into caring families caught up a great deal, especially when moved early. Children need a reliable, loving person, and the early years are a powerful time for that."
- Useful for kinship care, adoption and fostering families, and to stress that recovery is possible.

**Don't say**
- Don't apply this to ordinary families: "if you go back to work before 2 your child won't attach." BEIP concerns severe institutional deprivation.
- "After age 2 it's too late." Later-placed children also improved.

### 1.6 Other cohorts (brief or skipped)

- **ALSPAC (UK), Growing Up in Australia (LSAC) and Growing Up in New Zealand:** no source opened this round. Skip them in copy, or research them in a later round.
- **Pelotas:** see §1.3.

## PART B — Landmark trials

### 2.1 Jamaica home-visit play stimulation (the origin of Reach Up)

**Key findings**
- **Design:** RCT, 1986–87, with stunted toddlers aged 9–24 months. Community health workers made weekly home visits for 2 years, coaching mothers to play and talk with their child. [opened]
- **Age ~22 (Gertler et al. 2014, *Science*):** 105 of 129 participants were followed up. Stimulation **raised earnings by 25%**, enough to catch up with a non-stunted comparison group. [opened]
- **Age 31, psychological outcomes (Walker et al. 2022, *JCPP*):** the stimulation group, compared with no treatment, had:
  - higher IQ (Hedges g 0.57) and cognitive flexibility (0.61)
  - fewer depressive symptoms (0.61)
  - more grit and conscientiousness
  - less substance use and risk-taking
  - in total, 18 significant outcomes out of 33 [opened]
  - **But:** they still had lower IQ than the non-stunted comparison group. [opened]
- **Age 31 earnings (NBER w29292, 2021):** +37% earnings and +43% hourly wages. [search]
- **Wider evidence:** a 2023 meta-analysis of Reach Up RCTs is in round 3's notes (Jervis et al., *Pediatrics*).

**Sources**
- Gertler P et al. *Science* 2014;344(6187):998–1001. PMID 24876490, PMC4574862.
- Walker SP et al. *JCPP* 2022;63(6):626–35. PMID 34403137, PMC8850528.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w29292

**Evidence:** **Strong design but small sample** (about 100 children).

**Copy direction**
- "In Jamaica, a health worker visited once a week for two years, showing mothers simple ways to play and talk with their toddlers. Thirty years later, those children were earning more and doing better in many areas of life. The visits were about everyday play, not expensive toys."

**Don't say**
- Don't borrow the effect size for the app ("PlayNest games raise earnings 25%"). This was intensive in-person coaching for stunted children in poverty.
- Don't say "small sample means it's proven". Say "a small but carefully followed trial".

### 2.2 Perry Preschool and Abecedarian (brief)

**Key findings**
- **Perry Preschool:** 123 low-income 3–4-year-olds in Michigan, randomly assigned. HighScope reports that by age 40 the programme group had higher employment and fewer repeat arrests. [search] Figures vary between HighScope pages; check the 2005 *Lifetime Effects* monograph before quoting any.
- **Abecedarian (Campbell et al. 2014, *Science*):** children randomly assigned to intensive early care had fewer cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors in their mid-30s, especially males (control males' mean systolic BP 143 vs 126 mmHg). [opened]
- **Limits:**
  - **Small samples:** both are small (about 100 children each). [recall, unverified]
  - **Different setting:** both are US programmes from the 1960s–70s, where the comparison group had little else.

**Sources**
- Campbell F et al. *Science* 2014;343(6178):1478–85. PMID 24675955, PMC4028126.
- https://highscope.org/project/perry-preschool-study/

**Copy direction**
- "A few small, very long studies in the US found that high-quality early education for children growing up in poverty was linked with better adult health, jobs and less crime decades later."
- Use this only as a background "why early years matter" line.

**Don't say**
- "Preschool guarantees success."
- Don't quote the cost-benefit ratios. They are model-dependent and widely debated.

### 2.3 Nurse-Family Partnership (US) vs Family Nurse Partnership "Building Blocks" (England)

**Key findings**
- **US Elmira trial (Olds et al. 1997, *JAMA*):**
  - **Visits:** an average of 9 nurse visits in pregnancy and 23 to age 2. [opened]
  - **Abuse and neglect:** at the 15-year follow-up, nurse-visited mothers had fewer verified child-abuse and neglect reports (0.29 vs 0.54). [opened]
  - **Low-income unmarried mothers:** also had fewer arrests, less welfare use and longer spacing between births. [opened]
- **England, Building Blocks (Robling et al. 2016, *Lancet*):**
  - **Design:** RCT with 1,645 first-time mothers aged ≤19, offered up to 64 visits. [opened]
  - **Results:** **no benefit** on any primary outcome: smoking in pregnancy (56% vs 56%), birthweight, a second pregnancy within 2 years (66% vs 66%), and emergency attendances. [opened]
  - **Authors' conclusion:** "programme continuation is not justified on the basis of available evidence". [opened]
- **What this teaches:** a programme that worked in one place and time may add nothing where usual services are already good. The UK has universal health visiting and the NHS. Context and the comparison group matter. [My interpretation; consistent with published commentary — recall, unverified]

**Sources**
- Olds DL et al. *JAMA* 1997;278(8):637–43. PMID 9272895.
- Robling M et al. *Lancet* 2016;387(10014):146–55. PMID 26474809, PMC4707160.

**Evidence:** **Strong** for both results (RCTs). Together they show **context dependence**.

**Copy direction**
- This belongs in a "how to read research" box: "Even well-known programmes don't work everywhere. A famous US home-visiting programme helped families in the 1970s, but when England tested it in 2009–2014 alongside its existing health services, it added no measurable benefit. Good science tests things again, in new places."
- This is a respectful way to explain why PlayNest says "linked with" and avoids hype.

**Don't say**
- "Home visiting doesn't work." Many programmes do; the Jamaica trial is one.
- "The US study was wrong."

### 2.4 Video Interaction Project (VIP) and Reach Out and Read

**Key findings**
- **VIP (Mendelsohn et al. 2018, *Pediatrics*):**
  - **What it is:** an RCT delivered through paediatric primary care in New York. A facilitator video-recorded the parent reading or playing with the child, then reviewed the video together to reinforce positive moments. [opened]
  - **Behaviour at 4.5 years:** both phases (0–3 and 3–5) improved behaviour by d ≈ 0.25–0.30. The combined programme reduced hyperactivity by d = 0.63. [opened]
  - **Lasting effect:** the 0–3 effects lasted 1.5 years after the programme ended. [opened]
- **Reach Out and Read** (doctors give books and advice on reading aloud at check-ups): linked with more reading aloud at home and better vocabulary, in mostly non-randomised studies. [recall, unverified — no source opened]

**Source:** Mendelsohn AL et al. *Pediatrics* 2018;141(5):e20173393. PMID 29632254, PMC5914489.

**Evidence:** **Moderate–strong** for VIP (RCT). **Weak–moderate** for Reach Out and Read.

**Copy direction**
- "In a New York trial, parents who were shown short videos of themselves reading and playing with their child, with a coach pointing out what went well, had children with fewer behaviour problems at school entry."
- This supports PlayNest's tone of noticing what's going well. It also supports an optional feature idea: parents watch a short clip of themselves playing and note one good moment (private and on-device).

**Don't say**
- "Reading aloud prevents ADHD."
- "Reading aloud cures behaviour problems."

### 2.5 Conversational turns vs the "30 million word gap"

**Key findings**
- **Hart & Risley (1995):** claimed that low-income children hear about 30 million fewer words by age 4. [recall, unverified]
- **Sperry, Sperry & Miller 2019 (*Child Development*):**
  - **Sample:** 42 children in 5 US communities. [opened]
  - **Results:** did **not** support the 30-million claim. They found substantial variation within each income group, and said that counting only the primary caregiver undercounts the words low-income children hear from others. [opened]
- **Golinkoff, Hoff, Rowe, Tamis-LeMonda & Hirsh-Pasek 2019 (reply):** the gap should not be abandoned. **Child-directed** quality speech is the key ingredient, not overheard speech. [opened]
- **Romeo et al. 2018 (*Psychological Science*):**
  - **Sample:** 36 children aged 4–6. [opened]
  - **Results:** more **conversational turns** with adults predicted more activity in Broca's area during a story task. This was independent of family income, IQ and the number of adult words alone. [opened]
  - **Interpretation:** the authors concluded that conversation matters "over and above" sheer word quantity. [opened]
  - **Caveat:** the sample is small and correlational.
- **Synthesis:** both sides agree that **back-and-forth talk directed at the child** matters more than raw word counts. The open dispute is about the size of income-related gaps.

**Sources**
- Romeo RR et al. *Psychol Sci* 2018;29(5):700–10. PMID 29442613, PMC5945324.
- Sperry DE et al. *Child Dev* 2019;90(4):1303–18. PMID 29707767.
- Golinkoff RM et al. *Child Dev* 2019;90(3):985–92. PMID 30102419, PMC10370358.

**Evidence:** **Moderate** for conversation mattering. The **exact size of the "word gap" is contested**.

**Copy direction**
- "It isn't about how many words your child hears. It's about the back-and-forth. When you talk *with* your child, wait for their reply (even a babble or a point) and answer it, you are giving their brain language practice."
- "Every family and every language counts. Grandparents, siblings and aunts talking with your child all add up." This fits extended Iranian families well.

**Don't say**
- "Poor children hear 30 million fewer words."
- Don't make word counts a target, or imply that lower-income or less-educated parents are deficient.
- Don't say "conversational turns build Broca's area". The study shows a correlation.

### 2.6 Parenting Across Cultures (Lansford et al.): warmth helps everywhere, harsh discipline harms everywhere

**Key findings**
- **Physical discipline in 6 countries (Lansford et al. 2005, *Child Development*):**
  - **Sample:** 336 mother–child pairs in China, India, Italy, Kenya, the Philippines and Thailand. [opened]
  - **Normativeness:** physical discipline was **less strongly** linked with problems where it was seen as normal, **but** was still linked with more adverse outcomes regardless. [opened]
  - **In every country:** "in all countries higher use of physical discipline was associated with more aggression and anxiety". [opened]
- **Warmth across 9 countries (12 cultural groups):**
  - **Internalizing (Rothenberg et al. 2020, *JCPP*):** across ages 8–12, parental warmth was linked with internalizing problems more consistently than behavioural control was. [opened]
  - **Prosocial behaviour (Pastorelli et al. 2021):** in most of 11 cultural groups, more warmth at ages 9–10 predicted steeper increases in prosocial behaviour. [opened]
  - **Positive youth development (Bornstein et al. 2026):** warmth was linked with higher positive development in all three country-income groups. Control showed varied effects. [opened]
  - **Included countries:** these samples include **Jordan**, the closest Middle-Eastern comparison. Iran was not included. [opened, sample lists]
- **Spanking meta-analysis (Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor 2016):**
  - **Size:** 111 effect sizes and 160,927 children. [opened]
  - **Results:** 13 of 17 mean effects were significant, and **all** pointed to detrimental outcomes. Effect sizes did not differ much between spanking and physical abuse, or by study design. [opened]

**Sources**
- Lansford JE et al. *Child Dev* 2005;76(6):1234–46. PMID 16274437, PMC2766084.
- Rothenberg WA et al. *JCPP* 2020. PMID 31667849, PMC7078039.
- Pastorelli C et al. *J Youth Dev* 2021. PMID 34888590, PMC8651236.
- Bornstein MH et al. *J Res Adolesc* 2026. PMID 42083293, PMC13139747.
- Gershoff ET, Grogan-Kaylor A. *J Fam Psychol* 2016;30(4):453–69. PMID 27055181, PMC7992110.

**Evidence:** **Strong** for physical punishment being linked with worse outcomes. Mostly observational, but the finding is consistent across designs and cultures. **Moderate–strong** for warmth across cultures.

**Copy direction**
- "Researchers studied families in China, Colombia, Italy, Jordan, Kenya, the Philippines, Sweden, Thailand and the US. Everywhere, children whose parents were warm and affectionate did better. And in every country studied, more physical punishment was linked with more aggression and anxiety, even where smacking was common."
- Persian framing: respect the parent's intent ("Most parents who smack do it because they want a well-behaved child"), then offer what works: warmth plus clear limits. Make no religious ruling.

**Don't say**
- "Parents who smack are abusers."
- "Smacking causes [X]." Say "is linked with". There are no RCTs of spanking, for ethical reasons.
- Don't present Western culture as superior. The finding holds across non-Western samples.

## PART C — Myths, checked

Verdict scale: **Myth**, **Mostly myth**, **Half-true** or **Debated**.

### 3.1 "Sugar makes children hyperactive"
**Verdict:** Myth (for behaviour). There are other good reasons to limit sugar.

**Evidence**
- **Wolraich, Wilson & White 1995 (*JAMA*), meta-analysis:**
  - **Studies:** 16 reports and 23 double-blind, placebo-controlled studies. [opened]
  - **Results:** the 95% CI included zero for all 14 outcome measures. [opened]
  - **Conclusion:** sugar "does not affect the behavior or cognitive performance of children". A small effect, or effects in subsets of children, "cannot be ruled out". [opened]
- **Hoover & Milich 1994:**
  - **Design:** 35 boys whose mothers called them "sugar sensitive". **All** were given placebo. [opened]
  - **Results:** mothers told their son had had sugar rated him **more hyperactive**, and were more controlling and critical during play. [opened]

**Sources:** PMID 7474248; PMID 7963081.

**Copy direction:** "Careful studies where nobody knew who had sugar found no change in children's behaviour. Parties are exciting because they're parties. In one study, mothers who *thought* their son had sugar saw him as wilder, even though he hadn't had any. Limit sugar for teeth and health, not behaviour."

**Don't say:** "Sugar is harmless." Mention dental decay and diet quality.

### 3.2 "The Mozart effect: classical music makes babies smarter"
**Verdict:** Myth.

**Evidence**
- **Pietschnig, Voracek & Formann 2010 (*Intelligence*), meta-analysis:**
  - **Studies:** about 40 studies with over 3,000 people. [search]
  - **Results:** small effects, inflated by publication bias and by the original lab's results. [search]
  - **Conclusion:** little evidence for a specific Mozart effect. [search]
- **Chabris 1999 (*Nature*):** a meta-analysis finding any effect was small and likely due to arousal or enjoyment. [recall, unverified; PMID 10476958 opened, no abstract]
- **Original study:** concerned **adult** students and a brief spatial task, not babies. [recall, unverified]

**Copy direction:** "Playing music to babies doesn't raise IQ. But singing, dancing and music together are wonderful for bonding, rhythm and language, and the lullabies section is here for that."

**Don't say:** "Music is useless."

### 3.3 "Baby videos and flashcards teach words or early reading"
**Verdict:** Myth.

**Evidence**
- **DeLoache et al. 2010 (*Psychological Science*):**
  - **Design:** 12–18-month-olds watched a popular word-learning DVD several times a week for 4 weeks. [opened]
  - **Results:** they learned **no more words** than controls. The most learning happened when parents taught the words in everyday activities **without** the video. [opened]
  - **Parents' view:** parents who liked the DVD overestimated what their child had learned. [opened]
- **Neuman et al. 2014 (*J Educ Psychol*):**
  - **Design:** RCT with 117 infants aged 9–18 months, using a "baby reading" kit (DVDs, flashcards, books) daily for 7 months. [search]
  - **Results:** no difference on 13 of 14 measures, but parents *believed* their child was learning. [search]
- **Zimmerman, Christakis & Meltzoff 2007 (*J Pediatrics*):**
  - **Design:** phone survey of 1,008 parents. [opened]
  - **Results:** among 8–16-month-olds, each daily hour of baby DVDs was **linked with** lower vocabulary scores. There was no association in 17–24-month-olds. [opened]
  - **Caveat:** correlational; the authors call for further research. [opened]

**Sources:** PMID 20855901; doi:10.1037/a0035937; PMID 17889070.

**Copy direction:** "Babies learn words from people, not screens. In a careful study, toddlers learned more words when parents simply named things during the day than from a month of a 'baby genius' DVD. Save your money; talk, point and name."

**Don't say:**
- "Baby videos damage language." That rests on a single correlational survey.
- "Never let a baby see a screen." Video calls with grandparents are a common Iranian diaspora use; the app's screen guidance covers this elsewhere.

### 3.4 "Children have learning styles (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic)"
**Verdict:** Myth, in the sense that matching teaching to a style improves learning.

**Evidence**
- **Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork 2008 (*Psych Sci Public Interest*):**
  - **Scope:** a review commissioned to test the "meshing hypothesis" (that teaching in a learner's preferred style improves learning). [opened]
  - **Findings:** very few studies used a design able to test it, and several that did "flatly contradict" it. [opened]
  - **Conclusion:** there is "no adequate evidence base" for using learning-styles assessments. [opened]
  - **Caveat:** not every version has been tested. [opened]

**Source:** PMID 26162104.

**Copy direction:** "Children do have preferences and interests. But research hasn't found that teaching 'visual learners' only with pictures helps them learn more. Most children learn best from a mix: seeing, hearing, doing and talking about it."

**Don't say:** "Everyone learns the same way." Interests and abilities differ.

### 3.5 "Left-brained vs right-brained children"
**Verdict:** Myth (as a personality type).

**Evidence**
- **Nielsen et al. 2013 (*PLoS ONE*):**
  - **Data:** resting-state brain scans of 1,011 people aged 7–29. [opened]
  - **Results:** specific functions such as language are lateralised, but the data were "not consistent with a whole-brain phenotype" of left-brained or right-brained people. [opened]

**Source:** PMID 23967180, PMC3743825.

**Copy direction:** "Some skills lean more on one side of the brain, but people aren't 'left-brained' or 'right-brained'. Both halves work together all the time. A child can be both creative and logical."

### 3.6 "You'll spoil a baby by picking them up or responding to crying"
**Verdict:** Myth for young babies. There is nuance about mild fussing.

**Evidence**
- **AAP, HealthyChildren.org:**
  - **Updated:** 20 Feb 2025; adapted from *Caring for Your Baby and Young Child*, 8th ed. [opened]
  - **On spoiling:** "You cannot spoil a young baby with attention, and if you answer their calls for help, they'll cry less overall." [opened]
  - **On timing:** "respond promptly during their first few months". [opened]
  - **If a fed, dry, warm baby is tired and still crying:** leaving them in a safe place such as a crib is OK. [opened]
  - **If overwhelmed:** set the baby down safely and get help. Never shake. [opened]
- **van IJzendoorn & Hubbard 2000:**
  - **Design:** 50 Dutch families, over 20 hours of home observation each. [opened]
  - **Results:** they did **not** replicate Bell & Ainsworth 1972 (which found prompt response led to less crying). Mothers who more often left a crying bout unanswered in the first 9 weeks had babies who cried *less often* in the next 9 weeks. The authors suggest "benign neglect" of mild fussing may help babies learn to cope. [opened]
  - **Attachment:** crying did not predict attachment security. [opened]
- **Synthesis:** responding warmly does **not** spoil. Sensitive responsiveness overall is linked with secure attachment [recall, unverified: De Wolff & van IJzendoorn 1997 meta-analysis]. Not leaping at every whimper is also fine.

**Sources:**
- https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/crying-colic/Pages/Responding-To-Your-Babys-Cries.aspx
- van IJzendoorn & Hubbard, *Attach Hum Dev* 2000. PMID 11708224.

**Copy direction:** "You can't spoil a young baby by holding or comforting them. Babies cry to communicate, and answering builds trust. It's also fine to pause a moment when your baby is just fussing, to see if they settle. You don't have to be instant every time."

**Don't say:**
- "Always pick up immediately or you'll harm attachment." That puts guilt on tired parents.
- "Responding quickly makes babies cry less." The studies disagree.

### 3.7 "Sleep training causes lasting emotional harm"
**Verdict:** Not supported by the best long-term trial. It remains optional.

**Evidence**
- **Price et al. 2012 (*Pediatrics*):**
  - **Design:** 5-year follow-up of a cluster-RCT; 326 children whose parents reported sleep problems at 7 months, with 225 families (69%) followed up at age 6. [opened]
  - **Results:** **no differences** in emotional or behavioural problems, stress (cortisol), child–parent closeness or conflict, attachment, or maternal mental health. [opened]
  - **Conclusion:** "no marked long-lasting effects (positive or negative)". [opened]
  - **Caveat:** follow-up was 69%, and the intervention was graduated, not "cry it out" from birth.

**Source:** PMID 22966034. Round 3/4 notes already cover Gradisar 2016.

**Copy direction:** "A large Australian trial followed children for five years after gentle sleep-training methods (like checking in at gradually longer gaps). It found no harm to emotions, behaviour or the parent–child bond, and no lasting benefit either. Whether to use these methods is your family's choice."

**Don't say:**
- "Sleep training is proven safe for all babies."
- "Cry-it-out is harmless from birth." The trial started at 8–10 months.
- Don't push sleep training. Room-sharing and co-sleeping preferences are common in Iran, and the app's safe-sleep rules still apply.

### 3.8 "Teething causes high fever (and diarrhoea)"
**Verdict:** Myth for high fever. Mild symptoms are real.

**Evidence**
- **Macknin et al. 2000 (*Pediatrics*):**
  - **Design:** prospective; 125 infants, 19,422 child-days, 475 tooth eruptions, with daily temperatures. [opened]
  - **Linked with teething:** in the ~8 days around eruption, biting, drooling, gum-rubbing, irritability, wakefulness, ear-rubbing, facial rash, lower appetite for solids and **mild** temperature rise. [opened]
  - **Not linked:** congestion, sleep disturbance, loose stools, cough, vomiting, or fever over 102°F (38.9°C). [opened]
  - **Other findings:** no symptom occurred in more than 35% of teething infants, and no teething child had a fever of 104°F. [opened]
  - **Authors' warning:** "Before caregivers attribute any infants' signs or symptoms of a potentially serious illness to teething, other possible causes must be ruled out." [opened]
- **Massignan et al. 2016 (*Pediatrics*), meta-analysis of 16 studies:**
  - **Common signs:** gum irritation, irritability and drooling were most common. [opened]
  - **Temperature:** may rise, "but it was not characterized as fever". [opened]
- **Parents' beliefs (Pereira 2023, systematic review of 29 studies):** most parents worldwide believe in at least one teething symptom. [opened]

**Sources:** PMID 10742315; PMID 26908659; PMID 37017581.

**Copy direction:** "Teething can make babies drooly, chewy and grumpy for a few days, and slightly warm. It doesn't cause a real fever, diarrhoea or illness. If your baby has a fever or seems unwell, look for another cause and contact a health worker. Don't put it down to teeth."

**Don't say:** Any medicine or gel advice. No doses. Point to the app's existing fever and red-flag content.

### 3.9 "Cold weather or wet hair gives you a cold"
**Verdict:** Half-true. Colds are caused by viruses; one small trial suggests chilling may trigger symptoms in some people.

**Evidence**
- **Johnson & Eccles 2005 (*Family Practice*):**
  - **Design:** 180 adults randomised to a foot chill (feet in cold water) or a control procedure. [opened]
  - **Results:** 13/90 chilled vs 5/90 control participants reported a cold within 4–5 days (P = 0.047). [opened]
  - **Authors' view:** chilling may trigger symptoms in about 10% of people. They called for more research on whether this reflects real infection. [opened]
  - **Caveat:** small, adults, self-reported symptoms.
- **Background:** colds are viral. Winter colds are linked with time indoors together and with virus survival. [recall, unverified]

**Source:** PMID 16286463.

**Copy direction:** "Colds are caused by viruses, which spread person to person, so handwashing does more than a hat. Being chilled *may* make it easier for a cold to show up, so dressing children warmly is sensible, but going outside in cold weather doesn't on its own make children ill. Outdoor play in winter is good for them."
- Persian note: the everyday word سرماخوردگی (literally "catching cold") carries the belief. Acknowledge it gently ("there's some truth in it") rather than dismissing grandparents.

**Don't say:** "Cold has nothing to do with it." The one trial is mildly supportive.

### 3.10 "Baby walkers help babies walk"
**Verdict:** Myth, and walkers cause injuries.

**Evidence**
- **Iran (Shiva, Ghotbi & Yavari 2010, *Singapore Med J*):**
  - **Sample:** 414 infants seen at Iranian clinics. [opened]
  - **Use:** 54.5% used walkers; use was more common in one-child families and among more-educated parents. [opened]
  - **Walking age:** no difference. 78.6% of users vs 85% of non-users were walking by 12 months (P = 0.28). [opened]
  - **Beliefs:** 76.8% of users' parents believed walkers promote walking. [opened]
  - **Injuries:** 14.1% had minor walker injuries. [opened]
- **US (Sims et al. 2018, *Pediatrics*):**
  - **Scale:** about 230,676 children under 15 months were treated in US emergency departments for walker injuries in 1990–2014. [opened]
  - **Pattern:** 90.6% were head or neck injuries, and 74.1% were falls down stairs. [opened]
  - **Admitted children:** 37.8% had skull fractures. [opened]
  - **Policy:** the AAP calls for a US ban. [opened]
- **Canada:** first country to ban the sale, advertising and import of baby walkers, in April 2004, including second-hand ones. [search]
- **Motor development:** some studies suggest walkers may delay walking. [recall, unverified]

**Sources:**
- PMID 20848062; PMID 30224365.
- Canada: NBC News 2004 (https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna4685971) and Health Canada recalls pages. [search]

**Copy direction:** "Walkers don't help babies walk sooner. A study of Iranian babies found no difference in walking age, and walkers cause falls, especially down stairs, which is why Canada banned them. Floor time, cruising along furniture and pushing a sturdy toy are better practice."
- The Iranian data make this especially relevant: over half the infants in that sample used walkers.

**Don't say:** "Walkers cause brain damage." Shame-free wording: "Many loving parents use them; here's what the research found."

### 3.11 "Only children are spoiled, selfish or lonely"
**Verdict:** Mostly myth. Note one contrary Chinese study.

**Evidence**
- **Falbo & Polit 1986 (*Psychological Bulletin*), meta-analysis of 115 studies:** only children were similar to children with siblings on most outcomes, and slightly ahead on achievement and intelligence. [recall, unverified]
- **Falbo & Hooper 2015:**
  - **Data:** 22 Chinese studies using a symptom checklist. [opened]
  - **Results:** small but significant **advantages** for only children, especially those born after the policy. [opened]
  - **Caveat:** convenience samples. [opened]
- **Lin, Falbo et al. 2021:**
  - **Belief:** the "lonely only child" stereotype was common among Chinese young adults. [opened]
  - **Reality:** in three samples, only children reported **less** loneliness than peers with siblings. [opened]
- **Contrary evidence (Cameron et al. 2013, *Science*):**
  - **Design:** economics experiments with 421 Beijing adults born just before or after the one-child policy. [opened]
  - **Results:** those born under the policy were less trusting, less trustworthy, more risk-averse, less competitive, more pessimistic and less conscientious. [opened]
  - **Caveat:** a single study of a policy-era cohort, which may reflect the era as well as sibship.

**Sources:** PMID 25894306; PMID 34166053; PMID 23306438.

**Copy direction:** "Decades of studies find only children are, on average, much like children with siblings, and in some studies a little ahead at school and less lonely. What matters is the same for every child: warmth, limits, and chances to share and play with other children."
- Relevant in Iran, where one-child families are increasingly common in cities. [recall, unverified]

**Don't say:** "Science proves only children are better." Don't ignore the Cameron study; say the findings are mostly reassuring, not unanimous.

### 3.12 "Vaccines (MMR) cause autism"
**Verdict:** Myth.

**Evidence**
- **Hviid et al. 2019 (*Annals of Internal Medicine*):**
  - **Data:** 657,461 Danish children. [opened]
  - **Result:** autism hazard ratio 0.93 (95% CI 0.85–1.02) for vaccinated vs unvaccinated children. [opened]
  - **High-risk groups:** no increased risk in high-risk subgroups, including children with an autistic sibling, and no clustering after vaccination. [opened]
- **Background:** the original 1998 claim was retracted. [recall, unverified]

**Source:** PMID 30831578.

**Copy direction:** "One of the largest studies, covering more than 650,000 Danish children, found no link between the MMR vaccine and autism, including in children with an autistic brother or sister. Follow your national vaccination schedule." For Iran, that is the health network (شبکه بهداشت) schedule.

**Don't say:** Avoid ridiculing worried parents. Keep this brief and calm.

### 3.13 "Quality time beats quantity" / "more mother time is always better"
**Verdict:** Debated.

**Evidence**
- **Milkie, Nomaguchi & Denny 2015 (*Journal of Marriage and Family* 77:355–72):**
  - **Data:** US time diaries; 1,605 children aged 3–11 and 778 adolescents aged 12–18. [search]
  - **Ages 3–11:** the amount of mothers' time was not linked with child outcomes. Mothers' education and income mattered more. [search]
  - **Adolescents:** more engaged time with mothers was linked with fewer delinquent behaviours. More family time with both parents was linked with fewer risky behaviours. [search; recall for the specifics]
  - **Limits:** quality of time was not measured. [search]
  - **Critiques:** JMF commentaries (Waldfogel; Mayer & Kalil) argued the findings are more mixed than the headlines suggest. [search]

**Source:** doi:10.1111/jomf.12170; NICHD press release https://www.nichd.nih.gov/newsroom/releases/042715-time-with-parents [search].

**Copy direction:** "A US study found that the sheer number of hours mothers spent with young children wasn't linked with how the children turned out, and that a stressed, guilty parent doesn't help. What you do together and how you feel counts. Interestingly, time together seemed to matter more in the teen years, so don't step back too soon."
- This is an anti-guilt message that fits the app's ten-minute play ethos.

**Don't say:** "Time with your child doesn't matter." The study didn't measure quality, and the teen findings point the other way.

### 3.14 Bilingual families: "two languages confuse children or cause delay"
**Verdict:** Mostly myth. This is an added myth, relevant to Persian–English families.

**Evidence**
- **Hoff et al. 2012 (*J Child Lang*):**
  - **Sample:** high-SES children aged 1;10–2;6, comparing bilingual and monolingual children. [opened]
  - **Single language:** bilingual children scored lower than monolinguals in each language measured alone. [opened]
  - **Both languages:** they were **comparable on total vocabulary**. [opened]
  - **Input:** skill in each language tracked the amount of input in that language. [opened]
- **Byers-Heinlein & Lew-Williams 2013 ("What the science says"):** addresses common myths. [opened, abstract only]
- **Other claims:** that bilingual babies reach milestones such as first words on a similar timeline, and that mixing languages is normal. [recall, unverified]

**Sources:** PMID 21418730, PMC4323282; PMID 30288204, PMC6168212.

**Copy direction:** "Children can learn two languages from the start. Each language may look a little behind a one-language child's, but count both and it usually evens out. Each language grows with how much your child hears it, so keep speaking Persian at home if it's your heart language."

**Don't say:** "Bilingualism makes children smarter." The cognitive-advantage claim is contested.

## PART E — Daily tips (each ≤ 30 words)

1. Talk *with* your child, not just to them: say something, wait, and answer their babble or reply. Back-and-forth is what counts. (Romeo et al. 2018)
2. Name things during everyday routines like bath, cooking and shopping. Toddlers learned more words this way than from a word-teaching DVD. (DeLoache et al. 2010)
3. Keep bedtime roughly the same each night. Regular bedtimes were linked with calmer behaviour, and children improved when families switched. (Kelly et al. 2013)
4. Play a waiting game today, like "Simon says" or freeze dance. Self-control in childhood was linked with better adult health and finances. (Moffitt et al. 2011)
5. Showing warmth (a hug, a smile, "I love being with you") was linked with better wellbeing in children across nine countries. (Lansford/Rothenberg 2020)
6. Party energy isn't the sugar. Blinded studies found no behaviour change; limit sweets for teeth instead. (Wolraich et al. 1995)
7. A fever isn't "just teeth". Teething brings drool and grumpiness, not real fever; look for another cause. (Macknin et al. 2000)
8. Skip the baby walker: it doesn't speed walking and causes falls. Floor play and cruising along the sofa are better practice. (Shiva et al. 2010; Sims et al. 2018)
9. You can't spoil a young baby with cuddles. Comforting builds trust, and it's also fine to pause briefly when they're just fussing. (AAP HealthyChildren 2025)
10. Ten focused, happy minutes beat guilty hours. The amount of mothers' time at ages 3–11 wasn't linked with outcomes. (Milkie et al. 2015)
11. Speaking Persian at home? Keep going. Each language grows with how much a child hears it, and total vocabulary evens out. (Hoff et al. 2012)
12. Simple play matters: weekly coached play in Jamaica was linked with better adult wellbeing and earnings decades later. (Gertler 2014; Walker 2022)
13. Notice one thing you did well while playing today. Parents coached to spot good moments had children with fewer behaviour problems. (Mendelsohn et al. 2018)
14. Handwashing beats hats for preventing colds, though dressing warmly still makes sense. Colds come from viruses. (Johnson & Eccles 2005)
15. If you use childcare, look for warm carers and small groups. Quality was linked with better language, and home stays the biggest influence. (NICHD, Belsky 2007)
