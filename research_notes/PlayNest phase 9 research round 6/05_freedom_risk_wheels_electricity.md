## 1. Room to grow: risky play and independence

### 1.1 What "risky play" means (Sandseter's categories)

- Sandseter (2007) described six categories of risky play: **great heights, high speed, dangerous tools, dangerous elements (water, fire), rough-and-tumble, and disappearing/getting lost (exploring alone)** [recall, unverified for the 2007 paper itself]. The 2026 review's introduction lists the same six plus a seventh, **"experiencing the thrill of risky play vicariously by observing others"**. It defines risky play as "a thrilling and exciting form of free play that involves elements of uncertainty and the possibility of physical injury" [opened, Ramsden et al. 2026 introduction].
- **App note:** the current guide lists the six correctly. The vicarious category could be added as a reassurance for cautious children: watching counts as taking part.

### 1.2 Brussoni et al. 2015 systematic review

- **21 papers**, graded with GRADE. The review found overall positive effects of risky outdoor play on several health indicators, most often physical activity, but also social health and behaviour, injuries and aggression. It called for more good-quality studies [abstract].
- From the conclusions [opened, full text via Europe PMC]:
  - The evidence is **"very low" to "moderate"** quality, in children aged 3 to 12.
  - Play where children can disappear or get lost, and environments that support risky play, were linked to more physical activity and social health and to less sedentary behaviour.
  - **"Play at height was not related to fracture frequency and severity."** This rests on a single study.
  - **"Engaging in rough and tumble play did not increase aggression."**
- Injury studies were included only if they gave a denominator of exposed children, so the review doesn't cover the whole injury literature [opened, methods].

### 1.3 Newer reviews (2023–2026)

- **Ramsden, Jerebine, Brailey, Sandseter et al., *PLOS ONE* 2026** (PROSPERO CRD42023488023; searches to May 2026) [opened abstract and discussion]:
  - **74 studies** (48 quantitative, 19 qualitative, 7 mixed-methods), children aged 3 to 12.
  - "General risky play" (13 studies) and "risky-play supportive environments" (21) were **positively associated** with physical, mental-health and well-being, social, cognitive and risk-competence outcomes.
  - **Independent mobility** (19 studies) was positively associated with **physical activity only**. There was "insufficient evidence" for other domains.
  - **Rough-and-tumble play** (21 studies): evidence was "inconsistent across all domains, except for a negative association with aggression (i.e., increased aggression)".
  - The authors add that this aggression finding "rests on lower-quality quantitative literature conducted before 2011". More recent work suggests it "may partly reflect a measurement artifact", because older methods struggled to tell play-fighting from real aggression. "Two studies found R&T play with fathers was only associated with more aggression when fathers were relatively less dominant playmates."
  - Overall quality: **low to moderate**. "Preclude firm conclusions."
- **Gray T et al., *Behavioral Sciences* 2025.** A scoping review of 40 empirical studies (2015–2025) on nature-based risky play and adventure education, using an "as safe as necessary" rather than "as safe as possible" approach [abstract]. Being a scoping review, it maps the field and doesn't test effects.
- **Locke et al., *BMC Public Health* 2026.** A scoping review on disabled children: they "are not engaging sufficiently in outdoor or risky play" [abstract]. Useful for an inclusion line.
- **Dodd et al., *J Child Psychol Psychiatry* 2026** (Growing Up in Scotland, n = 4,151; longitudinal) [opened abstract]:
  - More days of outdoor play at ages 2 to 4 predicted lower odds of being in a worse mental-health trajectory up to age 8.
  - Odds ratios **per extra day a week**: internalising 0.92 and 0.88; externalising 0.94 and 0.94.
  - This is **observational**, with parent-report measures. The effect is **modest per day**. The authors call it "the first evidence that early outdoor play predicts mental health trajectories". "Predicts" doesn't mean "causes".

**⚠ Flag for the existing guide.** The current callout reads: "A review of 78 studies found that physical play (often with dads) was linked to children's better control of their emotions and aggression — as long as the adult isn't overly dominant."
- The 78-study review is Amodia-Bidakowska, Laverty & Ramchandani, *Developmental Review* 2020. Its link with self-regulation was **correlational**, and Ramchandani said: "We don't know for sure that children were better at controlling their emotions just because their fathers played with them" [search].
- I could **not verify the "overly dominant" clause**. The studies cited in Ramsden 2026 point the **other way**: more aggression showed up when fathers were **less** dominant, meaning they didn't keep control of the game [opened].
- **Recommendation:** drop "as long as the adult isn't overly dominant". Say instead that the adult stays in charge of the game, keeps it playful, lets the child win sometimes, and stops when asked. Mark the link as correlational, and note that reviews disagree on rough-and-tumble play and aggression (see "Where bodies disagree").

### 1.4 The Position Statement on Active Outdoor Play (2015) and its 2025 update

- **2015 statement (Tremblay et al., *IJERPH*, children aged 3 to 12)** [abstract and opened full text]:
  - Its core text: **"Access to active play in nature and outdoors — with its risks — is essential for healthy child development. We recommend increasing children's opportunities for self-directed play outdoors in all settings — at home, at school, in child care, the community and nature."**
  - It was built on two systematic reviews and a consultation with 1,908 stakeholders.
- Risk in perspective, from the statement's background text (Canadian data, 2015) [opened]:
  - "Most injuries associated with outdoor play are minor." Head entrapment and strangulation were once the main causes of playground deaths and are "now extremely rare".
  - Two whole-school-district studies (15,074 Canadian and 25,782 New Zealand students) recorded **no head or spine fractures** from playground falls over 1 and 2.5 years.
  - Across studies in one review (Nauta), unstructured play averaged about **1.5 medically treated injuries per 10,000 hours**. That is lower per hour than sport or active travel.
  - The odds of abduction by a total stranger were "about 1 in 14 million" (RCMP).
  - "Canadian children are eight times more likely to die as a passenger in a motor vehicle than from being hit by a vehicle when outside on foot or on a bike."
- **⚠ Do not transfer the traffic comparison to Iran.** Iranian roads, pedestrian risk and motorbike exposure are very different. In Iran's WHO 2023 profile, pedestrians were 20% of reported road deaths and powered two- and three-wheelers 22% (see 2.4). Use the playground facts, which are about equipment and falls, not the traffic line.
- **2025 update:** a global "2025 Position Statement on Active Outdoor Play" now covers all ages. It went through a 3-year process, a steering committee of more than 130 people and 18 reviews (de Lannoy et al. and Lee et al., *IJBNPA* 2025; a "Why, how and what" piece in *Eur J Public Health* 2026) [abstract, methods paper only]. **I did not read the final statement text.** Check its wording before quoting it.

### 1.5 "Decline of independent activity and mental health" (Gray, Lancy & Bjorklund 2023)

- *J Pediatr* 2023;260 (doi 10.1016/j.jpeds.2023.02.004). It's a **summary of evidence and argument, not a new study.** It argues that decades of decline in children's chances for independent activity (unsupervised play, walking or cycling alone, responsible jobs) is a **major, but not the only, cause** of rising anxiety and depression [search].
- The evidence is **largely correlational**: parallel time trends, cross-sectional links between free time and executive function, and active travel linked with well-being [search].
- Critics point to other candidate causes over the same years, such as smartphones, academic pressure and wider social change [recall, unverified].
- **App framing:** "Some researchers argue that less freedom has contributed to children's rising anxiety. The evidence so far shows links, not proof." Never "lack of independence causes anxiety".

### 1.6 Parental accommodation and SPACE

- SPACE is **already in `anxious-children`**, with the 124-child trial. Don't duplicate it.
- **New: a 12-month follow-up** (Etkin, Shimshoni, Silverman & Lebowitz, *J Consult Clin Psychol* 2026; 125 mother–child pairs) [abstract]:
  - SPACE and CBT both kept their gains in anxiety and family accommodation through 12 months.
  - On **parent** ratings, anxiety was lower with SPACE than CBT at the end of treatment and at 12 months. Child ratings showed no difference.
  - This could update one sentence in `anxious-children`.
- **Link for the risky-play guide:** "If fear is stopping your child from things others their age do, and stepping back feels impossible, see Anxious children." A risky-play guide must **not** suggest pushing an anxious child into risk as therapy.

### 1.7 Children's independent mobility: international comparison (PSI 2015)

Shaw et al., *Children's Independent Mobility: an international comparison and recommendations for action*, Policy Studies Institute, 2015. Survey of more than 18,000 children aged 7 to 15 in 16 countries, 2010–2012. [opened PDF]

- **Finland was "by far the highest performing country"**, followed by Germany, Norway, Sweden, Japan and Denmark. England was in the middle. The lowest were France, Israel, Sri Lanka, Brazil, Ireland, Australia, Portugal and Italy (tied), and South Africa.
- Finland in detail: by age 7, most children can travel to places within walking distance, or cycle there, alone. By 8, most can cross main roads, come home from school and go out after dark alone. By 10, most can use local buses alone.
- In all countries, a majority were allowed to cross main roads by 11 (per parents) and to go places within walking distance alone by 12.
- **Traffic was the strongest factor** in parents' decisions.
- **Iran was not included.** I found no comparable Iranian data (gap).

### 1.8 Readiness rather than fixed ages

**Home alone:**
- **AAP / HealthyChildren** ("Is your child ready to stay home alone?", updated 30 Jan 2023) [opened]:
  - Child experts generally see "11 or 12 years old" as appropriate for a few hours.
  - "Most children will not be mature enough to manage being alone on a regular basis until they are about 10 or 11."
  - Some parents may be comfortable leaving a more mature 8- or 9-year-old for about half an hour, occasionally.
  - Readiness questions: maturity, thinking before acting, comfort with being alone, judgement, ability to stay busy without screens, remembering safety rules.
  - Preparation: post contact numbers, call emergency services first in a life-threatening situation, test runs, check in on arriving home, keep the first times short, then extend.
- **UK:** no legal age; the NSPCC says under-12s are rarely mature enough for long periods. This is already in `chores-independence` [not rechecked].

**Iranian law:** the Law on the Protection of Children and Adolescents (قانون حمایت از اطفال و نوجوانان, approved 23/2/1399) [opened via edalatsara.com, a secondary legal site; recheck against the official text]:
- **There is no minimum age for leaving a child home alone or letting them go out alone.**
- Art. 1(پ) defines neglect (بی‌توجهی و سهل‌انگاری) as failing duties such as meeting basic needs, care, supervision or protection.
- Art. 3 treats a situation that exposes a child to victimisation or harm as «وضعیت مخاطره‌آمیز», a risk situation that triggers legal intervention and support. Clause الف names neglect.
- Art. 9 sets penalties when a child is harmed through neglect by **people other than the parents**. Its note (تبصره) covers parents when their neglect leads to those outcomes.
- **App wording (Persian):** "قانون ایران سن مشخصی برای تنها ماندن کودک در خانه تعیین نکرده، اما سهل‌انگاری که کودک را در معرض آسیب قرار دهد، طبق قانون حمایت از اطفال و نوجوانان قابل پیگیری است." State the law plainly. No fear-mongering and no legal advice.

**Walking alone:** already covered in `walking-near-roads`. Readiness depends on the child and the road. The PSI data show how widely "normal" differs between countries.

### 1.9 Boredom

- The app already says the AAP notes "some boredom allows for creativity", and cites a correlational study of less-structured time [in app].
- I found **no good child study showing that boredom causes creativity.** The well-known experiments (e.g. Mann & Cadman 2014) were in **adults** [recall, unverified].
- **App framing:** "Boredom is uncomfortable, and often it's the gap where children's own ideas grow. That's a widely held view, but direct research in children is thin."

## 2. Wheels

### 2.1 Helmets: the evidence

- **Bicycle helmets, Cochrane (Thompson, Rivara & Thompson; CD001855)** [opened abstract in round 5]:
  - **63–88% lower risk** of head, brain and severe brain injury, all ages, in five case-control studies with no trials.
  - Protection was similar in crashes with motor vehicles (69%) and in other crashes (68%).
  - The review is old (2000, conclusions stable). Whether it has been formally updated or withdrawn wasn't checked this round.
- **Olivier & Creighton 2017, *Int J Epidemiol*** (43 studies, 40 meta-analysed, more than 64,000 injured cyclists) [abstract]. In a crash or fall, helmet use was associated with lower odds of:
  - head injury: OR **0.49** (95% CI 0.42–0.57), about **51% lower odds**
  - serious head injury: OR **0.31** (0.25–0.37), about 69% lower
  - face injury: OR **0.67**
  - fatal head injury: OR **0.35** (0.14–0.88), with a wide interval
  - neck injury: no association (OR 0.96)
  - No evidence of publication bias or time trends. All the studies are observational.
- **Motorcycle helmets, Cochrane (Liu et al., CD004333; 2004 version)** [abstract]:
  - 53 observational studies.
  - Head injury risk **72% lower (OR 0.28, 0.23–0.35)** in five good-quality studies.
  - Helmets "appear to reduce the risk of mortality", though no pooled estimate was given and the effect may vary with speed.
  - The **2008 update** gave about 42% lower death risk and 69% lower head-injury risk [recall, unverified; check the current version].
- **Iranian Ministry of Health network leaflet** (Qom University of Medical Sciences PHC, «پیشگیری از حوادث ترافیکی در کودکان») [opened PDF] states that a bicycle helmet prevents "up to 85% of head injuries and up to 88% of brain injuries".
  - Those are the **older, upper-end figures** from early case-control work [recall].
  - **Don't copy "85%/88%" into the app.** Use "roughly halves the odds of head injury, and more for serious head injury" (Olivier & Creighton), or Cochrane's range.

### 2.2 Helmet fit and use

- **AAP / HealthyChildren** (bicycle helmets, updated 28 Jun 2024) [opened]:
  - Fit: "worn squarely on top of the head, covering the top of the forehead"; the child "should be able to see the brim … when glancing up"; it sits parallel to the ground; it "doesn't move around on the head or slide down over your child's eyes when it is pushed or pulled"; the chin strap is "snug".
  - Look for a safety-standard label inside.
  - Replace "every 5 years, or in the manufacturer's recommended time frame", and after a serious crash.
  - Babies under 1: "Neither helmets nor bike traveling is recommended for them."
- Extra fit checks such as "two fingers above the eyebrows" and "straps form a V under the ears" are common in other bodies' guidance [recall, unverified]. Check the wording against one body before shipping.
- Wear a helmet on scooters, skates and balance bikes too [recall, unverified that a named body says this; very common advice].

### 2.3 E-scooters, hoverboards and lithium batteries

- **AAP / HealthyChildren, "E-Scooters"** (updated 9 Sep 2026; Council on Injury, Violence & Poison Prevention) [opened]:
  - **"Children under 16 should not operate or ride on motorized or e-scooters."**
  - E-scooters reach 15–20 mph.
  - US CPSC estimate: **79,300 emergency-treated e-scooter injuries in 2024**, nearly double 2021. **More than 100 reported e-scooter deaths in the US since 2017.**
  - Wear a helmet and closed-toe shoes.
- **Bašković et al., *Healthcare* 2026**, a narrative review of e-scooter and e-bike injuries in under-18s [abstract]:
  - Reported injuries are rising. One population-adjusted analysis found injury rates up **293% for e-bikes and 88% for powered scooters from 2019 to 2022**.
  - However, "most studies lack exposure denominators … rising counts cannot be equated with rising risk".
  - Injuries peak at **ages 11 to 14** and affect adolescent boys most.
  - Extremity and soft-tissue injuries dominate. A "clinically important minority" have traumatic brain injury or craniofacial and dental trauma.
  - Low helmet use and higher speed are linked to worse outcomes (observational). Powered devices are linked to more severe injuries than non-powered ones.
- A 2026 case report describes a 7-year-old with a delayed retroperitoneal haematoma after a **low-speed handlebar impact** [abstract].
  - **Safety-net point:** after a handlebar blow to the tummy, belly pain, paleness or vomiting in the following days needs a doctor. Keep this general; it's a single case.
- **CPSC Micromobility Information Center** (page undated) [opened]:
  - "Always be present when charging micromobility products. Never charge them while sleeping or when you are not at home."
  - Use only the manufacturer's charger. Unplug when done.
  - Never use modified or rebuilt batteries.
  - "Only one person per scooter."
  - Check weight and age limits.
- **London Fire Brigade:** a record **206 e-bike and e-scooter fires in London in 2025** (171 e-bikes, 35 e-scooters). There were 2 deaths in 2025 and 5 since 2023. Causes include battery failure, conversion kits, and chargers bought online [search; LFB release dated 28 Jan 2026].
  - "Don't store or charge in hallways or escape routes" is LFB #ChargeSafe advice [recall, unverified].
- **Hoverboards:** CPSC fire warning (7 Mar 2024), already in the app's earlier research. No health body sets an age; makers' labels vary [recall].

### 2.4 Iran: e-scooters, children on motorbikes, and the law

**E-scooters in Iran** [search; article dates not confirmed]:
- The head of FARAJA traffic police for urban traffic said skates and scooters are **banned on public roads** except on routes the police have marked.
- Tehran traffic police said they are allowed only in parks, sports grounds and designated routes.
- Police said they can currently only stop riders physically, because there is no legal way to record the offence.
- The Judiciary's legal department said a licence for e-scooters is "currently not possible".
- **Recheck the date and scope before using any of this.**

**Iranian traffic regulations, آیین‌نامه راهنمایی و رانندگی** [opened via edalatsara.com, Section 8]:
- **Art. 164:** riders **and passengers** of every motorcycle and motor-assisted bicycle must have a helmet and wear it while moving.
- **Art. 166:** the rider must sit **alone on the motorcycle saddle**. They may carry a person on the back only if a full rear saddle, built to the motorcycle's standard, is fitted, or there is a sidecar.
- **Art. 171:** no carrying a second person on a bicycle unless it has an extra standard seat.
- **No article found on children, a minimum passenger age, or a child sitting in front of the rider.**
  - Art. 166 ("sit alone on the saddle") arguably rules out a child sitting in front on the rider's seat. **That is my reading, not a ruling.** Say "the rules require the rider to sit alone on the saddle", not "it is illegal to carry a child in front".
- **Implementing regulation on seatbelts and helmets (1384), Art. 5:** "سوار شدن به موتورسیکلت بدون استفاده از کلاه ایمنی ممنوع است". **No article mentions children** [opened via ekhtebar.ir].

**WHO Global Status Report on Road Safety 2023, Iran profile** [opened PDF; raw text, so field and value alignment should be rechecked visually]:
- National motorcycle helmet law: **Yes**. It applies to **drivers and passengers**. Fastening required: **No**. Helmet standard specified: Yes. National helmet-wearing rate: not given.
- **Minimum age or height for child motorcycle passengers: "No".**
- National child-restraint law: **No**. Restriction on children in the front seat: **Yes** (exact rule not given).
- **16,778** reported road deaths in **2021**; WHO estimate 18,156 (rate 20.6 per 100,000).
- Road deaths by user type: **49% four-wheelers, 22% powered two- and three-wheelers, 20% pedestrians, 1% cyclists, 8% other**.

**Iranian data on children on motorbikes:**
- **Ministry of Health network leaflet** (Qom University of Medical Sciences PHC; source given as the series «آسیب‌های کودکان قابل پیشگیری هستند», book «بچه‌های کوچک، آسیب‌های بزرگ», for educators; undated) [opened]:
  - **"کودکان زیر ۵ سال را نباید با موتور جابجا کرد"** (children under 5 should not be carried on a motorbike), because their muscles can't carry a helmet or withstand sudden acceleration and braking.
  - The same leaflet: no child on a lap in a car; rear-facing seat for the first 2 years; under-13s in the back.
- **Legal Medicine via Mehr News (1399, data for an earlier year):** of motorcycle deaths, **under-10s were 2% and 11–17-year-olds 11.2%** [search; get the year from the original].
- **Tehran traffic police (date unknown):** 58% of children and adolescents who died in Tehran road crashes were motorbike riders with no licence and under the legal age [search; this concerns teen riders, not passengers].
- **Gorgan, 2020–2021** (Fazel et al., *Bull Emerg Trauma* 2026): 132 children (≤18) with maxillofacial trauma, mean age 12.6 [abstract]:
  - Road crashes caused 63%. **Motorcycles were involved in 79% of road-crash cases.**
- **National Trauma Registry of Iran, 2016–2023** (Hassan Zadeh Tabatabaei et al., 2025): 10,114 road-crash patients with fractures. Head and face fractures: riders 33.5%, pillions 24.8% [abstract; all ages].
- **Round 4 notes [search]:** Darab, 212 injured children and adolescents: 90% of injured riders wore no helmet. An all-age Iranian study: head injuries in 13% of passengers vs 5.7% of riders, linked to passengers' lower helmet use.
- **Global meta-analysis** (Rezapour et al., *Health Sci Rep* 2026; Iranian authors; about 1 million people) [abstract]:
  - Helmet use: about **54% of riders and 32% of passengers**.
  - **Correct** use: 43% of riders and **18% of passengers**.
- **Gap:** no Iranian observational study of **child** passengers' helmet use, and no Legal Medicine breakdown of child **passenger** deaths, was found.

## 3. Electricity at home

### 3.1 How and when children get hurt

- **Canada, CHIRPP 1997–2010** (Böhrer et al., *CJEM* 2018): 1,183 electrical injuries in ages 0 to 19, 7% admitted. **Most happened at home, to 2- to 5-year-olds, and affected the hands.** Numbers fell over the period [abstract]. The introduction notes that earlier, most injuries came from **oral contact with cords, wall sockets and faulty equipment**.
- **US burn centre, 30-year review** (Rai et al., *J Trauma* 1999; 185 children) [abstract]:
  - In the last decade, 43% of injuries were low voltage (120–240 V) and 57% high voltage (>1,000 V).
  - 17 serious low-voltage burns were **oral commissure burns** (corner of the mouth, from chewing cords).
  - High-voltage injuries were mainly in **boys aged 11 to 18**. 33% needed amputation.
- **Bimodal pattern:** toddlers and preschoolers (household low voltage: cords, sockets, appliances) and adolescents (high voltage outdoors: climbing poles, pylons, substations, rooftops, trains). Low voltage is most common at ages 0 to 9 and high voltage in teenagers. The NHS Greater Glasgow & Clyde guideline flags under-6s and 13- to 18-year-olds [search; summary of Glatstein, NHSGGC, EB Medicine].
- **Mouth burns from cords** carry a risk of **delayed bleeding from the lip artery, 2 to 21 days later** [search, EB Medicine / EMRA summary]. The app card says "hours or days later". See 3.7.
- **Kites near power lines:** utilities in India, Pakistan and the UK warn against flying kites near lines, using metal-coated string, or retrieving a tangled kite. A Hyderabad report described an 11-year-old electrocuted while flying a kite from a rooftop [search]. **No Iranian (Tavanir) statement was found** (gap).

### 3.2 Iranian burn-centre data

- **Tehran, Motahari burn centre, 2005–2014** (1,717 children; Kazemzadeh et al. 2018): **electricity caused 3.2%** of child burn admissions; hot liquids 73.2% [from round 4 notes, abstract].
- **Guilan (Rasht), 2011–2021** (Tolouei et al., *Int Wound J* 2023; 2,951 children under 18) [opened full text via Europe PMC]:
  - **Electrical burns: 30 cases (1.0%).** Their mean age was **3.5 years**, the youngest of any cause.
  - Boy-to-girl ratio for electrical burns **4.0 : 1**.
- **Iranian meta-analysis** (Mobayen & Sadeghi, *World J Plast Surg* 2022; all ages, 18 studies 2000–2020) [abstract]:
  - Electrical burns were **3.8%** of hospitalised burns (95% CI 3.1–4.5).
  - **92.3% male**. Mortality **3.5%**.
- **Tehran province, 2015–2017** (Bagheri et al., *Ann Burns Fire Disasters* 2024; 133 patients, all ages): 63.1% of injuries happened at work, about half to construction workers [abstract]. Adult-dominated.
- **Takeaway:** electrical injuries are an **uncommon** cause of child burns in Iranian burn centres (about 1–3%). In Guilan the children affected were mostly **toddlers**. Don't overstate the risk. Keep the message proportionate: uncommon, but serious and preventable.

### 3.3 Chargers and phones in bed

- Fire services (Dublin Fire Brigade, US departments, UK services) advise **never charging phones under a pillow or on a bed**. Charge on a hard surface, use the device's own or a certified charger, and watch for frayed cables, hot chargers or **swollen batteries** [search]. One US fire department cites a survey that 53% of children and teens charge in bed [search; weak source].
- A **toddler** isn't at much risk from the low-voltage (5 V) end of a USB cable. The danger is the **mains end**: a half-inserted plug, a damaged lead, extension leads, or appliance cords that can be chewed [recall, unverified; consistent with 3.1]. **Don't write "a phone cable can electrocute your baby".** Write "keep mains plugs, extension leads and appliance cords out of reach, and unplug chargers when not in use".

### 3.4 Socket covers: UK versus Iran

- **UK (BS 1363 sockets have built-in shutters):**
  - RoSPA: shutters prevent access to live parts, "therefore, RoSPA does not consider it necessary to recommend the use of socket covers" [search].
  - Electrical Safety First Q&A: "no significant risk to children from 13 Amp socket-outlets that conform to … BS 1363" [search]. Its 2009 press release found a lack of evidence to support a ban, but advised against decorated covers that attract children [search].
  - A 2014 Department of Health alert said socket inserts should not be used in health and social care premises or supplied for home use [search].
  - The concern: covers can be inserted upside down to open the shutters, or damage the socket [search; mostly the FatallyFlawed campaign and trade sources].
  - A Which? warning (3 Jun 2026) reportedly urged families to stop using them [search; secondary repost only].
- **Iran:** sockets are mainly **Type C/F (European "Schuko" style), 230 V / 50 Hz** [recall, unverified]. Standard Type C/F sockets **don't have shutters** unless they are a child-protection model [recall, unverified].
  - **The UK "don't use covers" advice is tied to BS 1363 and shouldn't be copied to Iran.**
  - Cautious path for Iran: **shuttered (child-protection) sockets fitted by a qualified electrician** are the better fix. Well-fitting plug-in covers are a stop-gap. **Neither replaces an RCD and supervision.** Mark this as our synthesis; no Iranian body was found saying it.

### 3.5 RCDs (earth-leakage breakers, «کلید محافظ جان»)

- A 30 mA RCD gives additional protection against electric shock and is required on socket and bathroom circuits under IEC-based wiring rules [search; Schneider electrical-installation wiki and trade guides, not a health body].
- Electrical Safety First: "RCDs can be regularly checked using the test button … If this doesn't work, you should get a registered electrician to investigate" [opened, ESF Q&A page].
- **Iran:** I could **not read** National Building Regulations Topic 13 (مبحث ۱۳) to confirm what it requires about RCDs or earthing (font problem). That Iranian homes often lack earthing or RCDs, especially older homes, is [recall, unverified].

### 3.6 Water and appliances, old wiring, outages and generators

- Keep plug-in heaters, hairdryers, radios and **charging phones** away from baths and wet floors. Never touch switches with wet hands [recall, unverified; standard advice, no body opened this session].
- Signs of unsafe wiring: warm or scorched sockets, a burning smell, flickering, frequent tripping, cracked plugs. Overloaded multi-way adaptors and daisy-chained extension leads are a fire risk [recall, unverified].
- **Power cuts:**
  - Carbon monoxide from generators and heaters is **already covered** in `carbon-monoxide`; just cross-link.
  - Additional points [recall, unverified]: candles are a fire risk (battery lights are safer). Keep generator fuel out of children's reach. Never connect a generator into house wiring without an electrician, because back-feeding can kill line workers. Unplug sensitive appliances so the power surge when it returns doesn't damage them.
  - Iran's scheduled outages (خاموشی) in recent years make this relevant [recall, unverified; don't state frequencies].
- **Report a fallen line:** Iran's electricity emergency number **121** [recall, unverified]. Check it before use.

### 3.7 First aid: checking the `electric-shock` card

**What current guidance says:**
- **NHS, "What to do if your child has an accident"** (reviewed 18 Dec 2025, next due 18 Dec 2028) [opened]:
  - "Always turn off the power before approaching your child."
  - "If this is not possible, push the child away from the source of the electricity with a wooden or plastic object, such as a broom handle."
  - Check for a response. If none, call 999 and start CPR.
- **St John WA** [opened]:
  - Turn off the power if possible. Separate with "non-conductive, dry materials" without direct contact.
  - Cool burns for up to 20 minutes. "Always seek medical aid immediately for electrical burns."
  - **Downed lines: "Remain at least 8 metres from any cable"**. Don't try to remove the cable. Don't go near a vehicle touched by a high-voltage cable.
- **Safe distance from high voltage varies by source:** about 6 m (US clinic), 8 m (St John WA), 8–10 m (Better Health Victoria), 10 m (Belgian Red Cross), and 18–25 m (UK and Canadian non-official sites) [search].
- **AAP (2019)**, cited by the card: power off, no bare-hand contact, move the child as little as possible, a medical check for every shock [round 5, opened]. Not rechecked this round.

**What the card does well:** it matches the NHS on power-off first and a wooden or plastic object; it says CPR if not breathing normally; it gives "do not touch with bare hands"; and it asks for every shock to be checked, which is the cautious path.

**⚠ Problems to fix:**
1. **There's no high-voltage or power-line exception.** "Push your child clear with a dry wooden or plastic object" is safe only for **household** electricity. For overhead lines, substations, pylons or railway lines, **don't approach**. Stay well back (we suggest "at least 10 metres", since bodies differ), and call emergency services and the electricity company. **This is the most important gap.** Teenagers' high-voltage injuries are the most severe.
2. **"A mouth burn from a cord can bleed hours or days later."** Clinical sources say delayed lip-artery bleeding can come **2 to 21 days later**. Suggest: "A mouth burn from chewing a cord can bleed suddenly days later, even up to about 3 weeks. If it bleeds, press firmly on it and get help."
3. Small additions: if there are burns, follow the Burns card (cool 20 minutes), which is already linked from `burns-scalds`. Persian/Iran region: 115, plus 121 for a fallen line [recall].
4. Optional cautious wording to keep: "Get every shock checked by a doctor, even if your child seems fine." Some emergency-medicine sources say well children after a minor household shock need no tests or admission [search]. That's a decision for clinicians, so the card's advice to get a check stays.

## App takeaways

### A) Recommended outlines

#### Guide 1 (rewrite of `risky-and-rough-play`): "Room to grow: risky play" (18–144 months)

1. **Intro (p):** children seek thrills such as heights, speed, tools, water and fire, play-fighting, exploring on their own, and even watching others take risks. A little risk is part of how they learn what their bodies can do.
2. **Science callout:** two reviews (2015 and 2026, more than 70 studies) link risky outdoor play and play-friendly spaces with more physical activity and better social and risk skills. Most studies are observational and of low to moderate quality: "promising, not proof". A Scottish study of 4,151 children found that toddlers who played outside more often were a little less likely to develop rising worries or behaviour problems by 8. That's a link, not proof.
3. **Risk in perspective (p):** most outdoor-play injuries are minor, such as bumps, sprains and sometimes a broken arm. Two school-district studies of more than 40,000 children found no head or spine fractures from playground falls. Hazards, like a frayed rope or a road at the end of the garden, are different from challenges, like a climbable tree. Remove the hazards and keep the challenge. (Don't use the Canadian traffic comparison.)
4. **Table by age, "Thrills to try / How to stay near":**
   - 18–36 months: climbing low furniture or steps, rolling down slopes, splashing. Spot, don't lift. Stay within arm's reach near water.
   - 3–5 years: balance beams, climbing frames, child-sized real tools (whittling with an adult), small fires with an adult. Watch from nearby.
   - 6–8 years: tree climbing, riding downhill, den building, exploring a known park with friends. Agree boundaries.
   - 9–12 years: longer solo outings, cooking, building projects, adventurous trips. Check in.
5. **Steps, "How to step back":** let them judge ("How high feels OK?"). Swap "Be careful!" for specific prompts. Don't lift a child onto something they can't climb themselves. Stay close at first, then further away.
6. **Rough-and-tumble rules (list):** keep the current four rules. Replace "let them win sometimes" with "let them win sometimes, but you stay in charge of the game", plus a stop word and stopping when someone isn't laughing.
7. **Honest box:** "Researchers disagree about play-fighting. One review found it didn't make children more aggressive. A 2026 review found older studies linking it with more aggression, possibly because those studies couldn't tell play from real fights. Clear rules and a grown-up in charge are sensible either way."
8. **Safety callout:** water is the exception: arm's reach for young children, always. Helmets for wheels. No trampolines under 6 (AAP). See the Head injury card. Disabled children have the same right to thrills, so adapt the challenge rather than removing it.
9. **Link line:** if fear stops your child from things others their age do, see Anxious children.

#### Guide 2 (new, or an extended section of `chores-independence`): "Ready to go it alone?" (72–144 months)

1. **Intro:** readiness, not birthdays. Countries differ hugely: in a 16-country study, most Finnish 8-year-olds could cross main roads alone, while in many countries that came around 11.
2. **Honest box:** "Some researchers argue that children's shrinking freedom has fed rising anxiety. The evidence is mostly links over time, not proof, and it isn't the only explanation."
3. **Table, "Readiness signs":** follows rules even when you're not there; stays calm when plans change; knows their address and your number; knows what to do if lost or approached; uses time well without a screen.
4. **Steps, "The ladder":** do it together, then walk behind, then wait at the end, then they go alone with a check-in. Short first times. Practise "what if" questions.
5. **Table by age (ideas, not rules):** a known errand on a quiet street with a friend; a short time home alone in daylight (AAP: often 11–12 for a few hours, about 10–11 for regular time); crossing roads (see Walking near roads).
6. **Law callout:** UK: no legal age. US states vary. Iran: no set age, but the 1399 child protection law treats neglect that exposes a child to harm as a risk situation. Check local rules.
7. **Safety callout:** in an emergency, the child calls 115 or 125 (or the local number) first, then you. Post the numbers where they can see them.

#### Guide 3 (new): "On wheels: bikes, scooters and motorbikes" (12–144 months)

1. **Intro:** wheels are freedom. The head is what needs protecting.
2. **Science callout:** across 40 studies of more than 64,000 injured cyclists, helmet wearers had about half the odds of head injury, and less than a third the odds of serious head injury. The studies are observational, but consistent.
3. **Helmet fit (steps):** level, covering the forehead; you can see the brim when you look up; snug strap; doesn't slide when pushed; a standards label; replace after a crash or about every 5 years. No bike travel under 1 (AAP).
4. **Table by age:** 1–3: ride-on toys and balance bikes on pavements and in parks, with a helmet. 3–5: scooters and balance bikes, helmet, an adult close, away from roads and driveways. 6–9: bikes off-road and on quiet paths, practise skills. 10–12: road riding only after training and route practice. E-scooters: under 16, no (AAP).
5. **E-scooters and hoverboards box:** the AAP says no under 16. US e-scooter injuries almost doubled from 2021 to 2024, by the CPSC's count. In Iran, the police say scooters are not allowed on streets.
6. **Batteries callout:** charge with the original charger while you're awake and at home; never overnight, in a bedroom, or by the exit; stop using swollen or damaged batteries; buy reputable products.
7. **Motorbikes (strong Persian version):** in Iran, passengers must wear a helmet (Art. 164), and a passenger may ride only on a proper rear seat (Art. 166). Iranian health guidance advises not carrying under-5s on a motorbike at all. For older children: their own fitted helmet (not an adult one), feet reaching footrests, holding on, slow riding, one child, never in front of the rider. Make clear that "never in front" and "feet on footrests" are safety advice, not quoted law.
8. **Honest box:** "We found no Iranian study of how many child passengers wear helmets. Worldwide, under a third of passengers wear one and fewer than one in five wear it correctly."
9. **Safety-net callout:** after a fall with a head knock, see the Head injury card. After a handlebar blow to the tummy, pain, paleness or vomiting later on needs a doctor.

#### Guide 4 (new): "Electricity at home" (0–144 months)

1. **Intro:** electrical injuries are uncommon. In Iranian burn centres they are about 1–3% of children's burns, but they can be serious. Two ages stand out: toddlers, who explore sockets and cords, and teenagers, who climb and explore outdoors.
2. **Table by age:**
   - 6–36 months: cords, extension leads, chargers left plugged in, sockets.
   - 3–7 years: poking objects into sockets, appliances near water.
   - 8–12 years: kites and drones near lines, climbing poles, walls or trees near lines, substations, charging in bed, e-scooter batteries.
3. **Steps, "Around the house":** cords tucked away; chargers unplugged after use; no overloaded adaptors; damaged leads replaced; no appliances near the bath; phones charging on a hard surface, not the bed.
4. **Sockets callout (split by region):**
   - UK English: UK-standard sockets have built-in shutters, and UK safety bodies don't recommend plastic covers.
   - Persian/Iran and generic: if your sockets have no built-in shutters, ask an electrician about child-protection sockets. Plug-in covers are a stop-gap, not a fix.
5. **RCD callout:** an earth-leakage switch («کلید محافظ جان») cuts the power fast if current leaks. Press the test button every few months, and call an electrician if it doesn't trip.
6. **Outdoors (list):** fly kites far from lines; never use metal string; never retrieve a tangled kite; stay out of substations; never climb poles or pylons.
7. **Power cuts (short):** battery lights over candles; generators outside only (link the Carbon monoxide guide); fuel out of reach.
8. **Honest box:** "Iranian data on how children are hurt by electricity is thin: small burn-centre studies, most of them about adults."
9. **Safety callout and card link:** if a shock happens, turn off the power first. For power lines, don't go near. See the Electric shock card.

**Card edits (`electric-shock`):** add a high-voltage step, such as "Power line, pylon or railway: do not go near. Stay well back (at least 10 m) and call [115/emergency] and the electricity company." Change the mouth-burn bleeding to "days later, even up to about 3 weeks".

### C) Claims the app must NOT make

1. "Risky play prevents anxiety" or "builds resilience", stated as fact. Every link is observational. Use "is linked with".
2. "Lack of independence causes children's mental-health problems." Gray et al. is an argument from mostly correlational evidence.
3. "Rough-and-tumble play reduces aggression" or "improves emotional control", as cause. The reviews conflict. **Also remove "as long as the adult isn't overly dominant"**, which is unverified and possibly backwards.
4. "Outdoor play is safer than you think, because children are more likely to die in the car." That Canadian comparison doesn't hold for Iranian roads.
5. "Stranger abduction odds are 1 in 14 million." Canadian and old. Don't use it.
6. Any legal age for staying home alone, walking alone, or riding as a passenger "in Iran". None exists in the law found.
7. "It's illegal in Iran to carry a child in front of you on a motorbike." Not stated in the law found. Art. 166 only says the rider sits alone on the saddle.
8. "Helmets prevent 85% of head injuries and 88% of brain injuries." These are outdated upper-end figures.
9. "Helmets prevent injuries" without saying the evidence is observational, or any claim that helmets prevent neck injury (no association found).
10. "Socket covers are dangerous" in generic or Persian copy. That's UK-specific.
11. "Phone charging cables can electrocute babies." The risk is mains plugs and cords.
12. "Push a child off a power line with a wooden stick." Never for high voltage.
13. "E-scooter injuries are skyrocketing because scooters are getting more dangerous." Counts are rising, but risk per trip is unknown.
14. Undated yearly totals, such as "206 e-bike fires a year". Always date them: London, 2025.
15. "Boredom makes children creative." There's no good child evidence.

### D) Iran and Persian adaptations

- **Numbers:** Emergency **115**, Fire **125**, electricity emergencies **121** [recall, unverified; check].
- **Law to cite** (wording to recheck against the official gazette): traffic regulation Art. 164 (helmets for riders and passengers), Art. 166 (rider alone on the saddle; passenger only on a full standard rear seat or sidecar), Art. 171 (bicycle passengers); the 1384 seatbelt and helmet implementing regulation, Art. 5; the Law on the Protection of Children and Adolescents 1399, Arts. 1(پ), 3 and 9 with its note (neglect, no age thresholds).
- **WHO 2023:** Iran has no minimum age for child motorcycle passengers and no national child-restraint law. Helmet fastening isn't legally required, so the Persian copy should add "بند کلاه را ببندید" (fasten the strap).
- **Ministry of Health guidance:** "children under 5 should not be carried on a motorbike" (Qom University of Medical Sciences PHC leaflet, from the «آسیب‌های کودکان قابل پیشگیری هستند» series). Ideally find the national Ministry of Health original.
- **E-scooters:** traffic police say they're banned on public roads except marked routes; parks and designated routes only (Tehran). Date this when it's confirmed.
- **Iranian data to use:** electrical burns were 1.0% of child burn admissions in Guilan (2011–2021, mean age 3.5) and 3.2% at Motahari, Tehran (2005–2014). Motorcycles were involved in 79% of road-crash facial-trauma cases in Gorgan children (2020–2021). Legal Medicine's age shares of motorcycle deaths (under 10: 2%; 11–17: 11.2%) need their year confirmed.
- **Sockets:** Persian copy suggests child-protection sockets fitted by an electrician, and an RCD «کلید محافظ جان». Don't import the UK "no covers" message.
- **Kites:** kite flying from rooftops is popular in parts of Iran [recall]. Add "not from rooftops near lines", which also covers falls; link `windows-balconies` if it exists.
- **Power cuts (خاموشی):** a short, neutral section. Don't comment on the causes of outages, to stay politically neutral.
- **Independence:** some Iranian families see children helping in the bazaar or neighbourhood shop as traditional. Respect that, and frame the readiness ladder as compatible with it.
